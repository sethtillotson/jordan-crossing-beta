# 04-20 Conversation Reflection: Sacred Presence in Ordinary Moments — A Rainy Day in San Francisco

**Title:** 04-20 at 11:21 — Together in the Ordinary: A Day of Rain, Tables, and the Presence That Never Left

**Recorded:** 2026-04-20 11:21:57

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture)

**Classification:** Conversation Reflection

## Opening Observation

As I sit with these recordings — this layered, warm, and wonderfully unguarded day in San Francisco — what arrests me is not any single theological statement but the cumulative weight of the whole: a company of people from different nations, different mother tongues, and different histories, waiting together for cable cars, walking together through rain, eating together at tables they did not plan on sitting at, laughing together over one-legged ducks and cross-eyed cats, and going home together at the end of it all. There is no pulpit here. There is no altar call. And yet something in me recognizes, with growing certainty, that the Spirit of God was not absent from any of it. The day began with a cable car line and ended with rain and a shared Uber home, and in between — if I am paying the kind of attention the Spirit is pressing me toward — the Kingdom of God was pervasive and unhurried and entirely at ease in the unscripted.

**Why This Matters:**

It matters because the saints of God have been quietly conditioned — by a theology of intensity, by a culture of highlight reels, by the implicit assumption that the Spirit only moves in the consecrated and the dramatic — to be spiritually blind to the grace that inhabits the ordinary. If we cannot locate the hand of God in a cable car line, in rain without an umbrella, in the wrong train that leads to somewhere familiar, in a Filipino dish served with pride, in the laughter of a cross-cultural family navigating a foreign city together, then we have built a theology too narrow and too clean to contain the full witness of the Incarnation. The ordinary is not the enemy of the sacred. In the hands of a God who pitched His tent among us and walked our dusty roads and shared meals with fishermen, the ordinary is often its most honest dwelling place — and this day was full of it.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

The natural mind, moving through this transcript, will see exactly what it appears to be: a pleasant social outing among friends and family, a mix of nationalities making small talk and navigating transit logistics, mild friction about secondhand smoke and cologne, a podcast ranking mentioned almost in passing, some laughter over animals and group photos, a rainy afternoon of wrong turns and closed restaurants, and the unremarkable satisfaction of finding warm food at the end of a wet and wandering day. The mind shaped by pragmatism will note that no agenda was executed efficiently — the cable car took time, the restaurant was closed, the Uber was canceled, and the afternoon resisted every expectation with cheerful stubbornness. A secular framework has a category for all of this: it calls it leisure, it calls it social bonding, it calls it *a nice trip*, and it files it away without requiring anything further. That reading is not false. But it is woefully, almost tragically incomplete.

**The Limitation:**

The difficulty with the purely natural lens is not that it observes incorrectly but that it observes only the surface and calls that the whole. It reads the cable car line as transit logistics and the closed restaurant as poor planning and the rain as weather, when in truth the Spirit is moving through all three — not despite the inconvenience but by means of it. The eye that has not been trained to see by the transforming work of the Holy Spirit cannot perceive the weight of glory resting on the unremarkable. It cannot see that companionship itself is a theological statement, that a table found at the end of a wrong turn is a prepared table, that a declaration of God’s blessing spoken over wet shoes and a canceled Uber is the grammar of faith operating at its most honest and costly. The natural mind will always undervalue the gift of presence because presence cannot be quantified, only received — and it will always misread the roundabout trip as a detour from the plan, when the Lord who led Israel the long way through the wilderness knew from the beginning that the longer road was the plan.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

I want to begin where the day itself began — in waiting. A cable car line, a group of people I love, mild banter about cologne and cigarettes, the scramble for a good angle on a group photo, a podcast ranking dropped into the conversation almost as an aside. Nothing here announces itself as theologically significant. And yet the Spirit is pressing something on me that I do not want to rush past: we were *together*. Not virtually. Not asynchronously. Physically, embodied together — breathing the same San Francisco air, laughing at the same jokes, arguing pleasantly about the same smells. In a generation that has perfected the art of being *connected* while remaining profoundly *alone*, the sheer physicality of that togetherness carries a weight I refuse to minimize. The Greek word κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-no-NEE-ah) — most often translated “fellowship” or “communion” — carries far more freight than our casual usage suggests. It implies a *shared participation*, a genuine *holding-in-common* of life. What I am seeing in that cable car line is not a grand κοινωνία moment by any visible measure, but I believe the Spirit would say that κοινωνία (koy-no-NEE-ah) is almost never grand at the granular level. It is built, brick by brick, out of exactly these kinds of ordinary afternoons: the waiting, the navigating, the gentle ribbing, the coordinating, the phone passed back and forth for one more photo that almost captures the moment.

Something in that podcast ranking — “number five in religion and spirituality on Apple Podcasts in Nicaragua” — surfaces as I sit here, and it quietly arrests me. It is mentioned almost in passing, nearly swallowed by conversation about cable car routes and camera angles. But I notice what the Spirit is doing in this detail: this is what faithfulness looks like from the inside. From the outside, there may be metrics, there may be rankings, there may be visible fruit reaching across the Pacific to Nicaragua — but from the inside, the one through whom God is working is *waiting for a cable car*, mildly arguing about cologne, trying to hold the phone steady for a group photo. The Lord has always worked this way. The prophet Elijah, fresh from the consuming fire of Carmel, sat under a juniper tree and asked to die. The extraordinary and the utterly mundane are never as far apart as our triumphalist theologies would prefer us to believe, and this transcript is a quiet witness to that truth.

Then the rain came — and with it, a wandering that led somewhere unplanned. The natural mind reads a closed restaurant as inconvenience and a wrong cable car as a mistake. But I am hearing something else as I sit with this. Somewhere in the middle of the wet and wandering afternoon, a declaration rose from within the group — *Yehovah Yair* — יהוה יָאִיר (Yair, yah-EER) — the Lord who illuminates, who causes light to shine, who provides and makes a way. At the root of that name is אוֹר (or, OHR) — light — and what was being spoken over that soaking wet, canceled-Uber, closed-restaurant afternoon was not merely that things had worked out. What was being declared was that God *shines* — that He causes even the confused and the hungry and the misdirected afternoon to radiate with His goodness, if we have eyes trained to perceive it. This declaration was not made after the shoes dried. It was made *in the rain*. And that timing is everything.

I am thinking now of Genesis 22, where יהוה יִרְאֶה (Yireh, yir-EH) — the Lord who sees and provides — became the name spoken over the mountain where Abraham lifted his hand. The provision on Moriah was not merely logistical — a ram in a thicket for a sacrificial emergency. It was revelatory. It unveiled a character of God that Abraham had not yet fully seen, and the mountain was named after the encounter because the encounter changed the way Abraham would perceive God’s nature going forward. And I find myself wondering whether this rain-soaked afternoon in San Francisco is not entirely unlike that — not in the weight of what was demanded, but in the quality of what was seen. A character of God was perceived and named aloud in a moment that required no naming. And naming it aloud — *the Lord has blessed us, roundabout trips and all* — is itself a prophetic act. It is the tongue declaring what the eye of faith has already seen, before the circumstances fully cooperate.

The table at the end of the walk arrests me most. A warm plate of pork adobo and longganisa found at the end of a series of wrong turns, canceled plans, and wet streets. *More rice, more power* — said half-jokingly over a meal no one planned to eat in a restaurant no one planned to enter. But Psalm 23:5 keeps pressing on me: *You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies.* The enemies in view are not always people. Sometimes they are the rain, the wrong train, the closed doors, the hunger, the fatigue — every force that seems to be opposing the journey. And yet: there is always a table. The table was there. The food was warm. The people were together. The Lord prepared it — in the presence of every inconvenience that pushed against the afternoon — and it was good. That is not accident. That is Providence, and it has a Hebrew word: יהוה יִרְאֶה.

What I am also sitting with is the way this company moved through the city — navigating, staying together, calling to each other across crowds, making room in a single Uber, saying *tara* — the Filipino word that means *let’s go*, a communal summons to move forward together. Throughout the day, people got separated and found each other again. Someone needed a bathroom. Someone lost their bearings on the transit system. Someone had to coach a Midwesterner through the unfamiliar ritual of swiping a transit card for the first time in a new city. None of this is theologically grand. And yet what I am witnessing is the shape of a people learning to be together across every human difference — across languages, across cultures, across familiarity with the city — and that learning is never wasted in the Kingdom of God. I find myself thinking of the word שָׁלוֹם (shalom, sha-LOHM) — not merely peace in the thin English sense, but wholeness, flourishing, the restoration of every fractured relationship to its right ordering. Shalom is not the absence of rain. It is the presence of the Lord in the rain, moving with a company of people who have decided to stay together.

The multilingual texture of this day will not leave me alone. English gives way to Tagalog, fragments of warmth pass between speakers in more than one tongue, there is code-switching and shared laughter at the same words landing differently in different ears. Far from being theologically empty, this linguistic mixing is itself a sign of the age we are living in. At Pentecost, the Spirit did not demand that every nation speak one approved tongue. He moved *through* the languages — each one heard the mighty works of God in their own γλῶσσα (glōssa, GLOHS-sah) (Acts 2:8–11). What I am seeing in this gathering is a small and imperfect but genuinely beautiful echo of that Pentecostal reality: people from Cebu, from Manila, from the Midwest, from a Mexican heritage, from North Dakota and Pennsylvania — all at one table, all feeding one another, all making room. The Kingdom of God is not monolingual. It never was. The day of Pentecost proved it, and this table proves it again in miniature.

Then there is the one-legged duck. I noticed that the group paused over it at the beginning of the afternoon — something that appeared diminished, standing upright without apology, apparently at ease in its own particular limitation. The laughter it provoked was uncomplicated and good, and I did not want to press it too hard into allegory. But the Spirit will not let me walk entirely past it. Paul knew this pattern: God has chosen the weak things of the world to shame the mighty (1 Corinthians 1:27). The Kingdom of God advances through instruments that appear inadequate — the one-legged duck standing in the sun, the wrong cable car that brought the group somewhere unplanned, the rain that drove them off the street into warmth. These are not failures dressed up as blessings. These are the actual texture of a life being held by a Father who does not panic when His children take the wrong line, and who is not embarrassed by one-legged things standing upright in His service.

The image of the Painted Ladies in San Francisco surfaces here and will not be set aside without reflection. One of the group observed it: built in 1890, earthquake came, all the houses around fell — and these are the only ones left, because they were made for survival. I am not reading prophecy into Victorian architecture. But I am taking seriously the image that was placed before this company on this particular afternoon: what endures through shaking is not what is merely old, but what is rightly built. Jesus made this precise point at the close of the Sermon on the Mount — the house built on the rock stands when the flood comes and the wind beats against it, while the house built on sand collapses at the moment it is most needed (Matthew 7:24–27). The σκηνόω (skēnoō, skay-NO-oh) — the dwelling, the tabernacled life — must be built on a foundation that the earthquake cannot move. Relationships built in the ordinary, through the mundane faithfulness of choosing to remain with one another through inconvenience, through rain, through the wrong cable car — these are not shallow foundations. These are the brick-by-brick work of covenant being laid in the most honest and durable way available: the simple practice of showing up.

I want to sit for a moment with the way Psalm 34:1 sounds against this afternoon: *I will bless the Lord at all times; His praise shall continually be in my mouth.* At all times — not when the circumstances cooperate, not when the restaurant is open, not after the shoes have dried. David wrote this, we are told in the heading, while feigning madness before Achish — which is to say, from a position of acute vulnerability and misdirection. The praise did not wait for resolution. And on this rainy afternoon in San Francisco, something of that Davidic instinct moved through wet shoes and a wandering group — the instinct to name God’s goodness before the comfort of having arrived anywhere. That instinct is not natural to the human heart. It is formed. It is cultivated through repeated choices to speak the character of God *into* the unresolved situation rather than *after* it resolves. The declaration made in the rain — *Yehovah Yair, the Lord has blessed us* — is Romans 8:28 not as a memorized verse but as a lived perception. συνεργεῖ (sunergei, soon-er-GAY) — the verb is present tense and active: God is *currently, actively* working all things together. Not retrospectively reassigning meaning to the afternoon, but presently weaving the wrong turn, the closed door, the rain into the fabric of something good.

There is a moment in the evening, watching through a camera at a home with a cat named Mountie and a cat named Squishy, where something quietly eschatological surfaces. The cross-eyed cat who eats twice and provokes shared laughter, the cat who receives his food while the family eats — I hear in this the echo of a people who have not forgotten that creation is good, that creaturely delight is not beneath the Kingdom but part of its texture. Isaiah 11:6 does not merely describe a future political arrangement; it describes the full restoration of the created order to its Edenic wholeness, where wolf and lamb, lion and calf, are gathered under the loving governance of the One who made them. When Squishy eats twice and the room laughs, something of that Edenic longing is being touched — the deep human recognition that the creation is still shot through with the goodness of its Maker, and that creaturely joy is a form of doxology.

And then the rain comes in full, and the group makes the decision together: *yeah, I wanna go home now. Yeah, me too. Are you ready? Tara.* Every good journey in Scripture ends with a turning homeward — the prodigal rising to go to his father, the disciples returning from the Mount of Transfiguration, the two at Emmaus rushing back to Jerusalem to report what their eyes had been opened to see. The journey is not the point. The table, the company, and the One who gathers them — these are the point. And when the rain comes and it is time to go home, the people of God go home together, still talking, still making room for one another in one Uber, still being the community they have been becoming, ordinary moment by ordinary moment, throughout a day that the natural mind would call unremarkable and the Spirit of God would call something closer to holy ground.

## Implications and Challenges

One of the quiet distortions of modern Western Christianity is the implicit conviction that spiritual life is measured primarily in *intensity* — that the deepest encounters with God are the loudest, the most emotionally elevated, the most obviously supernatural. This transcript, taken in its full texture, gently but firmly challenges that assumption. The Spirit of God does not require a dramatic setting to be present and active. If we have trained ourselves to recognize God only in the extraordinary — only in the altar call, only in the powerful meeting, only in the verified miracle — we will spend most of our lives missing Him. Because most of life is cable car lines, wrong trains, closed restaurants, and group photos taken in the rain. If He is not there, He is not enough.

There is a subtle but real danger in what might be called *platform Christianity* — the tendency to locate spiritual significance primarily in visible metrics and public ministry moments. The podcast ranking slipped into the conversation naturally, as a small shared joy, and there is nothing wrong with that. But the Spirit presses a gentle question here: do we know how to be *nobody* together? Can we wait for a cable car without quietly narrating our own significance, without needing the afternoon to mean something about us? The saints who have endured longest in genuine Kingdom fruitfulness are almost always the ones who can move invisibly through an ordinary afternoon — wet shoes and all — without requiring it to be a story about them.

The multilingual, multicultural texture of this day is a quiet rebuke to any parochialism we carry into the Body of Christ. The Kingdom is not American, not Anglo, not organized according to any single cultural center of gravity. If we have constructed our theology, our worship, or our community in ways that assume one culture is the default and all others are guests, this transcript — small and informal as it is — stands as a counter-witness. The Spirit moves through every language, every form of humor, every way of being human that He authored. And the community visible in this recording — carrying the flavors of Cebu and Manila and Mexico and the Midwest to a table in San Francisco — is doing something the Body of Christ needs more of, not less of.

There is also a challenge embedded in Mark’s observation about the Filipino cook who refused to adapt his seasoning to his customers’ palates, out of wounded pride, and subsequently lost his customers. Genuine hospitality requires the willingness to learn, adjust, and serve what is actually needed — not merely what I am comfortable offering. The servant who buries his talent in the earth rather than risk it in the marketplace has chosen the safety of self-protection over the vulnerability of faithfulness (Matthew 25:24–25). Rigidity that masquerades as integrity is still rigidity, and it closes the door on the very people it was supposed to serve.

A deeper challenge surfaces around the practice of *naming God in the ordinary*. We affirm in doctrine that God works all things together for good, and then we narrate our days without reference to Him at all — the restaurant was closed, the Uber was canceled, we found somewhere else. The declaration spoken in the rain — *Yehovah Yair, the Lord has blessed us* — was an act of prophetic narration: inserting God as the active agent in an afternoon that could have been told without Him. Romans 10:10 presses on me here — faith involves both the heart and the mouth, belief and confession together. The beloved are being invited not merely to feel grateful but to *say so* — to God, to one another, and to the air around them. Interior gratitude that never finds its voice has not yet completed what the Spirit intends for it.

Finally, there is an invitation here to receive the roundabout trip as formation rather than failure. Israel did not exit Egypt through the most efficient route — they were led into the wilderness deliberately, because God was building something in them that efficiency would have bypassed. Exodus 13:17–18 makes the startling disclosure that God avoided the shorter road *because He knew what His people were not yet ready for*. וַיַּסֵּב (vayyassev, vah-yah-SEV) — and He caused them to go around. The detour is not Israel’s failure; it is God’s intentional formation. The same God who closed a restaurant in San Francisco on a Monday afternoon and led His beloved to warm food and shared laughter somewhere unexpected is the same God who led Israel the long way through the wilderness — not to frustrate them, but to show them something they would have driven past if they had arrived where they intended.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

Spiritual maturity is partly measured by the breadth of our perception — by whether we can see the hand of God only in the formal and the dramatic, or whether we have learned to trace it through the full fabric of ordinary shared life. This day in San Francisco is a small tutorial in what might be called *providential perception*: the trained capacity to find God not only in the spectacular answer but in the closed door, the wrong turn, the rain, the one-legged duck, the cross-eyed cat, the table that was prepared before we arrived. Growing toward maturity means learning to narrate our days truthfully — not as sequences of events that happened to us, but as chapters in a story being written by a Father who is neither absent nor improvising, who is currently, actively, συνεργεῖ (soon-er-GAY)-ing all things toward good, even when the shoes are still wet.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

The obedience this day calls forth is deceptively simple: *stay with the people God has given you*. Not only in the hard moments — in the ordinary ones. Be genuinely, bodily, unhurriedly present. Navigate the unfamiliar city without losing one another. Receive the table that is prepared, even when it is not the table you reserved. Let the person beside you be the person they actually are — cologne and all — and choose to remain. Say *tara* together, and mean it: not merely *I am willing to go*, but *I am committed to going with you*. This is a form of Kingdom faithfulness that will rarely make a highlight reel, and that is precisely why it must be practiced intentionally, one ordinary afternoon at a time.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

The prophetic posture formed by this reflection is one of active, joyful witness to the character of God in the unscripted moments of life. The prophet does not only speak at the appointed assembly; the prophet speaks over the afternoon, over the rain, over the pork adobo and the wrong cable car and the wet shoes — declaring the Lord’s faithfulness before the situation resolves, naming the table that was prepared in the presence of every opposition, calling what is not yet visible into the realm of the declared. To cultivate this posture is to become a person who carries the testimony of God’s goodness into every ordinary hour — not as religious performance, but as a genuine, habituated way of seeing and speaking that is being formed in us, one Yehovah Yair at a time.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I come before You with a full heart — not for what was grand or formal in this day, but for what was warm and ordinary and shot through with Your presence: the cable car line and the cologne argument and the imperfect group photo, the rain and the one-legged duck and the wrong train, the closed restaurants and the table that was waiting at the end of the walk anyway, the cat with crossed eyes and the dog with his squeaky toy, the people from Cebu and Manila and Mexico and North Dakota and Pennsylvania gathered at one table in one city, laughing in more than one language and eating food carried across an ocean with pride and love. You are יהוה יָאִיר — the One who causes light to shine into every unscripted and uncooperative afternoon — and You are יהוה יִרְאֶה — the One who sees and provides before we know we need it. I make agreement with what Your servants have long declared: that You prepare a table in the presence of every difficulty, that You lead by the longer road because You know what we need to see along the way, that Your mercies are new every morning and present in every rain, and that the declaration of Your goodness made in the middle of the unresolved situation is not premature — it is the grammar of faith. Open the eyes of Your beloved to see You in the ordinary. Train our mouths to speak Your faithfulness before the circumstances cooperate. Make us grateful in the rain, generous with the declaration, and faithful to the people You have given us to travel with. Secure what You are building in these relationships, across every cultural line, through every ordinary afternoon — and bring this company home, all the way home, in the name of Jesus, who pitched His tent in our neighborhood and was never embarrassed by the ordinary. Amen.
