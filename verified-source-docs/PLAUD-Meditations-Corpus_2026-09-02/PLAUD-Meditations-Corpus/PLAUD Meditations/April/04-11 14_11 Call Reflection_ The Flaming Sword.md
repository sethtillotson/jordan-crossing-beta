## ⚡ Opening Observation
As I listened to my brother Samuel walk through the texture of his week — the long shifts, the new job opening, the African co-workers who unknowingly preached a sermon about honor and covenant, and the girl in Spain who became an unexpected diagnostic instrument — I kept hearing one phrase return like a bell struck in an empty room: *comfort loop*. He named it himself, and the naming of it mattered more than any theological point either of us made after. I have been pressing through my own version of that same gravitational pull, carrying the weight of a call I have delayed and a relationship I have left unreconciled, and the Lord, speaking through a sister in the remnant, asked me plainly whether I am still on the mountain or whether I have simply grown comfortable in the altitude.
**Why This Matters:**
The comfort loop is not a neutral condition. It is a spiritual deceleration — the kind that does not look like rebellion from the outside and does not even feel like disobedience from the inside, but functions as both. The saints who are slowing down are often the ones who have seen enough to feel settled, who have accumulated enough encounter to believe they no longer need to pursue encounter. This is the same subtle seduction that produced the Laodicean letter in Revelation — not hatred of God, not apostasy, not gross sin, but lukewarmness born of sufficiency. If Samuel and I are to be useful in the hands of the One who forms His servants in the refiner's fire, we cannot afford to mistake proximity for progress, or familiarity for intimacy.
---
## 🌍 The Worldly Mind's Interpretation
From a purely natural vantage point, both Samuel and I are doing well. He has steady employment, potential for advancement, social connection at work, and is showing what any counselor would call healthy self-awareness — testing his emotional responses to interaction, asking what still needs healing, pacing himself. I have a stable home, a wife I am devoted to, a developing ministry framework, and an active inner life. The conversations we have would read to any outside observer as two young men being responsible, growing up, building something. The African co-workers' commentary on marriage and provision would register as a cultural anthropology moment — interesting, even funny, but not spiritually charged. The theological tangents about the flaming sword, the conscience of God, Leviathan, eschatology, and the Roman church would appear to any secular analyst as recreational intellectual sparring between two friends who share a religious hobby. Nothing alarming. Nothing urgent. Everything relatively fine.
**The Limitation:**
But the natural mind cannot perceive the weight that rests underneath the surface of a casual conversation between two men who are actually under assignment. It does not have the apparatus to recognize that the same Holy Spirit who moved over the waters in Genesis is moving through the language of a tired factory worker and a sleep-deprived husband who are trying to outpace the enemy's most sophisticated strategy — not temptation toward obvious sin, but the slow erosion of holy urgency. The flesh cannot feel the difference between rest and stagnation, between a season of refining and a drift into comfort. Only the Spirit of God, speaking through the body of Christ and through the quiet press of His voice on the human conscience, can distinguish the two — and only those who are listening closely enough will hear the distinction before it costs them their assignment.

## ✝️ What the Spirit Reveals Through It
What strikes me first, sitting with this conversation, is that Samuel named his condition before I named it for him. He said, without prompting, that if he stays still, he will be living in a *comfort loop* — not falling away dramatically, but circling, revolving, gradually mistaking the loop for the road. There is something the Spirit wants me to mark in that. When a man can accurately diagnose his own spiritual stagnation, that is not his own wisdom speaking. That is the רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — Spirit / breath / wind — moving through his understanding and creating self-awareness that the natural mind resists by instinct. The flesh always prefers comfort. The Spirit always presses toward more. Samuel's naming of the loop is itself evidence that the loop is already losing its grip.
I followed his naming with something that surprised even me as it came out: *proximity breeds contempt*. I meant it in the sense that the more you think you know something, the less urgently you seek it. This is not abstract philosophy — it is a warning embedded in the very way Israel treated the manna. In Exodus 16, the bread from heaven was a miracle. By Numbers 11, they despised it. The manna had not changed. They had changed. They had seen it so many times that it registered as ordinary, and the ordinary breeds contempt. The Christian who has read the Bible five times and believes that reading constitutes knowing is in the same danger. Knowing *about* Jesus and being known *by* Jesus are not the same category of experience. One is information. The other is the cry of the disciples on the Emmaus road — "Did not our heart burn within us while He talked with us on the road?" (Luke 24:32). You cannot mistake that burning for routine familiarity.
I said something I want to return to and press on more carefully: a believer *knows*, but a disciple *seeks to encounter*. I said the disciple wants to stand face to face with eyes that will purify the soul. I was not being poetic for effect — I was reaching for something that the book of Job actually describes when God finally speaks from the whirlwind. Job's friends had argued about God. Job had argued *with* God. But when God appeared, Job did not get answers — he got presence. And that presence was enough. He said in Job 42:5, "I have heard of You by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees You." That is the difference between the believer who accumulates information and the disciple who endures to the point of direct sight. The disciple does not want theology as a destination. The disciple wants the One the theology points toward.
And then there is my own unresolved obedience — the phone call I have been delaying, the reconciliation the Holy Spirit has been pressing me toward. The sister in the remnant asked me whether I still feel like Elijah on the mountain, and the question landed like a surgeon's blade. Because Elijah on the mountain was not resting — he was hiding. In 1 Kings 19, Elijah fled under the juniper tree in exhaustion and despair, and the angel of the Lord fed him not once but twice, saying, "Arise and eat, because the journey is too great for you." God did not rebuke Elijah for being tired. He fed him. But then He asked him a question: "What are you doing here, Elijah?" Not *why are you here*, but *what are you doing here*. The question assumes Elijah had an assignment that was not being fulfilled in the place where he was resting. I hear that same question being pressed against my own delay. What are you doing here, still carrying this unreconciled thing, when the Spirit has already told you what to do?
The Holy Spirit has been speaking clearly: I need to call a brother, someone who prayed for me during a season before I was where I am now, and thank him. Not to resolve the debt between us — that is a separate matter. But to tell him that his prayers were answered, that the Lord came and found me, that the intercession he offered did not return void. I have been hesitant because the debt exists, and I have been afraid that calling about the testimony will feel dishonest while the debt is still unresolved. But the Spirit has pressed through that logic: *tell him the call is about your testimony. He prayed for you. I came and found you. Tell him that.* The instruction is not complicated. My hesitation is not discernment — it is disobedience dressed in the language of discretion. And the longer I carry it, the more it douses what should be a fire. The water is not coming from the enemy. It is coming from my own hand.
When I said in the conversation that the enemy's tactic is not to destroy the saint outright but to *slow him down*, I was speaking from something the Spirit had already impressed on me. If the enemy cannot stop your obedience, he will work to delay it. And delay long enough becomes effective enough. A man can die having started his assignment but never having fulfilled it in the fullness of what God intended — not because he was faithless, but because he was slow enough that the window closed. I do not say that to produce fear. I say it because the urgency of obedience is not legalistic pressure — it is covenant love. When the Spirit says move, you move because you are responding to the One you love, not because you fear what happens if you don't.
The conversation took a remarkable theological turn when Samuel brought up the conscience of God. He was pressing on a real question: if God flooded the earth and then promised never to do it again, does that not imply something like moral development, or at least moral consistency shaped by His own nature? And I said it plainly: His conscience is perfect. We are made in His image and likeness. Genesis 1:26-27 does not say we were made *as* Him. It says we were made *in His image* — בְּצַלְמֵנוּ (betzalmenu, beh-tzahl-MEH-noo) — in our image. The image carries the conscience, the capacity for moral reasoning, the ability to distinguish between right and wrong. So our conscience is not our invention. It is derivative of His. That is why when the human conscience is undistorted by sin, it still recognizes what is good, even without explicit instruction. Paul confirms this in Romans 2:14-15 — the Gentiles who do not have the law do by nature the things of the law, showing the work of the law written in their hearts. The conscience is not merely psychological. It is a theological faculty.
Then Samuel made a connection that I found genuinely beautiful and sobering in the same breath. He said: the Romans killed Jesus. The Romans also built the world's largest church. He sat with that today at work, and I could hear him still sitting with it when he brought it up. And yes — I added immediately that the Roman church is still Babylon, that it is still Rome in its ecclesiastical dress, that it is still more performance than presence in many of its expressions. But I did not let that override what is also true: all things work together for the good of those who love God and are called according to His purpose (Romans 8:28). God used the empire that crucified His Son to carry His Son's name to the ends of the earth. That is not irony. That is sovereignty. The very instrument of shame became the instrument of proclamation — which is itself a typological echo of the cross itself. The instrument of execution became the instrument of salvation. God does not waste what the enemy intends for destruction. He recycles it into glory.
And this brought us naturally to the mystery of the flaming sword. When Adam and Eve were expelled from the garden in Genesis 3:24, the Lord placed cherubim and a flaming sword which turned every direction to guard the way to the tree of life. I said in the conversation that the flaming sword is what you must pass through to get back to communion with God — and that the sword cuts and burns. But I want to press this further now: the flaming sword is not merely a barrier. It is a description of the nature of re-entry. To come back into the presence of the Holy God, you must be cut open by truth and burned clean by His Spirit. This is not punishment. This is the nature of holiness. The prophet Isaiah experienced this in Isaiah 6, when the seraph touched his lips with a live coal from the altar: "Your iniquity is taken away, and your sin purged." The burning was mercy. The cutting was love. The sword that guards the garden is not your enemy — it is the description of what the cross accomplishes for every soul that is willing to pass through it.
This is why I also said that I would rather have my life systematically dismantled by God over years than be ripped in half at the judgment. The double-minded man of James 1:8 — δίψυχος (dipsychos, DIP-soo-khos) — double-souled, literally — is not the obvious apostate. He is the one who is half in and half out, loving the world and wanting God, seeking the blessing and resisting the burning. And the sobering word Jesus speaks to that person is not condemnation but a warning: the judgment will separate what is not of the Father from what is. The purification that happens in this life through the process of discipleship, obedience, repentance, and the work of the Spirit — that is the mercy version. That is the flaming sword we choose to walk through now rather than face without preparation later.
I ended the conversation with something that came out almost in passing but I want to anchor here: Samuel noticed that every sentence he was hearing was beginning with *I*. And I said — that is exactly the problem with the institutional church. It is not about me. It is about Christ in me. The Greek phrase is central to the Pauline epistles — Χριστὸς ἐν ἐμοί (Christos en emoi, khree-STOS en eh-MOY) — Christ in me. Colossians 1:27 calls this mystery the hope of glory. Not Christ *for* me, as if He were a service provider. Not Christ *near* me, as if He were a companion at a distance. Christ *in* me — inhabiting, indwelling, permeating, expressing Himself through the vessel that has been broken open enough to be transparent. The moment the Christian life becomes about *me* — my comfort, my blessing, my breakthrough, my safety — it has left the gravity of the gospel. The gospel is not self-improvement with divine assistance. It is death and resurrection. It is the old man crucified so the new man, hidden with Christ in God, can breathe.

## 🤔 Implications and Challenges
The comfort loop is a real and present danger for growing disciples, and the church at large has systematically failed to name it. Much of Western Christianity has built its entire architecture around spiritual comfort — comfortable seating, comfortable music, comfortable messages that affirm the congregation's existing assumptions and return them to their week feeling better rather than burning differently. But comfort is not the fruit of the Spirit. Love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control (Galatians 5:22-23) are fruit — and fruit requires seasons of winter. The disciple who never experiences discomfort is a disciple who has never really moved.
The conflation of Bible knowledge with relational intimacy is perhaps the most dangerous form of spiritual pride in the Western church. A man can have a PhD in systematic theology and be unknown by Jesus. The seven sons of Sceva in Acts 19 invoked the name of Jesus and the evil spirit said, "Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are you?" Knowing the name is not the same as being known by the Name. The church needs to recover the terrifying and beautiful distinction between doctrinal fluency and transforming encounter — not to despise theology, but to refuse to let theology replace the One theology describes.
The delay of obedience is not a minor spiritual inefficiency. It is a form of practical unbelief. If I truly believe that the Spirit of the Living God is pressing me toward an action — a phone call, a reconciliation, a word spoken, a step taken — and I defer that action without clear divine instruction to wait, then I am effectively saying: I know better than the Spirit about the timing of my obedience. That is a form of pride dressed in the clothing of prudence. The disciple who is actually listening does not accumulate unanswered promptings. He moves when moved, and trusts the One who moves him.
The world has wisdom about covenant that the Western church has largely abandoned, and God will sometimes use secular voices to press that wound. The African and Polish co-workers who described a wife who cooks, who serves, who honors — they were not preaching a sermon about gender roles in a theological sense. They were witnessing to a form of covenant love that the Western world has traded for radical individualism. Every human being carries within them a longing for a relationship where they are genuinely cared for, not because they performed well enough, but because they are *seen*. That longing is not created by culture. It is created by the One whose own covenant love is described in Hosea — the husband who pursues the unfaithful wife and covers her with His garments and says, "You are mine."
The missionary movement of the Roman Empire — even with all of its theological corruption and institutional violence — cannot be dismissed without also dismissing the sovereignty of God. This is a challenge to the kind of theological puritanism that refuses to acknowledge God's fingerprints on imperfect instruments. God used a donkey. God used a pagan king named Cyrus whom He called *His anointed* in Isaiah 45. God used the empire that crucified His Son to carry His Son's name to the nations. The right response to this is not to endorse every institution God has ever used, but to remain humble about the breadth and sovereignty of His purposes and to resist the arrogance of assuming He can only work through the theologically refined.
Finally — the Christos en emoi (khree-STOS en eh-MOY) reality is the corrective to every form of self-centered spirituality, and yet it must be preached with precision. Christ in me does not mean I am Christ. It does not mean my intuitions are automatically divine. It means that the same Spirit who raised Jesus from the dead now dwells in the mortal body of the believer (Romans 8:11) and is shaping that body, that mind, that will, toward conformity with the Son. The fruit of that indwelling is not self-aggrandizement. It is the willingness to become less so that He might become more — the posture of John the Baptist, who said in John 3:30, "He must increase, but I must decrease."

## 🚀 What This Means For Us
**For Spiritual Maturity:**
The comfort loop is broken not by dramatic crisis but by intentional obedience in the small things the Spirit has already named. Maturity is not measured by the size of the revelation you have received, but by the faithfulness with which you have acted on the last thing you were told. For me, that means making the phone call today. For every disciple reading this, it means asking honestly: what is the last clear instruction I received and have not yet obeyed? Begin there. The mountain climbs from the step you are currently standing on.
**For Kingdom Obedience:**
When the Spirit says move, you move. This is not spiritual performance. This is covenant responsiveness — the same responsiveness that Abraham demonstrated when he rose early in the morning, saddled his donkey, and took Isaac toward Moriah without demanding an explanation first (Genesis 22:3). The obedience came before the provision was revealed. The angel stayed his hand only after Abraham had already shown his heart. Kingdom obedience is not contingent on understanding the full plan. It is contingent on trusting the full Person.
**For Prophetic Posture:**
The saint who is in the refiner's fire and knows it is not the saint who is suffering meaninglessly — that saint is the one being prepared for a specific assignment that requires a specific kind of character that can only be forged in a specific kind of heat. Samuel is in that fire. I am in that fire. The proper posture is not to escape the fire or to demand that it end, but to remain in it with open hands and a willing will, watching carefully for what the enemy sends as counterfeit comfort — the flirtation that seems harmless, the theology that seems complete, the relationship that feels resolved without the work of true reconciliation. The prophetic saint stays awake to the counterfeits precisely because the genuine thing is so near.

I will not let the debt that is still unresolved keep me from delivering the testimony that belongs to You. Your Son's name is on the lips of every generation, engraved into the very architecture of how the world counts its years. Let it be equally engraved in the way I move, the way I obey, the way I love, the way I refuse to stay still when You have said go. To the One who permeates all things, who is in the breath of my speaking and the turning of every season, be all glory, honor, and power — now and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
