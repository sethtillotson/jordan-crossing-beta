# 06-12 Call Reflection: Brother’s Return to Grandfather’s House — Grief, Faith, and Generational Inheritance

**Title:** 06-12 at 17:35 — The Chair Still Holds the Shape of Him

**Recorded:** 2026-06-12 17:35:21

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture)

**Classification:** Call Reflection

## Opening Observation

As I listened to my brother move through his grandfather’s house — his voice shifting between grief and wonder, between the weight of what was lost and the strange warmth of what remained — something in me grew very still. This was not simply a man revisiting a childhood memory; this was a man standing at the intersection of generations, holding a name, holding a chair, holding the echo of a life that shaped him before he had words for it. There is something the Spirit does at thresholds like this that no theology textbook adequately prepares you for — a holy convergence of time, love, loss, and inheritance that Scripture calls to mind in ways the natural mind cannot immediately locate but the heart already knows.

**Why This Matters:**

The saints of God are not disconnected individuals floating through time. We are people of lineage, of inheritance, of covenantal continuity. When a man sits in his grandfather’s chair and feels both the absence and the nearness of the one who sat there before him, something eternal is pressing through the temporal. If we cannot receive that pressure with discernment — if we neither spiritualize it into abstraction nor flatten it into mere sentimentality — we will miss one of the most intimate ways the Holy Spirit teaches us about our place in the story of God. My brother’s return to that house was not just personal; it was prophetic in the deepest domestic sense, and I am still turning it over.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

The natural mind, when it encounters a scene like this, moves swiftly toward the psychological: grief processing, nostalgia, the neurological comfort of familiar spaces, the emotional complexity of returning to a place shaped by someone now gone. It might note the family dispute over the land, the fences where open fields once breathed, the willow tree that was cut down, and interpret all of it through the lens of loss and the passage of time. The natural mind would call the smashing of the Thai prayer figures an act of territorial emotion, perhaps even an overreaction, and it would file the sense of the grandfather’s presence under the category of memory projection — the mind constructing what the heart desperately wants to feel. From that vantage point, this whole experience is touching, perhaps cathartic, but ultimately reducible to human psychology and the universally shared ache of mortality.

**The Limitation:**

But the natural mind, for all its compassionate framing, cannot perceive the dimension in which this encounter was actually happening. There is a kind of knowing that arrives not through reasoning but through receptivity — the capacity to discern that what you are standing in is more than geography, more than biography, more than grief. When my brother said, *“I felt him with me when I walked through the door, but he wasn’t here — he was just with me,”* he was not describing a hallucination or a coping mechanism. He was describing a distinction that only the spiritually attuned can hold: the difference between a soul *trapped* in a place and a love that *accompanies* the living. That discernment is not native to the natural mind. It is the fruit of walking with the Holy Spirit closely enough to recognize the difference between the presence of the dead lingering in fear and the love of the departed resting in peace.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

I want to begin here, in the chair. My brother sat down in the same seat his grandfather occupied for years — a man who would look out over a field called West View and think for hours in silence. I am arrested by that image. There is something in it that resonates with the ancient Hebrew concept of זָכַר (zakar, ZAH-kar) — to remember, to call to mind, to actively invoke the reality of what has been. The Psalms are full of this posture. The psalmist does not merely recall; he זָכַר, he summons memory as a spiritual act that reconnects the present to the covenant. When my brother sat in that chair, he was doing something deeply biblical even if he had no category for it yet: he was performing an act of sacred remembrance, a זָכַר, that honored a life and reached through time toward meaning.

What strikes me further is that the grandfather was a man of quiet contemplation. He looked at that field, West View, and thought in silence for hours. I do not know what he believed about God. But I know that the pattern of a man who sits, observes, and meditates on what is before him is a pattern Scripture honors. The first Psalm opens with that posture — the man who does not walk in the counsel of the ungodly but who instead meditates, יֶהְגֶּה (yehgeh, yeh-GEH) — on the law of the LORD day and night. There is a kind of inheritance in contemplative posture itself, something passed from generation to generation in the act of sitting still and watching the fields move. Whether or not that grandfather knew the LORD by name, the Spirit can work generational patterns of receptivity into a family line in ways that become the very soil from which faith later grows.

I am thinking now about the land itself — the land that was divided by dispute, the fences that appeared where there were none. And I cannot help but hear the echo of Joshua and the allotments, or the lament of the prophets over Israel’s divided inheritance. Scripture takes boundaries seriously. Proverbs warns against removing the ancient landmark set by the fathers (Proverbs 22:28). When family disputes fragment what was once a unified inheritance, it is not merely a legal matter; it is a covenantal wound. The field called West View, once whole and unbroken, now parceled and fenced — this is the kind of image that carries the weight of Micah 2:2: *“They covet fields and take them by violence, also houses, and seize them. So they oppress a man and his house, a man and his inheritance.”* My brother felt this. He felt the wrongness of what had been done, not just emotionally but spiritually, in his bones.

And then there was the smashing of the Thai prayer figures. I want to sit with this rather than pass over it quickly. My brother entered a house that had been occupied by someone who had introduced spiritual objects foreign to the covenant of Christ into a space belonging to a family line. He did not hesitate. He did not ask permission. He smashed them. And then he said, without drama and without performance, that he was not intimidated — because he has the Lord Jesus Christ, and demons should fear that name. I felt something rise in my spirit when he said that, because it was not bravado. It was the simple confession of a man who has learned where his authority actually comes from. This is the spirit of those in Acts 19:19 who, when they believed, gathered their books of magic and burned them publicly — not out of fear, but out of the settled knowledge that Christ has already disarmed principalities and powers, παρρησίᾳ (parrēsia, par-RAY-see-ah) — with open boldness — making a public spectacle of them in His triumph (Colossians 2:15). My brother walked into his grandfather’s house and exercised dominion. That is the correct response.

The prayer over the house that followed — or rather, the desire for it — moved me deeply. My brother asked what the strongest prayer would be. I told him the simplest prayer with faith is the strongest. He landed on the Lord’s Prayer and then shifted to the idea of opening the Word, lighting a candle, and doing a Bible study focused on cleansing, family, and new beginnings. That instinct is right and it is beautiful. The Word of God is not merely informational; it is performative. When Ezekiel prophesied to the valley of dry bones, the word spoken became the breath that filled them (Ezekiel 37:4-10). When the disciples asked how to pray, Jesus gave them not a mystical formula but a covenantal framework — the Lord’s Prayer is a declaration of the Kingdom’s reality into whatever room it enters. To open Scripture in that house was to let the living Word begin its work of cleansing and consecration.

I am also struck by what my brother said about the name. The Collins name. Frederick Collins, who fought in both World Wars and built that house with the weight of history in his hands. My brother said the house doesn’t just belong to his grandfather — it belongs to that name. And he intends to leave it as a Collins would. I hear in this something of the priestly inheritance, the tribal name, the sense in which identity is not merely individual but dynastic. The New Testament does not erase this understanding; it deepens it. We who are in Christ are given a new name written on a white stone (Revelation 2:17), yet we are also grafted into an ancient family — the household of faith, the commonwealth of Israel (Ephesians 2:12), the covenant of the fathers renewed and fulfilled in the Son. My brother’s insistence on leaving that house with dignity is not mere pride. It is covenant fidelity to a name he bears.

The willow tree being cut down stopped me. My brother had climbed that tree more times than he could count. His grandfather had tended it. And it was cut down in the aftermath of family fracture. The willow in Scripture does not carry the symbolic weight of the cedar or the oak, but trees in biblical imagination almost always carry covenantal significance — they mark places where covenants were made, where God appeared, where fathers planted legacies for their children. When Abraham planted a tamarisk tree in Beersheba and called there on the name of the LORD (Genesis 21:33), the tree was a marker of belonging, of rootedness, of covenant memory. When that willow was cut down, something was being severed that went beyond horticulture. My brother knew it immediately. He said, *“Man, that’s fucked.”* And then, quietly, *“He’s still here. He’s still here. I can feel it.”* The tree was taken, but the presence it once shaded was not destroyed by the cutting.

I sat with that for a long time. Because there is a gospel word hidden inside it. You can cut down the tree, divide the land, break the fences, scatter the family, remove the landmarks — and yet the presence of the LORD, and the love of the saints, cannot be landlocked by fences or silenced by chainsaws. The Spirit is not contained by real estate. My brother felt his grandfather not *in* the house but *with* him as he entered it — and I believe the Spirit was using that distinction to show him something: that what he had inherited from that man was not the property. It was the posture. The contemplative gaze out over the West View field. The willingness to sit and think and feel and remember. The love that endures when the physical forms have all changed.

My own grandfather’s cabin came to mind during that call. I know exactly what my brother was describing — the way a place holds the echo of a person long after they are gone, the way walking through a door can feel like walking through time. The last words my grandfather said to me were ridiculous and perfect and very much him — and they made me laugh, and that laughter was a kind of grace. Grief, I am learning, does not have only one face. Sometimes it wears the face of someone saying the most irreverent thing at exactly the right moment, and you laugh instead of weep, and somehow both are true at once. This is the mystery of the resurrection hope pressing itself through ordinary human affection: death does not get to have the last word, not even in the small things, not even in the last sentences of elderly men who don’t want their soda anymore.

The grandfather’s story about New York — meeting his friend from Widnes in the same bar in that enormous city, unplanned, unannounced, against all statistical probability — I received that as a testimony of divine sovereignty wearing the clothing of coincidence. The Hebrew mind would call this הַשְׁגָּחָה (hashgachah, hash-gah-KHAH) — providence, the careful oversight of God over all the movements of His creation. The Greeks had no single word for this because they had no single God. But the covenant people always knew: there is a Hand that arranges meetings in unexpected cities, that places two men in the same bar across an ocean, that folds time and space around the small things of human life so that His people might see, even in the secular story, that they were never truly alone. My brother received his grandfather’s last great story as a gift, and I receive the telling of it as a testimony that the God of all providence was present in that grandfather’s life in ways the grandfather himself may not have fully mapped.

And finally, I return to the chair. The chair in the conservatory, facing West View. My brother sat in it and had a beer with his father, and they were there to clean the house and restore it and leave it as it ought to be. There is something quietly Christological in that image that I cannot shake — the Son returning to the Father’s house to put it in order, to drive out what does not belong, to restore what was broken, to honor what was built. Jesus cleansed the Temple not with bureaucratic procedure but with righteous authority, because *“zeal for Your house has eaten Me up”* (John 2:17; Psalm 69:9). My brother had zeal for that house. He smashed the idols. He prayed through the rooms. He wanted the Word opened within its walls. He sat in his grandfather’s chair and looked out over a changed and fractured landscape and refused to let grief have dominion over the honor due to that name. That is not merely sentiment. That is the Spirit of adoption crying out through a son who knows whose house he is in.

## Implications and Challenges

There is a temptation in Western Christianity to spiritualize grief to the point of bypassing it entirely — to say, *“He’s in a better place,”* and use that truth as a way of closing the door too quickly on the sacred weight of loss. What I witnessed in my brother was the opposite: he allowed the weight to be full weight. He sat in the chair. He looked at the fences. He grieved the willow tree. He felt the strangeness of sitting where his grandfather used to sit. And in doing all of that, he was not wallowing — he was honoring. Grief, when held in the Spirit, becomes a form of love that the resurrection does not negate but fulfills.

There is also a challenge here for how the church handles the spaces and objects of the dead. My brother walked into a house that had been spiritually compromised by objects dedicated to another spiritual allegiance. He did not perform a liturgical exorcism. He did not call a pastor. He smashed the idols and declared the name of Christ. This kind of immediate, unadorned authority in the believer is something the modern Western church has often outsourced to specialists, to programs, to scheduled prayer teams. But the New Testament places this authority in the hands of every believer who walks in genuine covenant with the Lord. My brother exercised that authority naturally, almost casually, and that is exactly right. It should be that natural.

The fracturing of the family inheritance over the land is a warning worth sitting with. Disputes over physical inheritance are one of the most common ways the enemy fractures families and severs generational continuity. Jesus warned that a house divided against itself cannot stand (Matthew 12:25). The fences that now bisect what was once open land are not just legal outcomes; they are spiritual wounds. The church must speak into family disputes before they become permanent landmarks — must call the sons and daughters back to the covenant of love that transcends property lines.

The desire my brother had — to open the Bible in that house, to let the Word be present in those rooms — reflects a biblical instinct that deserves affirmation and cultivation. Scripture was never meant only for sanctuaries or study rooms. It was meant to be in the home, written on the doorposts, spoken at the table, opened in the places where life actually happens. Deuteronomy 6:7 commands this: to speak the words of God when you sit in your house, when you walk by the way, when you lie down, when you rise up. To bring the Bible into a grandfather’s house as an act of consecration is to take that command seriously in its most incarnate form.

Finally, the Collins name tattooed on my brother’s hand and the insistence on leaving the house as *“a Collins would”* — this is a form of covenant identity that the church ought to understand and honor rather than dismiss as ethnic pride. We are people of names and lineages and inheritances. The saints throughout Scripture carried their names as covenantal identities — names that were sometimes changed by God to mark new purposes but never erased without replacement. To honor the name one bears, to carry it with integrity into the next generation, is a form of faithfulness that Scripture celebrates from Genesis through Revelation.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

Sitting in the chair of those who came before us — whether literally or figuratively — requires a kind of mature receptivity that neither sentimentalizes the past nor dismisses its weight. Spiritual maturity means learning to hold grief and gratitude in the same hand, to receive an inheritance without idolizing it, to honor what was built while clearing out what was left behind that should not remain. As I listened to my brother navigate all of that in real time, I was struck by how much wisdom the Spirit had already deposited in him — wisdom that showed up not as theology but as behavior: smashing the idols, opening the Word, sitting in the chair, honoring the name.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

The call to consecrate our spaces is not a call to religious performance. It is a call to take seriously that every threshold, every room, every chair and field and fence line exists within the domain of the King who made heaven and earth. Kingdom obedience means praying through the house — not as a superstitious ritual but as a genuine declaration that this place belongs to the LORD and His covenant people, that darkness has no authority here, that the name of Jesus Christ supersedes every other name written on or in these walls.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

To sit in the chair and look out over the changed landscape — to see where the fences now stand where open land once breathed, to feel the absence of the willow tree — and still say, *“He’s still here, I can feel it,”* is a prophetic posture. It is the posture of faith that does not require unchanged circumstances in order to perceive the unchanging presence of God. The prophetic saint is trained not to mistake stillness for absence, not to mistake loss for defeat, not to mistake the cutting of the tree for the uprooting of the covenant. We learn to discern the difference. We learn to hold the weight of what was and the hope of what is coming without letting either one crowd out the other.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I come before You now as one who stands in the inheritance of the saints, a servant of the living God, a co-heir with Christ Jesus, bound by covenant to the great cloud of witnesses and to the brethren You have placed beside me in this generation. I thank You for that phone call, for the sound of a brother walking through a grandfather’s house with grief and faith braided together, for the smashing of idols done without hesitation, for the desire to open Your Word in rooms that needed it. Lord, I pray for that house — let it be cleansed thoroughly by the blood of Jesus and the word of testimony, let every spiritual assignment against that family line be severed at the root, let the name of Collins be redeemed and consecrated to Your purposes in ways that go far beyond property and land. I ask You to honor the memory of the man who sat in that chair and looked over that field for hours in silence — I ask that whatever seeds of seeking were planted in his life, You who know all things would account them to Your mercy. And Lord, for my brother who went back — let the return become a consecration, let the grief become a gateway, let the remembrance become a זָכַר (ZAH-kar) that reaches forward into his children’s children. Let the house be left as it ought to be. Let the name be honored. And let the LORD who watches over all our inheritances receive the glory due His name. Amen.
