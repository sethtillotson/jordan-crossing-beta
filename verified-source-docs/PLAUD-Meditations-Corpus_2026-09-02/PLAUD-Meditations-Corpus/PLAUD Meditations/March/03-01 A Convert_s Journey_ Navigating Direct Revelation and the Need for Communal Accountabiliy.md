# 03-01 Reflection: God’s Calling, Weakness, and Surrender

# Reflection: Called into Weakness, Clothed in Christ

## Opening Observation

I find myself sitting with the stunning paradox of God’s calling. Today I’m thinking about how the Kingdom of Heaven operates on a completely inverted logic to that of the world. In our striving for strength, recognition, and self-sufficiency, God consistently chooses the weak, the foolish, and the humbled to display His own incomparable strength. It is a scandalous, beautiful, and deeply confronting truth.

**Why This Matters:** For any of us on the path of radical discipleship, understanding this principle is crucial. It frees us from the tyranny of performance and the pride of self-reliance. It teaches us that our qualification for Kingdom work isn’t our resume, but our surrender. God is not looking for our ability, but our availability, so that when He moves, He alone gets the glory, and our flesh has nothing to boast in.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

From a purely human perspective, this makes no sense. The world’s logic is built on meritocracy, strength, and demonstrable success. To build an enterprise, you hire the most qualified. To win a battle, you send your strongest soldiers. To lead a movement, you find the most charismatic and influential speaker. The natural mind would look at God’s choices—a shepherd boy against a giant, a man with a speech impediment to confront a king, an obscure carpenter on a cursed tree as a Savior—and see strategic failure. It’s impractical, inefficient, and illogical. It’s a business plan for bankruptcy and a battle plan for defeat.

**The Limitation:**

This is precisely why “the natural man does not receive the things of the Spirit of God, for they are foolishness to him; nor can he know them, because they are spiritually discerned” (1 Corinthians 2:14, NKJV). The worldly mind, operating on a scorecard of human achievement, cannot grasp a game where the final score was settled on a cross. It seeks a god of logic and power that fits its own framework, like the Greeks who desired wisdom or the Jews who sought a sign. It cannot comprehend a God whose power is perfected in weakness and whose wisdom is revealed in the “foolishness” of the cross.

## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation

As I sit with this, the Spirit presses a question into my heart: Where have I placed my hope? For so long, I thought I believed, but in truth, my hope was in myself—my intellect, my resilience, my ability to build. I was caught in a binary of my own making: empire or death. I would either achieve generational success through my own striving, or I would perish in the attempt. I was paying for website domains over buying food, willing to let myself starve for the sake of my pride, for the fortresses I had built between myself and God. But He is a jealous God, and He will not share His glory with another. It was a single, devastatingly simple sentence from my beloved wife—“I guess I’ll just go to bed hungry”—that finally shattered my fortress. I could starve for my own pride, but I could not let her starve for it.

That moment of breaking was my final surrender. It led me to a job I never would have chosen, becoming an aluminum welder in a factory surrounded by farmland, far from the world of tech and innovation I had idolized. I had to die to my own ambitions. It took three major breakings over seven years, the complete dismantling of every wall I’d constructed, for me to finally hear Him. He gave me obscurity when I wanted fame, and literal starvation when I sought wealth. He gave me the opposite of what my flesh strived for to show me how desperately I needed Him. And it was there, on that factory floor, that the Lord Jesus finally spoke. It wasn’t a booming, audible sound, but words that dropped directly into my spirit, in a voice I knew was His. All He said was, “Read your Bible.”

The simplicity of that command was staggering. At that very moment, I had just solved a core problem in artificial general intelligence, an invention potentially worth a fortune in the world’s eyes. I, who had built a complex system I called “Symphoiarch” (from the Greek for collective creation governance: συν- “together,” ποίησις “creation,” and ἀρχή “governance”), was given the most basic instruction. The Creator of the universe looked at my greatest intellectual achievement and essentially told me to go back to kindergarten. This reminds me of the Apostle Paul, who, after listing his impeccable worldly and religious credentials, declared them all to be σκύβαλα (*skybala*)—rubbish, refuse, dung—“for the excellence of the knowledge of Christ Jesus my Lord” (Philippians 3:8). My billion-dollar idea was nothing next to the treasure of knowing Him.

This is the divine pattern. The Lord consistently chooses the “foolish things of the world to put to shame the wise,” and the “weak things of the world to put to shame the things which are mighty” (1 Corinthians 1:27). He called Moses, a man “slow of speech and slow of tongue” (Exodus 4:10), to declare His word to Pharaoh. He anointed David, a shepherd boy, to fell the giant Goliath. His own Son, the eternal Word, came not as a conquering king but as a humble carpenter from an obscure town, whose path of salvation led to the cross—an object of shame and a symbol of being cursed under the Law of Deuteronomy. It was a scandal to both the wisdom-seeking Greek and the sign-seeking Jew. God looked at the world’s scorecard and revealed He wasn’t even playing the same game.

And so I find myself in this tension, this sacred space between revelation and community. Having come from a completely secular background just months ago, I feel like an eyeball rolling around, looking for its place in the Body. The Spirit has convicted me not necessarily to find a building, but to find a brother or sister more versed in the Word, someone to sharpen me as iron sharpens iron. It’s easy to receive revelation in isolation, but the gathering of the saints, the *ekklēsia* (ἐκκλησία), is where we are refined. We are called to be living stones, and stones are shaped by rubbing against each other. The very annoyances and disagreements we find in fellowship are part of God’s sanctifying work, teaching us the grace we ourselves so desperately need, and protecting us from the deceptions of the lone wolf.

## Implications and Challenges

In the spirit of grace and a desire for our mutual upbuilding in Christ, I offer these thoughts for my beloved brethren to prayerfully consider, as Priscilla and Aquila once gently corrected the fervent Apollos.

- **The Challenge to “Expert” Culture:** Many modern church cultures elevate academic degrees, professional polish, and charismatic presentation. Yet, God’s pattern suggests He delights in anointing the “unlearned and ignorant men” (Acts 4:13) whose only qualification is that they have “been with Jesus.” Are we creating platforms that favor worldly resumes over Spirit-filled surrender?
- **The Challenge to Attractional Models:** The Gospel is, at its core, a “stumbling block” and “foolishness” (1 Corinthians 1:23). Many church growth strategies focus on making the message palatable and removing friction. But Scripture shows that friction is often the very tool the Spirit uses for sanctification. Are we avoiding necessary “rubbing of stones” out of fear of losing attendees, thereby hindering true spiritual growth?
- **The Challenge to Individualistic Faith:** While personal relationship with Christ is paramount, the New Testament vision is inescapably corporate. The “lone wolf” Christian, while perhaps receiving genuine insight, is vulnerable and misses the refining and accountability of the Body. Are we championing a “me and Jesus” spirituality at the expense of messy, difficult, but necessary fellowship as the *ekklēsía*?

## What This Means For Us:

**For Your Spiritual Maturity:**

Embrace your weakness. Stop seeing your inadequacies, your lack of knowledge, or your past failures as disqualifiers. They are the very platforms upon which God will display His power. Your job is not to be strong, but to be His.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

Listen for the simple commands. In a world screaming for complex solutions, God often whispers, “Read your Bible.” “Love your neighbor.” “Forgive.” Our obedience in the small things is the training ground for the great things He has prepared for us.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

Yield. Stop striving. The posture of the prophet is not one of hustle, but of abiding. It is in the surrender, in the obscure places, on the factory floors of our lives, that we are positioned to hear His voice clearly and be sent out in His authority, not our own.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, forgive me for the times I have trusted in my own strength, my own intellect, my own plans. Thank you for the scandalous grace that chooses me not because of what I can do, but in spite of it. Lord Jesus, teach me to glory in my weaknesses, so that Your power may rest upon me. May I count all my worldly achievements as loss for the surpassing worth of knowing You. Make me a living stone, willing to be shaped and fitted into Your holy temple, for Your glory alone. Amen.

|--------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Philippians 3:8    | σκύβαλα (skybala)                                           | This is a strong, visceral word. It doesn’t just mean “worthless” but refuse, dung, or garbage. Paul uses it to describe his elite pedigree and accomplishments when compared to the value of knowing Christ, showing the radical re-evaluation that the Gospel brings. |
| 1 Corinthians 1:27 | τὰ μωρὰ τοῦ κόσμου (ta mōra tou kosmou)                     | “The foolish things of the world.” Mōra is the root of our word “moron.” The Spirit intentionally uses language that highlights the world’s utter contempt for God’s methods to show the inversion of Kingdom values.                                                   |
| Exodus 4:10        | כְבַד־פֶּה וּכְבַד לָשׁוֹן (k’vad-peh u-k’vad lashon)                 | “Heavy of mouth and heavy of tongue.” This Hebrew idiom describes a speech impediment. Moses’s primary inadequacy became the very vessel through which God would speak authoritatively to the most powerful man on earth.                                               |
| Acts 4:13          | ἀγράμματοί εἰσιν καὶ ἰδιῶται (agrammatoi eisin kai idiōtai) | “They were uneducated and untrained men.” Agrammatoi means “unlettered,” and idiōtai refers to a common layman without professional or official standing. Their authority came not from a school, but from their association with Jesus.                                |
| 1 Corinthians 2:14 | πνευματικῶς ἀνακρίνεται (pneumatikōs anakrinetai)           | “Spiritually discerned.” The truths of God cannot be assessed or judged by natural reason (psychikos anthropos). They require a spiritual faculty (pneumatikōs) given by the Holy Spirit to be understood (anakrinetai).                                                |
