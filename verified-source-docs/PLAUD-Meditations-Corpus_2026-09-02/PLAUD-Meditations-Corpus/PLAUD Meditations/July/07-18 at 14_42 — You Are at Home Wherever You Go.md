# 07-18 Call Reflection: Indwelling Holy Spirit, Wilderness Faith, and Spiritual Homelessness

**Title:** 07-18 at 14:42 — You Are at Home Wherever You Go

**Recorded:** 2026-07-18 14:42:31

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture)

**Classification:** Call Reflection

## Opening Observation

As I listen back to this conversation with my brother Samuel, I am struck by how much theology was spoken aloud in the middle of what looked, on the surface, like a practical survival call — navigating a dodgy part of town, managing a dying phone battery, waiting on a man named Toby for a place to sleep. What arrested me most was this: in the middle of streets he described as dangerous, in the middle of uncertainty about housing and finances and the next twelve hours, my brother said, *“I carry the Holy Spirit.”* And I found myself saying back to him — almost before I realized how weighty it was — *“Not only that, He literally moved His home into you, so you are at home wherever you go.”* That line has not left me since.

**Why This Matters:**

In a season when so many believers are wrestling with displacement — literal or spiritual, geographic or emotional — the question of where home is has never been more pressing. There is a generation of saints who are physically wandering, economically stripped, circumstantially unsettled, and the temptation is to read that as God’s absence or disfavor. But what if the wilderness is not a sign that God has withdrawn? What if it is precisely the geography in which He speaks most clearly — and the posture in which the indwelling of the Holy Spirit becomes not a doctrinal footnote but a lived reality? What is at stake here is not just encouragement for a brother on a dark street with three percent battery. What is at stake is the question of whether the Church actually believes what she says she believes about the Temple of God.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

From a purely natural vantage point, this conversation reads as two young men — one of them navigating what sounds like genuine homelessness or near-homelessness, the other offering remote moral support — exchanging practical advice and occasional humor to manage a stressful situation. The natural mind would draw a clean line between the theological moments and the logistical ones, treating the spiritual language as coping mechanism: a way to manage anxiety, to find dignity in the midst of instability, to make meaning out of what is otherwise just bad luck or poor planning. The natural mind might also flag the tension around the cannabis, the mention of PTSD and anxiety, the talk of a bike and a tent and traveling Europe, and frame all of it under the heading of a young man who hasn’t quite found stable footing yet — spiritually or otherwise. It would offer sympathy. It would suggest a support worker or a housing program. It would try to fix the external variables and assume that once the external situation is resolved, the internal one will follow.

**The Limitation:**

But that interpretive framework cannot perceive what is actually happening in this conversation, because it cannot see that the most stable thing in the entire exchange is not the prospect of the HMO or the Airbnb or the viewing on Tuesday — it is the indwelling of God Himself. The natural mind, even a compassionate one, tends to evaluate a person’s security by what they have access to from the outside: money, shelter, relationships, credentials. It cannot perceive that a man walking through a dangerous neighborhood at night with three percent battery and nowhere to sleep is simultaneously inhabiting the most secure address in the universe — because God, by His Spirit, has taken up residence inside of him. You cannot explain that with cause-and-effect thinking. You cannot map it onto Maslow’s hierarchy. It requires the eyes of faith to see that the man who appears most externally vulnerable may be, by the standard of the Kingdom, the most internally anchored person on the street.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

As I sat with this conversation after it ended, what the Spirit began to press into me was the profound collision of two kingdoms playing out in real time — and how my brother Samuel, in the middle of a practical and somewhat chaotic call, was actually articulating something that the Apostle Paul took multiple letters to unfold. When Samuel said, *“I carry the Holy Spirit,”* and then *“I am in the Lord gang,”* I recognized, even under the colloquial language, a theological declaration that is both ancient and desperately needed in the present hour. Paul wrote to the Corinthians, *“Do you not know that you are the temple of God and that the Spirit of God dwells in you?”* (1 Corinthians 3:16, NKJV). That word “temple” carries enormous weight. The Greek is ναός (naos, NAH-oss) — not the outer courts, not the surrounding precincts, but the inner sanctuary, the holy of holies. Paul is saying that every blood-bought believer has become the very dwelling place of the presence of God in the same way that the Most Holy Place in the tabernacle and the temple of Solomon contained the Shekinah glory.

What strikes me about Samuel navigating those streets is that this is not merely poetic language. He was literally walking through a space that the world had marked as dangerous, and the most real thing about his situation was not the darkness of the neighborhood — it was the glory inside him. The Shekinah does not stop dwelling in the temple because the temple is in a rough part of town. The presence does not evacuate when the circumstances are uncertain. This is not motivational metaphor. This is the logic of the New Covenant — and it is staggering if we let it land.

I think of what Jesus said in John 14:23 (NKJV): *“If anyone loves Me, he will keep My word; and My Father will love him, and We will come to him and make Our home with him.”* That phrase, *“make Our home,”* is the Greek μονή (monē, moh-NAY) — the same root used just two verses earlier when Jesus says *“In My Father’s house are many mansions”* or dwelling places. Jesus is not promising two separate realities — one where we eventually get a room in heaven, and another where we just get a nice feeling now. He is pressing toward a single reality: mutual indwelling. He comes to make His home in us, and we are eventually welcomed home fully into Him. The home is already here. The home has already arrived. Samuel was not homeless on that street. He was the home.

I also hear in this conversation an echo of something the wilderness always does to God’s people. Samuel himself said it near the end: *“What I need to do is go to the wilderness. The wilderness is always the answer.”* And I responded: *“That’s where God speaks.”* This is not a throwaway line. The wilderness is one of the most theologically loaded spaces in all of Scripture. It is the place where Israel received the Torah — not in a palace, not in a city, but in the desert. It is the place where Elijah fled after his greatest victory and heard the still small voice (1 Kings 19:12). It is the place where John the Baptist prepared the way of the Lord (Matthew 3:1). Most significantly, it is the place where Jesus Himself was led by the Spirit immediately after His baptism (Matthew 4:1). The wilderness does not mean abandonment. The wilderness means consecration. It means God has drawn someone aside so that nothing competes with His voice.

What I notice about the pattern of the wilderness in Scripture is that it tends to precede the assignment, not follow from failure. Israel’s forty years in the wilderness were complicated by sin and unbelief, yes — but the destination was always Canaan. The wilderness is the training ground for the promised land. And when I hear Samuel talking about selling his belongings, riding a bike across Europe, preaching wherever he goes — I am not hearing delusion. I am hearing the faint but recognizable outline of an apostolic call beginning to form. The men God uses to carry the gospel across geography have always looked a little like this in their early seasons: untethered, mobile, more attached to His presence than to a fixed address.

The Apostle Paul himself described his condition in terms that sound, on the surface, a great deal like Samuel’s: *“I know how to be abased, and I know how to abound. Everywhere and in all things I have learned both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need. I can do all things through Christ who strengthens me.”* (Philippians 4:12–13, NKJV). That word *“learned”* is important. The Greek is μεμύημαι (memyēmai, meh-MOO-ay-my) — a term drawn from the language of initiation into mysteries, the kind of deep learning that only comes through being put through the actual experience. Paul did not receive this contentment as a download. He was trained into it by seasons of both abundance and lack. What I see in Samuel is a man being initiated — being put through the actual experience of what it means to trust God for the next twelve hours, the next five percent of battery, the next bed.

I also want to sit for a moment with the exchange about water, because I think it carries more theological freight than its lightness in the conversation suggested. Samuel’s mouth was dry. He needed water. And we found ourselves talking about whether it’s legal and appropriate for someone to simply ask for water from a business and receive it. I told him that in America there are laws protecting this because it is basic human dignity. And that is true — but it is also something older. Jesus said to the woman at the well, *“If you knew the gift of God, and who it is who says to you, ‘Give Me a drink,’ you would have asked Him, and He would have given you living water.”* (John 4:10, NKJV). The image of a thirsty man navigating unfamiliar streets at night, needing water, pointed me straight to that well in Samaria — and to the God who is not offended by thirst but is, in fact, the answer to it. Every ordinary human need is a doorway into an encounter with the One who said *“I am the bread of life”* and *“I am the living water.”* Samuel’s dry mouth is not too small for theology. Nothing is.

Something else I noticed in this conversation was the contrast between survival posture and rest posture. Samuel described himself as being in *“survival mode”* — walking fast, staying low, keeping his eyes open, moving with an agenda. And in that context I said to him: *“Everyone’s out there trying to survive. You can take your leisure and survive.”* This was not merely practical advice. There is something deeply spiritual about the distinction between striving and abiding. Jesus said, *“Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest.”* (Matthew 11:28, NKJV). The Greek for rest there is ἀνάπαυσις (anapausis, ah-nah-PAW-sis) — not passive inactivity but the rest of one who has set down a burden they were never meant to carry. Samuel’s natural gifting, by his own admission, is numbers and business — a businessman who navigates by statistics and strategy. There is nothing wrong with that. But the Kingdom does not run on survival calculations alone. It runs on the rest-posture of a son who trusts that his Father is already moving before the request is made. I said it to Samuel directly: *“The Lord already knows what you need. He’s already moving before you ask.”* That is simply Matthew 6:8 spoken into a phone call.

The tension around the cannabis is one I want to address with care, because I think it is spiritually instructive beyond the surface-level behavior. Samuel mentioned it was for his anxiety and PTSD. And when it became clear that carrying it into someone else’s home would create a problem — that it was generating the very anxiety he was using it to manage — I simply said: *“Sounds like it’s giving you anxiety. So just getting rid of it might help with that.”* What the Spirit is showing me in that exchange is not a morality lecture. It is a pattern. We often carry things into new spaces that we tell ourselves we need for the journey, but which are actually compromising the very hospitality we are being invited into. This is true of habits, of thought patterns, of relational dynamics, of theological frameworks we have never submitted for scrutiny. The counsel of the Kingdom is not always complex. Sometimes it is simply: *leave that at the door. You don’t need it where you’re going.*

What I come back to, at the end of sitting with this conversation, is the profound simplicity of what my brother carried in his chest through those streets. Not a plan. Not a lease agreement. Not a stable income. He carried the Holy Spirit. He carried the living Temple. And as strange and scattered as the night appeared from the outside — dying phone, dodgy streets, waiting on a stranger’s father’s car — the most real thing about that moment was the eternal, unshakeable, indwelling presence of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit making their home in a young man walking in faith. *“Walk by faith, not by sight”* (2 Corinthians 5:7, NKJV). That is what I saw. That is what I heard. That is what I am still processing.

## Implications and Challenges

The first challenge this conversation presents is to any theology that measures the blessing of God primarily through external stability. A version of Christianity has become widespread — particularly in Western contexts — that quietly teaches believers to read material comfort as confirmation of divine favor and material instability as evidence of spiritual lack or disobedience. But the witness of Scripture — from Abraham in the wilderness, to Joseph in the pit, to Paul in chains — consistently shows that God’s most concentrated work often happens in seasons that look like loss from the outside. We must be careful that we do not preach a gospel that would have told Samuel on that street: *“You must be out of God’s will.”* He was precisely in God’s will. He was walking.

The second challenge is to a thin pneumatology — a belief in the Holy Spirit that is doctrinally stated but experientially vague. When Samuel said, *“I carry the Holy Spirit,”* he was not speaking theologically in the sense of having studied the doctrine carefully. He was speaking experientially. He knew the Spirit was with him. The challenge to the Church is this: do we actually live as though the Spirit dwells in us, or do we treat the indwelling as a saved-seat arrangement — something true in principle but not practically life-altering? The theology of ναός (NAH-oss) must become a lived reality, not a catechism answer.

The third challenge is to our fear of the wilderness. Much of the counsel the Church offers to people in liminal, unsettled seasons is aimed at resolving the wilderness as quickly as possible — getting them into a house, a job, a community group, a structured program. These are not bad things. But sometimes the Spirit has drawn a person into the wilderness intentionally, and the most faithful response is not to expedite the exit but to deepen the encounter. The wilderness is where God speaks. We should not be in such a hurry to leave that we miss what He is saying.

The fourth challenge is to the habit of carrying things into new spaces that belong to the old. What we cannot yield, what we insist on managing ourselves, what we justify as medicating legitimate pain — these things do not just affect us. They affect the households we enter, the communities we join, the brothers and sisters who extend hospitality to us. The counsel is gentle but clear: some things need to be left at the door. Not because they are unforgivable, but because where you are going requires both hands free.

The fifth and final challenge is a grace-filled one: the call to take leisure. Many believers who genuinely love God are running in survival mode — managing, striving, problem-solving, calculating. And the Lord is not annoyed by their diligence. But He is calling them to something deeper: the rest of a child who trusts that their Father’s eyes are already on the situation. *“Your Father knows the things you have need of before you ask Him”* (Matthew 6:8, NKJV). This is not passivity. It is the peace of one who is both fully engaged and fully surrendered. Samuel made it to Tesco. The phone didn’t die. The walk took less than seven minutes. It was already done.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

Maturity in Christ is not the absence of difficult circumstances — it is the deepening confidence that the indwelling Spirit is the truest thing about us, more stable than our phone signal, more present than our financial plan, more at home in us than we sometimes feel in ourselves. Growing up into Christ means learning, as Paul said, through actual initiation — real hunger, real lack, real uncertainty — that God’s sufficiency is not theoretical. It is enough. Right now. In this moment. On this street.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

The response this meditation calls forth is a willingness to submit the survival instinct to the rest of sonship. It is asking honestly: what am I carrying into new spaces that needs to be left behind? It is choosing to walk through the dangerous and uncertain stretches of the journey — not in frantic survival mode — but with the settled, purposeful stride of someone who knows whose they are. And for those who feel the pull toward the road, toward mobility, toward proclamation without a fixed address: this may be the season. Do not wait until it is more comfortable. Walk by faith, not by sight.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

The saints are being trained to recognize the wilderness as a place of encounter rather than exile. The prophetic posture here is one of alert rest — eyes wide open, as Samuel said, but not anxious. Watching. Listening. Moving with an agenda that is not self-generated but Spirit-led. The saints who will carry the testimony furthest in this hour are those who have learned that wherever they set foot, they bring the Temple with them. They are the home. They are the lampstand in the dark street. And the darkness does not overcome it.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I come before You as one who has been made Your dwelling place — not by my own worthiness but by the blood of Your Son and the breath of Your Spirit — and I stand in agreement with what You are doing in my brother Samuel’s life and in every life that feels the weight of unsettled seasons right now. You are not absent from the dodgy streets. You are not silent in the wilderness. You are not distant when the phone is on three percent and the next bed is uncertain. You are the ναός, the Most Holy Place, made mobile — walking in the body of Your sons and daughters through every geography You have ordained for them. Lord, I ask that You deepen in us the revelation of Your indwelling — that it would cease to be doctrine and become breath, would cease to be theology and become the very atmosphere we move through. Strip from us what we carry unnecessarily. Call us into the rest that only sons and daughters know. And where the road is long and the night is dark and the streets are unfamiliar — let us walk like people who know exactly where their home is: in You, and You in us, forever. We are the answer to the prayers You have already prayed, servants of the living God, co-heirs of a Kingdom that cannot be shaken. Let it be so on the streets of the UK, the roads of Europe, and every wilderness You have appointed for the preaching of Your name. In the name of Jesus Christ — who was homeless that we might be homed in Him — amen.
