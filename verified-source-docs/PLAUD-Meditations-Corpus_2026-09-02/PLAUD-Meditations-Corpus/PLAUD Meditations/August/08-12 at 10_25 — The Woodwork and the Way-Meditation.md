# 08-13 Reflection: Walking the Way and Forgiving the Past

**Title:** 08-12 at 10:25 — The Woodwork and the Way

**Recorded:** 2026-08-13 17:16 (call reflection concerning a missed call logged 08-12 at 10:25)

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture)

**Classification:** Call Reflection

## Opening Observation

As I sat listening to my brother recount the drunken call from a woman out of his past, I felt something quieter than the drama he was describing rise up in me. Beneath the entangled ache of an old relationship, I heard the ancient question of how a person can hate someone, wound them, and yet keep circling back like a moth to a flame they cannot name. Something in me recognized that the real subject was never Chloe or the phone or the past — it was the difference between walking in the Way and stumbling over it.

**Why This Matters:**

This matters because every disciple eventually meets the ghosts of who they used to be — either in the people who return to accuse us, or in our own hearts when we are tempted to return to old patterns. How we handle those returning voices reveals whether Christ has truly become our compass or whether we are still being pulled by the gravity of the flesh. My brother’s restraint in that call, and the small Scripture that surfaced between us at the end, opened a door onto forgiveness, the futility of insanity apart from Christ, and the mercy of having a fixed and living Way to walk in.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

Read naturally, this is a familiar and even mildly comic story: an intoxicated ex-girlfriend calls a man she cannot let go of, rehearses old grievances, weeps, curses, and clings, while he manages the encounter with composure and then blocks her again. The worldly mind frames it in terms of attachment psychology, unresolved trauma, poor boundaries, and the messy aftermath of teenage love. It might praise my brother for “handling it well,” diagnose the woman as codependent, and reduce the whole thing to emotional immaturity on both sides that time and therapy will eventually smooth over. Even the closing banter about pizza, dumpster cats, and black pudding fits that reading — life moves on, appetites remain, and pain is metabolized through humor.

**The Limitation:**

But the natural mind cannot see the spiritual mechanism operating underneath the human wreckage. It can describe the pattern of returning and raging and clinging, yet it cannot explain why coming closer to Christ so often stirs the old life to rise up against us, nor why forgiveness is medicine for the one who gives it rather than merely a favor to the one who receives it. The flesh treats these encounters as problems to be managed; the Spirit treats them as thresholds where a soul is either becoming a stumbling stone or a stone laid firmly in the Way. What looks like a drunken phone call is, in the unseen realm, a person stumbling over the very righteousness she once brushed against.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

As I listened to my brother describe her voice cracking between “I loved you” and “fuck you,” I heard something older than her grief — I heard the sound of a soul that has no fixed point to orient itself around. She is not merely drunk on alcohol; she is drunk on the past, circling a memory as though it were an altar, unable to move because she has no way forward. And that is precisely where the Spirit began to press on me: the difference between her and my brother in that moment was not moral superiority but direction. One of them had a Way; the other had only a wound.

The word that rose in me as we spoke was the proverb, *“The way of the LORD is strength for the upright, but destruction will come to the workers of iniquity”* (Proverbs 10:29), and echoing near it, *“For the ways of the LORD are right; the righteous walk in them, but transgressors stumble in them”* (Hosea 14:9). I notice how the very same path is strength to one and stumbling to another. The righteousness that steadied my brother’s tongue was the same righteousness that the returning voice kept crashing against. She was not stumbling over him; she was stumbling over the Way that now lives in him.

Something arrests me here about the Hebrew behind that “way.” The word is דֶּרֶךְ (derek, DEH-rek) — a road, a journey, a manner of life. It is not an abstraction but a trodden path, a direction one’s whole life is walking. To be “in the way of the LORD” is not to hold an opinion but to be traveling a road. This is why my brother could say, gently and without cruelty, “we’ve said it all, there’s nothing left to say” — because he had turned onto a road that no longer runs back through that door. She kept knocking on a house he had already left.

And then the deeper thing surfaced when my brother wrestled aloud with the madness of it — how a person can slander you, break your heart repeatedly, and then call fifteen times. I found myself saying that Christ is the only real compass, and that He literally declares, *“I am the way, the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through Me”* (John 14:6). The Greek there is ἡ ὁδός (hē hodos, hey ho-DOSS) — the road, the way. Jesus does not say He points to the way or teaches the way; He *is* the road itself. Without Him, a person is not merely lost in the sense of being confused — they are a traveler with no road under their feet, which is why they keep spinning in circles back to the same wound.

As I sit with this, I keep returning to the mercy hidden in my brother’s instinct about forgiveness. I said to him that forgiveness is not for the other person; it is for the interior person — so that I can have shalom, שָׁלוֹם (shalom, shah-LOME) — wholeness, peace, the rest of God — and so that when I pray, the Father forgives me my transgressions. This is not sentiment; it is the plain teaching of the Lord: *“For if you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you”* (Matthew 6:14). Forgiveness is the doorway back into the rest of God. To withhold it is to remain in the very circling madness that the drunken caller displayed — bound to an offense, unable to walk the road.

The Spirit pressed something further as we spoke about people coming “out of the woodwork” whenever a person draws near to Christ. I have seen this in my own life, and it stopped feeling coincidental long ago. When a soul turns decisively toward the Lord, the old life often sends messengers. It is written that the enemy “departed from Him until an opportune time” (Luke 4:13) — even Christ’s temptations returned at strategic hours. The returning voices are not always demonic, but they are always a test of whether we will keep walking the derek (DEH-rek) or turn back to comfort the flesh’s nostalgia.

I notice, too, the honesty my brother offered without cruelty — “I’m always going to love you and care for you, but that doesn’t mean I’m going to stick around; they’re two different things.” There is a maturity here that the flesh mistakes for coldness. Love does not require the dismantling of every boundary. Even the Lord loves those who will not enter His rest, yet He does not force the door. My brother’s diagnosis — “you would have done anything for me, that was the problem” — carries an unintended parable: a heart poured out entirely onto something that cannot bear its weight will always be broken. Only Christ can bear the full weight of a soul that “would do anything.” Given to a fifteen-year-old boy, or to any created thing, such devotion becomes idolatry, and idols always break their worshippers.

And here is the Kingdom tension that gripped me most: when I reminded my brother that we were once exactly the same — dead in our sins, the very definition of insanity, doing the same thing over and over expecting a different result — I felt the pride drain out of the whole conversation. *“For we ourselves were also once foolish, disobedient, deceived, serving various lusts and pleasures”* (Titus 3:3). We are not the sober ones looking down on the drunk. We are the formerly drunk, given a road to walk, and the only difference is grace. The returning caller is a mirror, not merely a nuisance — she shows us the circling we were rescued from.

So the meditation lands here for me: to be Christian is, as my brother put it, to realize you have to do everything differently. Repentance, μετάνοια (metanoia, meh-TAH-noy-ah) — a change of mind, a turning of the whole direction — is the end of insanity because it puts a road beneath our feet where before there was only the endless loop of the flesh. The drunken call, the tears, the accusations, the fifteen-year-old memory dredged up as a fresh wound — all of it is the sound of a soul with no derek (DEH-rek), stumbling on the very goodness it once brushed past and refused.

## Implications and Challenges

I want to begin gently, because it would be easy to read this as a story about a foolish woman and a wise man, and that is not the note the Spirit is sounding.

First, the challenge of forgiveness misunderstood: much of modern Christian culture treats forgiveness as an emotional transaction we owe to the offender, and then feels defrauded when the offender is not “worthy” of it. But forgiveness is first the release of our own soul into shalom (shah-LOME) and the reopening of our access to the Father’s mercy. If we withhold it, we imprison ourselves, not them.

Second, the challenge of boundaries mistaken for unlove: our therapeutic age either dissolves all boundaries in the name of compassion or weaponizes them in the name of self-care. The Way of Christ holds both — genuine love that will not pretend, and firm direction that will not return to the old house. My brother’s “there’s nothing left to say” was not hardness; it was faithfulness to the road.

Third, the challenge of the returning voices: many disciples are ambushed when, having drawn near to Christ, the old life comes calling. We must stop being surprised by this and stop interpreting it as failure. It is often the confirmation that we are actually walking somewhere new, and the past is trying to pull us back to the loop.

Fourth, the challenge of self-righteousness: it is spiritually lethal to observe another’s circling insanity without remembering we were rescued from the identical madness. The saint who forgets Titus 3:3 becomes a Pharisee. Let every returning “stumbler” remind us of the pit from which we were drawn.

Fifth, a gentle word about idolized devotion: the world celebrates “I would have done anything for you” as the height of love, but a devotion that a mortal cannot bear will always end in breaking. This is an invitation to relocate our absolute devotion onto the only One who can carry it.

Finally, and with grace: the ordinary domestic banter that followed — the pizza, the cat, the black pudding heavy with blood — is not spiritually empty. Even our laughter and our shared table are part of walking the road together as brothers, and there is holiness in a friendship that can move from a hard confession to easy laughter without losing reverence for either.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

We grow when we stop measuring ourselves against the stumbling of others and start remembering we were once stumbling too. Maturity forgives for the sake of its own shalom (shah-LOME), speaks the truth without cruelty, and refuses to reenter the loops of the old life no matter how sweetly they call.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

Obedience here looks like keeping our feet on the derek (DEH-rek) — the road that is Christ Himself — even when nostalgia, guilt, or old affections summon us back. It means loving people genuinely while entrusting the door to the Lord, and it means doing everything differently, because we are no longer who we were.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

We are trained to discern the returning voices for what they are — tests of direction — and to watch our own hearts for the subtle drunkenness of the past. The prophetic saint holds mercy and firmness together, intercedes for the circling soul rather than despising it, and remains anchored to the Way while the world spins in its loops.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I thank You that in Your mercy You did not leave me circling the ruins of my old life, but You set my feet upon a Way, and the Way is Your Son. I confess that I was once the foolish one, the disobedient one, doing the same thing over and over and calling it living, and it was only Your grace that drew me out of the dumpster of my sins and set me at Your table. Teach me to forgive as I have been forgiven, that I may walk in Your shalom and stand unashamed before You in prayer. Guard my brother and me from the returning voices that would pull us back, and make us gentle stones in Your road rather than stumbling stones for the weak. For every soul still spinning in the loop of the past, I intercede — send them a compass, send them the Way, send them Christ. As a servant and co-heir under the New Covenant, I make agreement with Your will between the heavens and the earth: let the righteous walk in Your ways, and let those who stumble find mercy enough to rise and walk with us. In the name of Jesus, the Way, the Truth, and the Life, amen.
