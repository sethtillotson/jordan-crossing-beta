# 03-05 Summary: Obedience, Spiritual Victory, and the Limits of Transcendent Communication

# Personal Meditation: He Who Has Ears — On Stewardship, Obedience, and the Genocide of Unclean Spirits

## Opening Observation

I find myself sitting with something that has been pressing on me deeply — this tension between what is meant to be spoken openly and what is too vast, too eternal, too *other* for human language to contain. Something has been stirring in me about the nature of divine stewardship: not the stewardship of silence, but the stewardship of *truth* — and what it actually costs to carry it faithfully. I cannot shake the weight of what it means that the mysteries of heaven are given to some and withheld from others, not arbitrarily, but by the sovereign design of a God who knew the end before He spoke the beginning into existence.

**Why This Matters:** We are living in a moment when the Body of Christ is being called to distinguish between the comfort of familiar religion and the confronting fire of genuine Kingdom reality. The question of *who gets to hear* is not an elitist one — it is a mercy question, a readiness question, and ultimately a sovereignty question. If we are to walk as commissioned priests of the eternal order of Melchizedek, we must reckon honestly with what true obedience costs, what true worship means now that the sin debt has been utterly annihilated, and what it looks like to approach the spiritual war not as combatants clawing for victory, but as sons and daughters already standing *in* the victory that Christ secured before the first atom was set spinning. This is not abstract theology. This is the ground we stand on every single day.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

From a purely human perspective, everything described here would be filtered through the lens of psychology, social theory, and self-preservation. The natural mind would interpret “testing the spirits” as a form of critical thinking — a reasonable epistemic framework for evaluating claims. It would read Ananias’s reluctance to go to Saul as entirely rational risk assessment: a man with an arrest warrant wants you to heal him? Common sense says *run*. The natural mind would categorize the language of spiritual warfare as metaphor at best, delusion at worst — and it would almost certainly reframe the call to “suffer willingly” as a trauma response dressed in religious clothing. Secular psychology would suggest that the impulse to welcome persecution is a self-defeating cognitive distortion, perhaps rooted in shame or unworthiness. And the idea that God *planned* the fall of Lucifer, that the Lamb was *already* slain from before the foundation of the world — that God authored the very conditions that made sin possible — would be philosophically categorized as a theodicy problem, a contradiction in the character of a supposedly good God.

**The Limitation:** The natural mind does not receive the things of the Spirit of God because, as Paul declares in 1 Corinthians 2:14 (NKJV), “they are foolishness to him; nor can he know them, because they are spiritually discerned.” The mind of the flesh operates within the prison of linear time, cause-and-effect logic, and the self-protective ego. It cannot hold paradox. It cannot suspend two contradictory truths simultaneously and find them both fully, gloriously real. It cannot comprehend a God who wounds *in order to* heal, who conceals *in order to* reveal, who ordains suffering as the very corridor through which His greatest glory passes. And so — with compassion, not condemnation — we simply acknowledge: there are things the natural eye has not seen, the natural ear has not heard, and the natural heart has not imagined. This is not an insult to human intelligence. It is an invitation into something *more*.

## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation

I am sitting with Matthew 13:11 this morning, and something arrests my attention in the way Jesus answers His disciples’ question about why He speaks in parables. He says, *“Because it has been given to you to know the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it has not been given.”* The Greek word here for “mysteries” is μυστήριον — *mystērion* — and it does not mean something spooky or esoteric in the way our modern imagination reaches for. It means something deliberately *veiled* until the appointed moment of unveiling, a divine secret held in trust and released only to those whose hearts have been prepared to receive it without shattering. The Lord is not being cruel when He withholds. He is being precisely surgical. He conceals from the hardened not because He hates them, but because the mysteries, received prematurely, would only deepen the callus on the unready heart. So I find myself asking: *what does it mean to have been given ears to hear?* And immediately I sense the answer is not about intellectual capacity. It is about surrender. It is about the posture of the prepared soil in that very same chapter.

As I sit with this, I am drawn into the tension of stewardship — that I carry things, revelations, understandings, that I know cannot be *reduced* without being *ruined*. Language itself is the limitation. And I notice something almost alarming here: the Apostle Paul himself, when he was caught up to the third heaven in 2 Corinthians 12:4, heard “inexpressible words, which it is not lawful for a man to utter.” The Greek is ἄρρητα ῥήματα — *arrhēta rhēmata* — literally “unutterable words.” Not just secret, but *structurally incommunicable*. Human language, with all its grammatical scaffolding, its linear syntax, its speaker-to-receiver compression and decompression of meaning, simply cannot be the vessel for certain orders of divine reality. What I hold in the spirit when I am pressing into these things and what arrives in the mind of the one who hears me speak of them — these are as different as heads and tails of the same coin. We both agree it is a coin. We are simply looking at opposite faces. And the only way for us both to see the whole coin at once is for it to be *spinning* — held in the beautiful, suspended tension of paradox, which only the Spirit can engineer.

This is where the dual nature of Scripture begins to open up to me in a breathtaking way. Scripture is not a flat document. It is a living, breathing, multi-dimensional architecture of revelation — Hebrews 4:12 calls it alive and active, a two-edged sword, and the Greek word used there is ζάω — *zaō* — which means not merely existing but *vitally, actively, presently alive*. It cuts on both the forward and the backward stroke. And I am beginning to see that this is not only true of Scripture’s effect on human hearts but of its very *structure*. Numbers carry geometric weight. Colors carry judicial and emotional resonance. Animals depict spiritual realities. Geographic elements — the mountain, the sea, the wilderness, the garden — map the interior landscape of human experience with God. The sea, for instance, carries throughout the canon this double meaning: chaos, the abyss, the place of unclean spirits, and simultaneously the place of God’s sovereign dominion over the deep. When Micah 7:19 declares that God will cast all our sins “into the depths of the sea,” and when I hold that image against Revelation’s declaration that in the new creation “there was no more sea” — I feel something crack open inside me. The abyss that held the record of our transgression is *gone*. Abolished. The geography of condemnation has been removed from existence.

And this brings me crashing into the stunning reality of *tetelestai* — τετέλεσται — that single word Jesus exhales from the cross in John 19:30. It is a perfect passive indicative — meaning the action is *completed, with permanent results, by an external agent*. It was not “I am finishing.” It was not “I have mostly finished.” It was the cry of a divine accounting ledger being stamped: *PAID IN FULL — NEVER TO BE REOPENED*. In the commerce of the ancient world, when a debt was entirely discharged, this exact term was written across the record. Jesus, as both the High Priest and the Sacrifice, as both the Creditor and the Payment, spoke this word as the ultimate legal declaration over all of human history — backwards and forwards across every timeline simultaneously, because He exists outside of time in the eternal I AM. The Lamb was slain from the foundation of the world (Revelation 13:8) — which means the redemption was *decided* before the rebellion was *enacted*, which means the fall of Lucifer was not a divine emergency. It was a divine *appointment*.

I have to sit with that for a long moment, because it challenges everything the anxious religious mind wants to believe about God scrambling to fix a catastrophe. Before Genesis 1:1, before “בְּרֵאשִׁית” — *bereshit* — before “in the beginning,” God was already *there* in the eternal now, and the plan of redemption was already sealed. Job 38 carries this haunting echo — God speaking from the whirlwind, reminding Job that He was *there* when the foundations of the earth were laid, when the morning stars sang together. And in the ancient Hebrew understanding of that text, the implication is staggering: God is not reacting to history. God is *authoring* it, every thread of it, including the ones that appear from the inside to be catastrophic loose ends. The adversary asked to reign upon the earth rather than be cast into annihilation — and God permitted it. Not because God was outmaneuvered, but because the permission itself was woven into the pattern already. Murphy’s Law within eternity: given infinite time and genuine free will, every possibility *will* occur. And God, who inhabits eternity, had already prepared for it *before time had a before*.

This illuminates for me, in a wild and confronting way, the story of Ananias in Acts 9. The Lord does not give Ananias a theological seminar before sending him to lay hands on Saul. He does not argue back when Ananias pushes back. He simply says, “Go, for he is a chosen vessel of Mine” (Acts 9:15, NKJV). The Hebrew root beneath our understanding of obedience — שָׁמַע, *shama* — does not primarily mean to follow rules. It means *to hear, to listen with the intent of response*. Obedience, understood through *shama*, is fundamentally a relational act. It is not compliance with a code; it is *responsiveness to a Person*. Ananias heard the voice of the Lord, argued, received a single clarifying word, and then *went*. No further explanation was given because no further explanation was needed. The will of God was sufficient. And I am feeling the weight of what this means for those of us who are waiting for God to make every step make sense before we move. He is simply saying, *“Go.”* Not because He is indifferent to our fear, but because He has already gone before us — the Hebrew word in Deuteronomy 31:8, הָלַךְ — *halak* — describes God as one who *continuously walks ahead* of His people, making the crooked places straight before the foot even arrives.

I notice the text says “do not fear” or its equivalent appears approximately 365 times in Scripture — one for every day of the solar year. This is not coincidence. This is the Living Tapestry at work — God, the divine architect of language and number, encoding into the canon a daily antidote to the paralysis that will inevitably accompany genuine Spirit-led obedience. Because let’s be honest with ourselves: the moments the Holy Spirit truly compels us to move, they are almost always the moments that feel the most dangerous, the most counterintuitive, the most antithetical to everything the worldly belief systems inscribed in us during our formation. The very nature of faith, as the writer of Hebrews declares, is “the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen” (Hebrews 11:1, NKJV). The Greek word for “substance” here is ὑπόστασις — *hypostasis* — which means *that which stands under, the very foundation and support*. Faith is not wishful thinking. Faith is the structural load-bearing reality of an invisible Kingdom pressing into visible existence.

And then the Spirit pulls me into the priestly dimension of all of this, and I feel it shifting everything again. We are called priests after the eternal order of Melchizedek — not the Levitical order, which was temporary, tied to genealogy, and oriented around the repeated sacrifice for ongoing sin. No — the order of Melchizedek, as Hebrews 7 unpacks with breathtaking deliberateness, is eternal, without recorded beginning or end, without genealogical qualification, and oriented not around repeated sacrifice but around *the once-and-for-all, eternally sufficient offering of Christ*. Psalm 110:4 declared it prophetically — “You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek” — and the writer of Hebrews spends three entire chapters demonstrating that this is fulfilled in Christ, and that *we*, united with Him, are caught up into this same eternal priesthood (1 Peter 2:9). So if the sin debt is utterly gone — hurled into the abyss, remembered no more — then what exactly does our priestly worship consist of? What are we offering? I am seeing it now: we offer the sacrifice of our own comfort, our own understanding, our own ego-identity, our own sleep, our own safety. We offer what costs us something. We offer the watchman’s posture in the eleventh hour when every biological instinct says rest. We offer intercession for the one who betrayed us, prayer for the success of the very competitor who undermines us. *This* is the living sacrifice of Romans 12:1 — παραστῆσαι τὰ σώματα ὑμῶν θυσίαν ζῶσαν — “to present your bodies a living sacrifice.” The altar has shifted. The offering is no longer an animal. The offering is *us*.

And here is where the spiritual war reframes itself entirely. If I approach the battle as though I am trying to *win* it, I have already misunderstood my position. The enemy of our souls is not a peer-level opponent locked in a cosmic arm-wrestle with a God who might lose on a bad day. The unclean spirits operating in the earthly realm are *already judged*. They operate not from strength but from *terror* — trembling at the name of the Lord Jesus Christ (James 2:19), working their deceits and ensnaring their territories in the full knowledge that they are condemned, buying time, exploiting the loopholes of human free will and the authority and dominion originally given to mankind in Genesis 1:28. But the moment a son or daughter of God stands in the full revelation of τετέλεσται — knowing that Christ’s victory is not *upcoming* but *accomplished*, not *potential* but *actual* — the dynamic shifts from warfare to something closer to what I can only describe as the enforcement of an already-settled verdict. “No weapon formed against you shall prosper” (Isaiah 54:17, NKJV) — not because weapons won’t be formed. They will. They already are. But formed weapons in the hands of a condemned enemy, facing a child walking in the authority of the risen King Jesus, are formations of futility. They tremble as they forge them. They know the verdict. We simply need to know it too.

I am resting now in the simplest and most arresting truth of all of this: the Sabbath is not merely a day. It is a *posture*. It is the seventh-day rest of God after completion — שַׁבָּת, *shabbat* — the ceasing that comes not from exhaustion but from *finishedness*. God rested on the seventh day not because He was tired, but because the work was *perfect and complete*. And Hebrews 4 invites us — not commands us to strive, but *invites* us — to enter into that same rest. To abide in the completion. To stop fighting for a victory that has already been won and start *walking in it*. This is what it means to carry the mysteries faithfully. Not to hoard them. Not to water them down for comfort’s sake. But to speak them with the Pauline grace — commendation, loving correction, and a return to Christ — passing the Johannine tests with every breath: Is He confessed as the incarnate, risen, ascended Lord? Does it align with Scripture? Does it produce love? If yes, then go. If it cuts, let it cut. The Holy Spirit is a surgeon, not a bandage. And you cannot heal a tumor without first opening the patient.

## Implications and Challenges

Grace and peace to you, beloved, from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. What follows is offered not as condemnation but as the same loving correction Priscilla and Aquila extended to the gifted Apollos — that he might walk more perfectly in the way of God (Acts 18:26). Receive it as such.

The first implication that confronts much of modern Western Christianity is the *domestication of obedience*. The prevailing cultural Christianity of our era has largely redefined obedience as moral conformity — keeping a behavioral checklist, attending services, abstaining from certain vices. But the biblical revelation of שָׁמַע (*shama*) and the story of Ananias make clear that obedience is *relational responsiveness to a living Person*, not rule-following. The Holy Spirit is not a policy document. When He speaks — especially when what He says violates every rational and cultural instinct — He does not owe us an explanation. The invitation here is to examine honestly: have we made obedience comfortable by redefining it as something we can manage on our own terms?

The second implication challenges the widely embraced *prosperity and comfort gospel*, which has taught vast numbers of believers that faithfulness to God produces health, wealth, safety, and the approval of one’s social environment. The testimony of Scripture from Abel to the Apostle John on Patmos is precisely the opposite. The baptism of fire is not a metaphor for a difficult season on the way to earthly success. It is the normative corridor of transformation for those who are genuinely becoming conformed to the image of the Son. If our theology has no category for welcomed suffering, for night-watch intercession at personal cost, for praying for our persecutors from a place of genuine love rather than obligation — then we may be following a Christ of our own construction rather than the Christ of the cross.

The third implication is perhaps the most theologically arresting: the *reduction of priestly worship to emotional experience*. Modern worship culture has largely equated worship with the quality of a musical moment or the intensity of a feeling in a corporate gathering. But if we are indeed priests after the eternal order of Melchizedek, and if the sin debt has been categorically and permanently annihilated, then our priestly offering must be reconceived entirely. The sacrifice we bring is not a song — it is ourselves (Romans 12:1). The altar is not a stage — it is every moment where comfort and obedience conflict, and we choose obedience. This does not diminish the beauty of sung worship; it infinitely *deepens* it by grounding it in the reality of a life laid down.

The fourth implication confronts the modern Church’s *anxiety about spiritual warfare* — the tendency to engage the enemy as though the outcome is uncertain, as though we are fighting for a victory not yet secured. This posture, however sincere, actually misreads the post-resurrection reality. The enemy is not a rival who might win. He is a condemned prisoner operating in the final hours of a sentence already declared. When we approach spiritual realities from a posture of striving and fear, we are functionally living *before* the cross, as if τετέλεσται had not yet been spoken. The invitation is to repent of strategic anxiety and step into the enforcement of an already-settled verdict — not from pride, but from the humility of trusting that Christ’s word from the cross was sufficient.

May the God of all grace, who called us to His eternal glory by Christ Jesus, establish, strengthen, settle, and perfect you in these things (1 Peter 5:10). All grace and peace to you.

## What This Means For Us

**For Your Spiritual Maturity:** The revelation that the Lamb was slain from the foundation of the world is not a theological footnote — it is the very bedrock of our spiritual stability. Receiving it fully means we stop interpreting our suffering as divine abandonment and start reading it as divine appointment. It means we can hold the paradox of a God who is both the author of free will *and* the sovereign architect of redemption without the framework collapsing into either fatalism or chaos. Maturing in this understanding transforms the way we read every difficult season: not “why is this happening to me?” but “what is being formed in me, and what is being cleared away?”

**For Kingdom Obedience:** The posture this calls us toward is the posture of Ananias after the Lord’s second word: *movement without full understanding*. We are being called to cultivate a listening interior life — a *shama* posture — in which we are so attuned to the voice of the Holy Spirit as a living Person that when He speaks, we have the relational trust to move. This means investing in the practices that cultivate that attunement: extended solitude, Scripture saturation not for information but for encounter, and the willingness to be corrected, redirected, and surgically altered without resistance. Kingdom obedience is not heroic striving. It is the quiet, daily, costly act of choosing the Spirit’s direction over our own comfort.

**For Prophetic Posture:** We are not called to *win* the spiritual war. We are called to *walk in the finished victory of the One who already won it*. This means our prophetic posture is not one of aggression or spiritual performance — it is one of abiding (John 15:4-5), of resting in the Sabbath-completion of God, and of being the vessels through which the already-settled verdict of heaven gets enforced on earth. We are sent ones — commissioned and clothed, as the returning prodigal was dressed in the best robe, with signet ring and sandals — not to earn our place but to *occupy* it. The open door of the Kingdom is not somewhere we are striving to reach. It is the ground beneath our feet, here and now. Our posture is not striving. Our posture is yielding, abiding, and going when He says go.

## A Closing Prayer

Lord Jesus — King eternal, reigning from the throne that was never threatened and will never be vacated — I pour out before You everything that has been stirring in me, and I lay it at Your feet as an act of priestly offering. Thank You that You did not wait for me to understand before You redeemed me. Thank You that the Lamb was slain *before* I was born, *before* I could ask, *before* I could earn, *before* I could even conceive of needing it. Your grace is not a reaction. It is an *origin*. Father, I receive the commission to steward these truths faithfully — not for my own recognition, not to be right, but because every revelation of Your glory belongs to Your children and I am simply the vessel through which it flows back to them. Holy Spirit, You are the Surgeon and I am willing to be on the table again — cut what needs to be cut, remove what is still hiding beneath the surface, and heal me in the places I don’t yet know are wounded. For every person who hears these words and feels the sting of conviction, I intercede: go to them, Lord. Meet them in the most personal, most unmistakable, most undeniable way that only You can. Let them not be offended at me — let them be *arrested by You*. I surrender this understanding back into Your hands. All glory belongs to You — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — forever and ever. May every ear that is ready to hear, hear. And may every heart that is ready to receive, receive. It is finished. Amen.
