# 07-06 Call Reflection: The Book of Matthew and Old Testament Fulfillment

**Title:** 07-06 at 11:03 — The Book of Matthew and the Thread That Runs Through Everything

## Opening Observation

I am sitting with a phone call — the kind that starts loud with good news and ends almost accidentally with something holy. My brother was celebrating closed deals, pipeline revenue, and a CEO who fought for him. I was resting, watching an animated walkthrough of the New Testament on YouTube. And then, almost as a footnote before the call ended, I said something about the book of Matthew and its one hundred and sixty to one hundred and seventy direct references to the Old Testament — and something about that observation has not let me go.

The conversation moved fast, jumped between business wins and heartbreak films and musicals and an unknown UK number cutting the call short. But the Spirit keeps pulling me back to that moment: the moment I said, almost casually, *“That’s about as far as I got before I called you.”* I had stumbled onto something, and I want to go further now than I got then.

**Why This Matters:**

There is a kind of revelation that arrives not in a cathedral moment but in the middle of ordinary days — rest days, conversations with friends, YouTube videos watched while a wife naps in the other room. The danger is not that God fails to speak in these moments. The danger is that we note the insight and move on before the Spirit finishes unfolding it. The book of Matthew is not merely the most Old-Testament-quoted gospel for stylistic reasons or literary convention. It is structured as a deliberate theological argument — a prosecutorial case, laid before a Jewish audience, that the man Jesus of Nazareth is the one the entire Hebrew canon had been building toward. To take that seriously is to take seriously the nature of Scripture itself: that the Old Testament is not backdrop but foundation, not prequel but prophecy, not context but covenant promise awaiting its walking, breathing fulfillment.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

The natural mind, encountering the observation that Matthew quotes the Old Testament more than any other gospel, will reach first for the literary explanation. It will say: of course Matthew quotes the Hebrew Scriptures heavily — he was writing for a Jewish audience, so it is simply good rhetorical strategy, audience awareness, cultural accommodation. The secular scholar frames it as source criticism, redaction theory, the Q hypothesis. The cultural observer frames it as identity politics of the first century — a Jewish author writing to Jewish readers who needed Jewish proof-texts to accept a Jewish rabbi. From this angle, Matthew’s construction is smart persuasion, good communication design, ancient rhetoric. Nothing more.

**The Limitation:**

But this frame, however intelligent, is ultimately deaf to what is actually happening in the text. It treats prophecy as rhetoric and fulfillment as literary device. It looks at the scaffolding and concludes that scaffolding is the building. What the natural mind cannot perceive from the outside is that Matthew is not merely *quoting* the Old Testament — he is declaring that the Old Testament has a *heartbeat*, and that heartbeat has a name. The entire Hebrew canon was written as a body waiting for its animating breath, and Matthew’s gospel is the moment that breath arrives and the body stands upright. A mind that has not been opened by the Spirit will look at the one hundred and sixty-plus citations and see scholarship. The Spirit-opened mind looks at the same citations and sees a God who never forgets a promise — who spoke through Moses, and David, and Isaiah, and Micah, and then came Himself.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

I did not set out to study the book of Matthew today. I was resting. My wife was napping. I put on something visual and instructive, the kind of low-intensity content appropriate for a day off, and then without planning it I found myself arrested by a simple statistic: Matthew references the Old Testament more than all the other gospels. Between one hundred and sixty and one hundred and seventy direct references. And as I was hearing this described in the video, I felt something register in my chest that I did not immediately know how to name. I think now I would name it this: *recognition*. Not surprise. Recognition.

Matthew writes under what might be called the צֶלֶם אֱלֹהִים (tselem Elohim, TSEH-lem eh-lo-HEEM) problem — the image-of-God problem that runs from Genesis forward. Humanity, made in the image of God, shattered by the fall, groping through covenant and law and sacrifice and exile, was always the story of a people trying to find their way back into a likeness they had lost. Every king who was supposed to be a son of God fell short. Every priest who was supposed to mediate between the holy and the common eventually died or corrupted. Every prophet who was supposed to speak the Word of the LORD spoke it partially and then went silent. Matthew opens his gospel not with a miracle or a theological treatise but with a genealogy — and that genealogy is a theological statement dressed as a family tree. He is saying: the image is being restored. The line has arrived at its appointed end.

The phrase Matthew uses repeatedly — “that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the Lord through the prophet” — is worth sitting with at the level of the Greek. The word translated “fulfilled” is πληρόω (plēroō, play-ROH-oh), and it does not merely mean “completed” in the way a checklist is completed. It carries the image of something being *filled to capacity* — a container brought to its full measure. Matthew is not saying the prophets made predictions and Jesus happened to match them, the way a horoscope might vaguely describe a person’s week. He is saying that every prophetic word was a vessel, and the arrival of Jesus is what finally filled it until it overflowed. The prophecies were not just pointing forward; they were incomplete until He came. They had content but lacked their fullness.

This is what I had begun to sense in the video before I called my brother. Matthew was not the most quotation-heavy gospel by accident or audience strategy alone. Matthew was written with the specific theological burden of demonstrating, line by line, prophecy by prophecy, that Israel’s entire history was one long πληρόω (play-ROH-oh) — one long filling — and Jesus is the substance that was always being poured. The book of Isaiah alone contributes some of the most staggering fulfillments: the virgin birth, the ministry in Galilee, the entry on a donkey, the silence before accusers, the scattering of the flock. Micah gives Bethlehem. Hosea gives the return from Egypt. The Psalms give the cry of dereliction from the cross. Matthew gathers all of these not as a debate tactic but as a doxology in prose.

What moves me as I sit with this is the sheer density of covenant faithfulness it reveals in God. These prophecies were not written in one sitting or by one man. They span centuries. They span exile and return, famine and conquest, temple construction and temple destruction. The men who wrote them did not live to see their words filled. Isaiah was likely martyred. Jeremiah wept his entire ministry. David, who wrote prophetically of the crucifixion in Psalm 22 — “My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?” — wrote those words a thousand years before crucifixion was even a known form of execution. And yet they arrived, every one of them, in the thirty-three years of one man’s life. That is not coincidence. That is the architecture of an eternal God who writes in centuries the way a composer writes in measures.

I think about why I have always loved Matthew specifically — something I mentioned on the call almost in passing — and I recognize now that I love it because it refuses to let the New Testament exist without the Old. Matthew does not allow Jesus to be a new idea. He insists that Jesus is the ancient answer. The Sermon on the Mount is not Jesus revising the Torah; it is Jesus revealing what the Torah had always meant at its deepest level. “You have heard that it was said… but I say to you” is not contradiction. It is the Author clarifying the intent behind what had been misread by those who handled the text without the Spirit. He does not abolish; He fills — πληρόω (play-ROH-oh) again.

There is a theological weight to this that I do not want to rush past. We live in an era where the Old Testament is frequently treated in Christian circles as optional reading — spiritual history, cultural background, interesting but not essential. I have heard it said, even in sincere discipleship contexts, that we live in the New Covenant so we primarily live in the New Testament, as though the New Testament were a standalone document that arrived without roots. But Matthew will not allow that. The very first gospel written for a Jewish audience — possibly the first gospel written at all — is saturated from its opening genealogy to its closing commission with the insistence that you cannot understand who Jesus is without understanding what the whole canon has been saying. The gospel is not a replacement for the Hebrew Scriptures. It is their culmination, their πληρόω (play-ROH-oh).

And then there is the Christological declaration that was being made. The video framed it simply: Matthew was probably written to prove that Jesus was the Messiah. I received that framing. But I want to press deeper than proof. Proof is a legal concept. What Matthew is doing is closer to what we might call in Hebrew עֵדוּת (edut, eh-DOOT) — testimony. Testimony given before witnesses, at cost, with everything staked on its veracity. Matthew was not writing a pamphlet for debate. He was writing testimony for people whose entire national identity, whose covenant relationship with YHWH, whose understanding of election and promise and land and temple — all of it rested on whether this man was truly the one Moses said would come, the one the prophets described, the one the Psalms cried out for. The stakes were not intellectual. They were eternal and social and familial and national.

And yet God in His grace did not merely send the Messiah and leave the people to recognize Him unaided. He sent the testimony. He raised up a Matthew — a tax collector, of all people, someone the religious establishment would have dismissed — to write the most Scripture-saturated gospel in the canon. The most unlikely scribe for the most necessary document. That pattern itself is worth noting: God consistently uses the ones the gatekeepers have written off. Matthew was probably written off by the Pharisees long before he followed Jesus. But he followed Jesus with a pen.

As I sit now in the aftermath of that call — my brother celebrating his business momentum, the cat trying to eat the cords, the Sierra being sipped, the UK number cutting everything short — I am not dismissing any of that. The momentum my brother is walking in is real, and I celebrated it genuinely. But the thread I keep returning to is the one I almost let pass: that the book of Matthew has one hundred and sixty to one hundred and seventy Old Testament references, and that each one of them is a witness standing up in a courtroom that runs from Genesis to Revelation, pointing at Jesus and saying, *“Him. It was always Him.”* I did not finish watching the video. But the Spirit is finishing what the video started.

There is one more thing pressing me here, and I want to honor it. The reason Matthew’s density of Old Testament reference mattered in the first century was that the audience had to know the Old Testament for the citations to land. You cannot hear a fulfillment if you have never heard the prophecy. This raises the question for us now: are we people who know the Old Testament well enough to feel the weight of Matthew’s argument? Are we reading the whole book? Are we sitting with the Torah, the Writings, the Prophets — not just the Psalms we like and the Isaiah passages that get quoted at Christmas — but the whole canon, so that when Matthew says “that it might be fulfilled,” we actually know what was being fulfilled? The Spirit is pressing me on that this afternoon more than any single theological insight. Read the whole thing. Sit with the whole thing. Because the whole thing is about Jesus, and Matthew went to the trouble of showing us where to look.

## Implications and Challenges

There is a widespread habit in contemporary Christian culture of treating the Old Testament as spiritual background noise — something to consult for encouraging Psalms or striking narrative, but not as the living foundation of everything the New Testament declares. Matthew’s gospel is a direct challenge to that habit. One hundred and sixty-plus prophetic references are not decorative. They are load-bearing walls. To remove the Old Testament from serious discipleship is to pull out the load-bearing walls of the house Jesus built.

There is also the challenge of how we understand fulfillment itself. The word πληρόω (play-ROH-oh) means to fill to capacity, not merely to complete a checklist. This matters because much of modern evangelical engagement with prophecy has drifted toward a kind of spiritual scorekeeping — marking off predictions as they are matched. But Matthew’s framework is deeper: the prophetic word was always incomplete until Jesus. The law itself was always pointing past itself. The sacrificial system was always confessing its own insufficiency — *“for it is not possible that the blood of bulls and goats could take away sins”* (Hebrews 10:4). To read the Old Testament as merely predictive is to reduce its voice. It is simultaneously prophetic, confessional, and formative. It is also deeply personal in a way that scorekeeping never allows.

There is a correction needed — gently but clearly — for the tendency to make Jesus exotic rather than covenantal. Much of modern preaching presents Jesus as a dramatic interruption in history, a surprise arrival, a revolution that no one saw coming. Matthew’s entire architecture refutes this framing. Jesus was the most announced arrival in the history of the world. Centuries of prophets, poets, priests, and kings pointed in one direction. The surprise was not His coming. The surprise, in the hardness of human hearts, was His form. They expected a conqueror; they received a servant-king. But the testimony was there all along for those with ears to hear it.

There is a gentler implication for those of us in seasons of rest — day-off seasons, waiting seasons, seasons where the demands have lightened for a moment. Those are not seasons to disengage from the Word. They are the seasons in which, unrushed, without agenda, something like an animated walkthrough of the New Testament can open a door that a busy season would have kept closed. God speaks into margin. He fills the moments we stop filling ourselves.

Finally, there is something humbling about the kind of revelation that arrives not through formal study or structured devotion but through rest, conversation, and an unexpected media encounter. The Spirit is not bound to our methods. He will use a YouTube video on a day off. He will press a word through a phone call about business deals. He will interrupt the ordinary to say something extraordinary — and then it is on us whether we let the call end and move on, or whether we stay with what He was saying until it lands fully in us.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

Spiritual maturity does not mean we have moved past the Old Testament. It means we have moved deeper into it — seeing, with increasing clarity, how every covenant and commandment and sacrifice and prophecy was always building toward the face of Jesus. Maturity means reading Matthew’s citations not as footnotes but as the sound of God keeping every promise He ever made, simultaneously, in thirty-three years on the earth.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

The obedience this calls forth is simple and demanding: read the whole canon. Sit with the Prophets. Learn the Torah. Let the Psalms form your prayer vocabulary. Come to the New Testament not as a stranger to what came before but as someone who has lived long enough in the Old Testament to feel the arrival of Jesus the way a people feel rain after years of drought. That kind of reading forms a different kind of disciple — one who is not easily moved by novelty, because they know the ancient promises too well to trade them for something newer and shinier.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

Matthew’s gospel teaches us something about prophetic posture: the faithful prophetic voice is always, at its root, a voice that points to Jesus and says, *“It was always Him.”* It does not invent. It does not sensationalize. It gathers what God has already said and holds it up in the light of the moment, so that the people of God can recognize where they are in the story. To dwell in Matthew is to be trained in that posture — to see the present moment through the lens of the fulfilled Word, and to testify with confidence that the God who kept every Old Testament promise is still keeping His promises now.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I come before You as a son in Your house, a co-heir with Christ, standing in the New Covenant You have sealed with the blood of the Lamb — and I am grateful, genuinely grateful, for a day off and a YouTube video and a phone call and a passing comment about the book of Matthew that You would not let stay passing. I receive the instruction You pressed through the ordinary: You have always been saying something. The whole canon has always been saying something. And what it has always been saying is Jesus — the Word made flesh, the prophecy made person, the πληρόω (play-ROH-oh) of every vessel the prophets carried for centuries. I ask for grace to read the whole thing — not just the New, but the Old, not just the familiar, but the chapters that require patience and returning — because I want to know Jesus the way Matthew knew Him: as the one the entire testimony of heaven had been building toward. Let the saints who read this be formed in the same hunger. Let us not settle for a partial canon and a partial Christ. Let the whole Book be alive to us, and let the whole Book point us, every time, to the same name. To the glory of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen.
