# 04-06 Theological Meditation: Body as Revelation, Divine Utterance, and the End of Exile

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation with extended post-reflection (Seth and Samuel, live theological exchange on April 6, 2026, with Seth’s progressive revelatory meditation woven throughout and culminating in a unified theological vision)

**Personal Meditation: The Word That Became the World — Body, Utterance, and the End of Exile**

## Opening Observation

Something broke open across a single morning’s conversation on April 6, 2026 — not once, but in three successive waves, each one pressing deeper than the last, until what began as a discussion about the body and revelation had become something I can only describe as the collapse of the foundational assumption beneath every spiritual framework I have ever operated within. What I found, as I listened to my brother Samuel and thought aloud alongside him, is that three theological realities — the body as revelatory organ, the self as divine utterance still being spoken, and the exile from God as the oldest fiction ever told — are not three separate meditations. They are one meditation, arriving in sequence, like three movements of a single symphony the Spirit had been composing long before I knew I was listening.

**Why This Matters:**

The stakes here are not merely theological precision. They are pastoral and prophetic, because the invisible premise beneath most of the modern church’s spiritual formation — that there is a stable, pre-existing self standing at a managed distance from God, using the body as a vehicle, striving toward a union that has not yet been fully granted — this premise, however sincerely held, may be quietly generating the very exhaustion it was meant to cure. If the body is the message rather than the medium, if the believer is a sentence God is still speaking rather than a contractor building toward His approval, and if the exile from His presence is not a condition to be overcome but a fiction to be demolished by the cross — then everything changes: how we pray, how we interpret suffering, how we receive the wilderness, how we understand the prophets, how we treat one another in the Body, and what we mean when we dare to present ourselves before Him. This is not a small adjustment. This is a recalibration of the entire gravitational field of Christian existence.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

The natural mind, encountering a conversation like the one I had with Samuel this morning, will reach for its familiar instruments of interpretation. It will hear a man who has walked through a failed AI startup, returned to welding in an isolated factory town, lost a marriage, and is now constructing an elaborate theological framework — and it will call this meaning-making, the psyche’s graceful response to fracture, the human need for coherence when life refuses to cooperate. It will hear language about the dissolution of the self and label it mysticism or, more anxiously, pantheism. It will hear that the body is not a conduit but the message itself and reach for biology — the nervous system’s role in emotional experience, somatic response to atmospheric stimulus, the well-documented capacity of religious environments to produce genuine feelings through purely physical means. And when the conversation turns toward the claim that the exile from God is itself a fiction abolished by the cross, the natural mind will immediately marshal its doctrinal guardrails: sin is real, judgment is real, accountability is real, and this kind of language has a history of eroding all three. The natural mind is not wrong to be cautious. It has seen what happens when transcendence is preached without the cross. It has watched communities dissolve into formless spirituality and call it freedom.

**The Limitation:**

But the natural mind is constitutionally incapable of perceiving the thing it is most urgently trying to classify, because it is reasoning from within the very premise that this meditation calls into question. It assumes the soul is a bounded object that must navigate its way toward a separate bounded God — and from that starting point, all of its theology, psychology, and philosophy flow like tributaries from a source it never examines. What it cannot see from within its own architecture is that the map it is working from was drawn by the same instrument that needs reorientation. You cannot reason your way to the recognition that you were never actually separate from God, because the reasoning apparatus itself is the thing that keeps insisting on the separation. What the Spirit was doing in that conversation — pressing past each framework Samuel and I reached for, collapsing each binary we erected, pressing further still into territory neither of us had intended to enter — is precisely what the unassisted intellect cannot perform for itself. Some doors do not open from the inside. This is one of them.

## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation

I want to begin where the Spirit began: with the body. I had been describing the semantic architecture of my theological work to Samuel — the weighted nodes, the conceptual clusters, the sixty-seven meditations mapped in three dimensions — and somewhere in the middle of explaining it, a sentence came out of me that I was not fully prepared for: *What if your body is not the delivery mechanism? What if your body is the message?* I want to trace what that means, because I do not think I have reached the bottom of it yet.

The framework I had been operating with, without fully naming it, was gravitational and directional: heaven is the source, the believer is the receptor, and the flow moves downward — through prayer, through the Word, through κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-noh-NEE-ah) — shared participation, genuine communion. In this framework, the body functions as a sanctified container: holy, consecrated, the temple of the Spirit, but still essentially packaging. The content matters; the package is instrumental. And it is precisely this instrumental understanding of the body that the prophets will not allow me to maintain. When I pressed not on the prophets’ words but on their bodies — what was asked of their flesh, what was done to their flesh — everything shifted.

Ezekiel did not merely receive a message. He ate the scroll — “So I ate it, and it was as sweet as honey in my mouth” (Ezekiel 3:3). The Hebrew verb אָכַל (akal, ah-KAHL) — to eat, consume, devour — is not metaphorical here. He ingested the revelation, and what he swallowed became what he spoke. His jaw was then locked by the Spirit in a kind of prophetic speechlessness, and he was commanded to lie on his left side for three hundred and ninety days, one day for each year of Israel’s iniquity — not as a symbolic gesture performed at arm’s length, but as a physical bearing of the nation’s sin in his own flesh. The revelation did not pass *through* him on its way to a more important destination. It was *enacted in* him. His body was the text.

Daniel, during the twenty-one-day fast of Daniel chapter ten, lost his physical strength as Gabriel approached. He writes: “No strength remained in me; for my vigor was turned to frailty in me, and I retained no strength” (Daniel 10:8). The Hebrew word כֹּחַ (koach, KOH-akh) — strength, power, capacity — simply drained from his frame. This was not psychological overwhelm. This was the body registering proximity to a higher order of reality, the natural self yielding to what it had no capacity to contain. And John on Patmos: “And when I saw Him, I fell at His feet as dead” (Revelation 1:17). The Greek ὡς νεκρός (hos nekros, hoss neh-KROSS) — as dead, like a corpse — is not the language of emotional response. It is somatic collapse in the presence of the risen Christ. The body of the prophet was not incidental to the reception of revelation. The body was where the revelation landed, registered, and was subsequently carried.

This distinction — between the body as instrument and the body as organ — is the one I have been sitting with most carefully. An instrument is external to the one who wields it: a tool, a channel, something employed and then set aside. But an organ is not external. The eye does not merely transmit light; it is the organ of seeing. The ear does not merely relay vibration; it is the organ of hearing. And what I am beginning to believe — and I hold this with open hands before the Lord — is that the body of the believer, indwelt by the Spirit of God, is not the instrument through which revelation is received and retransmitted. It is the organ of perception through which God, having taken up residence in flesh, sees and addresses the earth. Paul writes in First Corinthians 6:19: “Or do you not know that your body is the temple of the Holy Spirit who is in you, whom you have from God?” The word for temple is ναός (naos, NAH-oss) — not the outer courts, not the broader temple complex, but the inner sanctuary, the Holy of Holies, the precise location where the presence of God rested between the cherubim. Paul is not saying the body is like a temple. He is saying it is the ναός. This is a theological relocation of the Shekinah glory — from the space between golden wings to the space within a body made of dust and breath, walking around in the world.

What follows from that relocation is decisive: the Incarnation is not a temporary divine concession, God stooping to our level and wearing our form for a season before discarding it at the ascension. The Incarnation is the permanent declaration of what God intends to do with flesh. “The Word became flesh and dwelt among us” (John 1:14). The Greek σὰρξ ἐγένετο (sarx egeneto, SARX eh-GEH-neh-toh) — flesh, it became — is not the language of temporary habitation. The Logos (λόγος, LOH-gos) — the rational, creative, self-expressive principle of God through whom all things were made — did not pass through Mary’s womb on its way to a ministry of words. It became flesh. And the resurrection does not discard that flesh — it glorifies it. Thomas, grief-struck and skeptical, is invited to touch the wounds still present in the risen body: “Reach your finger here, and look at My hands; and reach your hand here, and put it into My side” (John 20:27). The scars remain in Sunday’s glory. The marks of Friday are preserved in the resurrection body — not as remnants of suffering that God chose not to clean up, but as the permanent testimony that the body was never merely packaging. The wounds were the message. The body of Jesus, scarred and risen, standing in a room, is the revelation.

This is the point at which the conversation with Samuel moved, unexpectedly, from the body as revelatory organ to a second and deeper claim: that the believer is not merely a vessel who receives and transmits the word of God, but a *sentence God is still speaking*. Every binary we reached for — striving versus abiding, slave versus son, sand versus rock — while genuinely scriptural and spiritually necessary, still carried within it a hidden assumption. Every one of those contrasts presupposes a self that must choose correctly, orient correctly, position itself rightly before God. And I do not want to dismiss those contrasts; Jesus Himself offered them, and they are real guardrails. But they point beyond themselves toward something the New Testament writers were straining the limits of language to articulate: that the most fundamental truth about the believer is not that they are a correctly positioned agent, but that they are a specific location where divine life becomes perceptible. The question this reorientation generates is not *am I building on the rock?* but *what in me still believes existence must be managed rather than received?*

The architecture of management goes all the way down. It is not only the obvious forms of self-reliance — proud self-sufficiency, open rebellion, the entrepreneurial spirit turned inward. It is woven into the very way we frame spiritual growth, our reading of Scripture, our sense of obedience, our most sincere attempts at surrender. Many of us — and I include myself without hesitation — have been running a theologically articulate self-improvement project and calling it discipleship. The invitation I heard the Spirit making through our conversation is not to abandon structure or formation, but to examine whether our structures are serving divine utterance or protecting self-authorship. There is a kind of high-end theological furniture that a person can acquire — correct positions on striving versus abiding, on grace versus works, on the nature of surrender — that fills the interior of a room whose walls are still constructed of self. The *I* doing the abiding is still running the project. And the deeper question is: what is this language protecting? What in me is using the right words to avoid the transparency that would cost me my editorial authority over my own existence?

Here is where the Spirit pressed hardest, and I want to say it carefully because I am still sitting with the edges of it: what you call *my life* is, at depth, a sentence still being uttered by God. The self is not surrendered as a project and then left in repose. It is *relevated* as utterance — its origin is in the mouth of the Eternal, and its continuation is in the ongoing breath of the One who said *Let there be*, and there was. John 1:1–3 grounds this: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God… All things were made through Him.” The λόγος (LOH-gos) through whom all things were made is not merely the originating cause of creation. He is its ongoing coherence. Creation is not merely made by God — it is *spoken* by Him, which means all existence is inherently communicative in nature. And the believer, indwelt by that same λόγος (LOH-gos) made flesh, is not simply a recipient of revelation but a participant in the ongoing divine utterance — a specific articulation of what God is saying into this moment, this generation, this situation.

I watched something shift in Samuel when I said it plainly: *Consider this — incarnation doesn’t use flesh to transport divinity. It is the revelation.* And what rose in me as that sentence landed was the recognition that this frame completely renarrates the wilderness. I think of my own wilderness — the AI startup that collapsed, the return to welding in what felt like the far edge of nowhere, the demotion in the eyes of the world, the distance from everything I had built. The natural mind calls that failure, recalibration, a learning experience at best. But I am seeing it now through Genesis 50:20, where Joseph, after everything — the pit, the slavery, the false accusation, the prison — looks at his brothers and says: “You intended to harm me, but God intended it for good.” The Hebrew word חָשַׁב (chashav, khah-SHAV) — to weave, to plan, to reckon — governs both clauses. The brothers wove a plan for destruction. God wove the same threads into a garment of mission. He did not override the broken threads. He wove them into something that bore His name. The wilderness is a חָשַׁב (khah-SHAV) moment — not a gap between the real chapters, not a setback on the way to the assignment, but the place where God’s weaving is most visible precisely because there is nothing else left to look at.

This brings me to the third layer of what the Spirit was unfolding, and it is the most radical of the three: the possibility that the exile from God — the experiential distance that all of us have known, that persistent ontological loneliness, the unexamined conviction that we are a separate center of being standing *in front of* God rather than *within* Him — is not a condition to be overcome but a fiction to be abolished. Paul writes in Romans 8:38–39 that nothing in all creation can separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord. The Greek verb χωρίζω (chōrizō, khoh-REE-zo) — to separate, to divide — is placed in the realm of structural impossibility. This is not merely a promise about God’s persistence in pursuit. It is an ontological declaration about the very architecture of reality under the New Covenant. The Hebrew concept יְהוָה שָׁמָּה (YHWH shammah, yeh-HOH-vah SHAM-mah) — the LORD is there — distributed throughout the prophets, is not describing God’s visits to consecrated spaces. It is describing what space is. The Incarnation is not the story of God entering a world from which He was previously absent. It is the public visibility of a union that was always true — God making flesh the announcement that flesh was never outside of Him.

I said to Samuel during that conversation: the sign of Jonah has always been preached as typology — three days in the belly of the fish prefiguring three days in the tomb. And it is that. But there is something more specific happening when you account for the detail about Oannes, the Ninevite pagan deity depicted as a man emerging from the mouth of a fish. God did not merely rescue Jonah from his disobedience. He arranged the entire spectacle of Jonah’s failure so that the washed-up prophet crawling from the mouth of a great fish on Nineveh’s shoreline would be the one announcement their mythology had already prepared them to receive. God borrowed their false image to shatter it and replace it with Himself. He wasted nothing — not the rebellion, not the wrong direction, not the fear. The nine sailors converted during the storm before Jonah ever reached Nineveh are evidence of the same extravagance: even the direction of Jonah’s disobedience produced harvest. I see myself in Jonah with a clarity that is almost embarrassing. The prophecy of fifty thousand — I received it as a performance target, a business metric. I misread the word completely and ran with my misreading. And God gave it anyway, knowing exactly how I would distort it, because He is so far ahead of my rebellion that my rebellion is already accounted for in His unfolding. That is not the theology of a God barely holding the plan together. That is the theology of a God so lavishly sovereign that He can weave the malicious threads of my own misreading into a garment that still carries His name.

What unites these three movements — body as message, self as divine utterance, exile as fiction — is this: they all arrive at the same place from different directions. They are all saying that the premise of distance, the assumption that there is a *me* who must work its way toward a God who is somewhere more holy and more present than here, is the original lie, the first distortion. In Genesis 3:1, the serpent’s first move was not denial but distortion: אַף כִּי (aph ki, ahf-KEE) — “Has God indeed said?” — a question that introduced interpretive distance between Eve and the word she had received. The lie of separation did not begin with the act of eating. It began with the question that made God’s word feel uncertain, mediated, possibly misheard. And the entire history of redemption is the story of God systematically dismantling that question — through the tabernacle, through the prophets, through the Incarnation, through the torn veil, through Pentecost, through the New Covenant promise of Hebrews 10:17: “Their sins and their lawless deeds I will remember no more.” The Greek μιμνῄσκομαι (mimnēskomai, mim-NACE-ko-my) — to remember, to call to mind — describes an active recollection God covenantally refuses. He does not merely forgive the record. He removes the very category that made distance feel necessary.

The wilderness, in this unified frame, is the place where God removes your last ability to remain internally fictional. Every comfort that allowed you to maintain the story of a self that could survive without Him is stripped not cruelly but surgically — until you are left with nothing except the reality that was always there beneath the noise: you are not alone because you were never singular. Jonah in the belly of שְׁאוֹל (Sheol, sheh-OHL) — the underworld, the realm of the dead — was not merely being disciplined for disobedience. He was being loved out of the last illusion that he could define himself in opposition to God and still have a self worth defining. And when the prayer rises from the fish’s belly in Jonah chapter two, it is not a bargaining prayer. It is a disintegration prayer. It is a man who has run out of self. And from that emptiness — not from his repaired character, not from his improved theology — God speaks, the fish obeys, and the shoreline of Nineveh receives the most improbable prophet in the history of Israel. What the belly of the fish was to Jonah, the courtyard was to Peter: the womb of restoration. The place where every identity that could survive apart from His presence was stripped away — and where, on the far side of the stripping, the question by the sea came three times like a liturgical reversal of three denials: *“Do you love Me?”*

The Incarnation, the cross, and the resurrection are not three separate events in this frame. They are one continuous declaration: God making flesh the announcement that flesh was never outside of Him, the cross executing the lie of separateness in public, and the resurrection unveiling a life no longer organized around the boundary of self. And Pentecost is not merely empowerment — it is the abolition of spiritual privatization, the רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — the Spirit, the breath, the wind — distributed without borders, without institutional gatekeeping, without the demand that you first construct a sufficiently ordered self before He will consent to dwell. The kavod (כָּבוֹד, kah-VODE) — the weight, the heaviness, the substance of glory — that once filled the tabernacle now distributes itself to every ναός (NAH-oss) walking the earth. And the deepest question, the one I cannot stop sitting with, is not *am I building rightly* or even *am I receiving rightly*, but this: *what false authorship in me still interrupts the sentence God is making with my existence?* What voice — whether the enemy’s accusation, a generational narrative passed through family speech, my own ego dressed in theological clothing — is running a counter-narration over what God is saying about me right now?

## Implications and Challenges

There is a grace-filled but serious challenge embedded in this unified meditation for the contemporary Western church — and I want to approach it with the care that the revelation itself demands, because the critique arises not from superiority but from the same place of repentance I am standing in.

The first challenge is to the spirituality of spiritual self-management. Much of what passes for discipleship is an extraordinarily sincere project of self-renovation — becoming more disciplined in prayer, more generous, more sexually pure, more doctrinally precise. None of these are wrong pursuits in themselves. But when they are treated as the *core* of spiritual formation, they quietly reaffirm the very premise the gospel subverts: that there is a stable, pre-existing self to improve. Galatians 2:20 does not say the old self was upgraded. It says it was crucified. “It is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me.” The life that remains is not mine managing itself more skillfully. It is His life expressing itself through flesh that once thought it belonged to someone else. The body Paul presents as a living sacrifice in Romans 12:1 — παραστῆσαι (parastesai, pah-rah-STAY-sai) — to present, place beside, offer — is not discarded but taken up as the site of God’s ongoing reasonable worship. The body does not become more spiritual by being minimized. It becomes more revelatory by being yielded.

The second challenge is to what I would call manufactured atmosphere — the high-production-value worship environment designed to produce a specific emotional state and then identify that state as the presence of God. The kavod (kah-VODE) is not an emotional frequency. It is a gravitational reality — a weight that lands, not a feeling that rises. When the congregation has never been discipled into the distinction between somatic response to beautiful music and the somatic registration of divine weight, it cannot discern between an atmosphere it created and a presence that arrived. The body, trained in genuine encounter rather than manufactured stimulus, learns to distinguish these. Discernment requires a ναός (NAH-oss) that has been trained in the secret place, not merely in the stage-lit sanctuary.

The third challenge is to confessional distance — the habit of approaching God primarily as one who must be appeased before communion is possible. I have watched brothers and sisters who cannot enter prayer without first cataloguing everything that might make them unworthy to be heard. There is something right about coming with honesty and transparency. But the New Covenant does not place confession as a toll booth before the presence becomes accessible. The veil was torn — past tense, from top to bottom, by hands that were not human. Hebrews 10 is not a motivational statement. It is the announcement that the architecture of distance has been demolished. The chronic self-condemnation of God’s people — the rehearsal of old failures, the insistence that current shame accurately represents present standing — is itself a form of false authorship, a narrating of something God has already thrown into the depths of the sea, as Micah 7:19 declares with the force of a verb that means to hurl violently: וְתַשְׁלִיךְ (vetashlikh, veh-tash-LEEKH). He does not reluctantly discard sin. He hurls it.

The fourth challenge is to what I called in the conversation a Judas condition — and I want to be precise here, because the danger is not uniquely external. Judas is not primarily the type of the enemy-as-infiltrator. He is the type of what happens when consciousness treats itself as private property — when a soul in proximity to Christ still experiences itself as a separate self with a separate plan. The kiss in the garden is the sorrow of intimacy without union — closeness to Love itself while remaining sealed against it. That condition is not only a historical betrayal. It is a pattern within any soul that has stopped trusting and turned toward the management of its own image. And the cure is not psychological technique. It is encounter — the kind that leaves you, like Daniel, without the כֹּחַ (KOH-akh) to maintain the posture of a self that was never as independent as it believed.

The fifth challenge is to theological tribalism, and I say this with particular sorrow because I have participated in it. By the time Samuel and I reached the end of our conversation that morning, I said it plainly: everyone is too busy arguing over doctrine, theology, and who is right to spend enough time with God to get to the place where they discover this is all it was. Jerusalem’s tragedy in Luke 19 was not primarily doctrinal error. They had the texts. They had the temple. They had the priesthood and the lineage and the tradition. What they did not have was recognition — ἐπισκοπή (episkopē, eh-pis-koh-PAY), the ability to perceive the day of visitation when the Author was standing in front of them. Doctrine that does not regularly return to the presence of the One the doctrine describes can become its own form of spiritual privatization — the rightness of the position replacing the Person the position is about.

The sixth challenge — and this one is both the most tender and the most important — is to the way we preach and pastor the wilderness. If the wilderness is consistently framed as something to get through — the gap between the last chapter and the next assignment, the consequence of some failure you are now paying back — then we produce a discipleship culture always in transit, always waiting for the real thing to begin. But the מִדְבָּר (midbar, mid-BAHR) — the wilderness, etymologically connected to דָּבָר (davar, dah-VAHR), the word, the thing, the act — is itself a place of speaking. God’s word becomes uncluttered there by everything you used to use to define yourself. The wilderness is not punishment. It is the womb. And the saints need pastors courageous enough to tell them so.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

Maturity, after this meditation, can no longer be measured by the sophistication of our spiritual self-management, the correctness of our doctrinal positioning, or the impressiveness of our ministry output. It must be measured by the degree of increasing transparency to the divine utterance — by the progressive decrease of what Colossians 3:3 calls the hiddenness with Christ in God being obscured by the noise of false selfhood. This means growth looks like increasing surrender to what was always already true, not increasing capability in the management of what was never actually ours to manage. Failure is not evidence that the self needs more work. It is evidence that an illusion is still in operation. Silence is not the absence of God. It is the frequency at which the always-present voice becomes audible. And the ordinary moment — Tuesday afternoon, the commute, the meal prepared in a small kitchen — is not a secondary arena in which God occasionally visits. It is the native medium of incarnation. It is where He has always preferred to speak.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

The call is not to strive harder or to surrender with more theological precision. It is to inhabit each day as the specific location where divine speech takes on the texture of your particular flesh, your particular hands, your particular moment. The body offered as a living sacrifice is not discarded but taken up as the site of God’s ongoing utterance. This means that private, secret prayer is not preparation for ministry — it is where ministry originates. You cannot have public fruit without private encounter. The prophets did not receive their assignments and then go pray about them. They were people of such sustained nearness that the word required no distance to travel. The reduction of prayer to a devotional warm-up has produced ministers performing ministry rather than issuing from it. What the body carries in secret is what the Body speaks in public. These are not two separate economies. They are one.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

The saints who will carry prophetic witness in this generation are not those who have perfected their hearing technique. They are those who have become so undone from the premise of self-sufficiency — so cleared of the internal counter-narration — that what comes through them is recognizably not their own production. The prophetic posture this unified meditation calls for is embodied receptivity: not passivity, but active, yielded attentiveness. Like Daniel whose כֹּחַ (KOH-akh) drained before Gabriel spoke. Like John who fell ὡς νεκρός (hoss neh-KROSS) before the revelation unfolded. Like Ezekiel whose body bore what his words would later describe. The prophetic community must resist the pressure to produce. The watchers and recorders are always present — let what they record be the residue of genuine κοινωνία (koy-noh-NEE-ah), not the performance of spiritual industry. The deepest prophetic word is not the most dramatic. It is the one that carries the weight of the One who spoke it — the kavod (kah-VODE) that arrives as reality rather than feeling, as substance rather than atmosphere, landing on a body that has learned to hold it.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I come before You on the morning of April 6, 2026, as one of Your servants — a co-heir of the Kingdom under the blood of the New Covenant, one of the prophets to whom You have revealed Your secrets, standing in agreement with what You have already spoken before the foundations of the earth were laid. I thank You for the brother You set beside me this season, for the conversation that began in one place and was pulled by Your Spirit into somewhere deeper than either of us intended to go. I thank You that the Word became flesh — that You did not merely speak *about* us but *into* us, not merely *to* us but *as* one of us — and that the body You wore still bears the marks of what You endured, scars preserved in the resurrection as permanent testimony that the flesh was never incidental to the message. I repent of every form of spiritual privatization — every prayer that was more performance than presence, every season I spent managing myself before You rather than being undone by You, every time I misread the wilderness as abandonment and every time I misread Your word as a performance target. Lord, speak over those patterns the word that ends them. We consent to be voiced. We consent to be the medium through which Your articulation of love and truth and holiness becomes perceptible in the earth. Repaint the interior picture in every beloved brother and sister who encounters this word — where the wilderness has been misread as abandonment, let them receive it now as appointment; where the body has been treated as mere packaging, let them receive the revelation that their flesh, their scars, their weariness, their very breath is the site where Your glory is pleased to dwell; and where the fiction of separation has been maintained — however sincerely, however devoutly, however theologically — let the cross do its public work, let the veil remain torn, and let the ruach (ROO-akh) move without border or gate or institutional credential through every ναός (NAH-oss) walking the earth in this hour. We are Your servants. We are the prophets. We are the ones to whom You have revealed Your secrets, and we agree with what You have already written. Speak us into the earth. In the name of Jesus — the Word made flesh, risen and reigning, the Lamb who was slain and who stands, the One in whom the sentence God is speaking over all creation finds its eternal coherence — amen.
