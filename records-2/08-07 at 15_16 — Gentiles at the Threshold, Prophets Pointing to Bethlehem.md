# 08-07 Call Reflection: Gentiles and the Abrahamic Promise in Scripture

**Title:** 08-07 at 15:16 — Gentiles at the Threshold, Prophets Pointing to Bethlehem

**Recorded:** Not provided

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture)

**Classification:** Call Reflection

## Opening Observation

As I listened to my brother Samuel over the phone tonight — half of it laughter, trains, and the sting of Fireball whiskey — I noticed how the Spirit slipped a real revelation into the middle of our jokes. We began planning to read the birth of Jesus, and somewhere between the Christmas warmth and the whiskey we stumbled into something weightier: the question of who the Gentiles really are, and whether God has always been calling them home. Something arrests me here, that a phone call full of banter can turn, almost by accident, into holy ground.

**Why This Matters:**

This matters because the way we understand the Gentiles is the way we understand the reach of God’s mercy — and therefore the way we understand our own inclusion in the covenant. If God only ever called the already-holy, then none of us would be here. But if the God of Abraham has always reached across the line into the “outsider,” then the birth of Jesus is not the beginning of grace toward the nations but the fulfillment of a mercy older than the Law itself. For disciples learning to love the outsider, evangelize the far-off, and receive our own salvation as gift rather than pedigree, this question shapes everything about how we carry the gospel.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

To the natural mind, this was simply two friends killing time before Bible study — one waiting on a train in Casselton, the other halfway through a bottle of vodka, trading jokes about British nose gestures and old exorcists. The theological content could be read as trivia: how many Gospels record the Nativity, which prophet named Bethlehem, whether Adam’s children counted as Gentiles. A cultural observer might note the casual mixing of alcohol, crude humor, and Scripture as a kind of contradiction, or might see the whole thing as two men intellectualizing faith to feel connected. The “Gentile question” itself could be filed away as a matter of ancient sociology — who was in, who was out, and why it no longer applies.

**The Limitation:**

But the natural mind cannot feel the pull beneath the surface. It hears the jokes and misses the hunger. It sees two men who are, frankly, imperfect — one drinking to sleep, both laughing at demons — and it cannot recognize that the Spirit delights to move through jars of clay who keep returning to the Word. The flesh reads the Gentile question as settled trivia; it cannot see that my brother’s simple wondering — “how many Gentiles loved God during His time?” — brushed against one of the deepest arteries in all of Scripture. The Spirit does not wait for us to be polished before He teaches. He met us right there, on a train platform and a living room floor, and began to open the mystery of the nations.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

As I sit with this exchange, the first thing the Spirit presses on me is how the whole conversation bent, without either of us planning it, toward one question: has God always been reaching for the outsider? My brother wanted the warm Christmas story of Luke, and that longing was pure — but the Spirit used it to walk us backward, from the manger to the prophets, and then further still, past Sinai, all the way to a man in Ur who did not yet know the name of God.

I hear myself asking Samuel whether Abraham was a Gentile, and I notice now that this was not merely a clever debate move. It was the Spirit exposing something. When God called Abram in Genesis 12, there was no Torah, no circumcision, no chosen nation. There was only a pagan man from a family of idolaters — Joshua 24:2 says plainly that Terah “served other gods.” The father of the faithful began as an outsider. The covenant did not find Abram righteous; it found him lost and made him a promise.

Something arrests me in the word גּוֹי (goy, GOH-ee) — nation, or Gentile. In the very promise given to Abram, God says, “I will make you a great goy,” and then, “in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed” (Genesis 12:2-3). The word used for the outsider is the same word God speaks over Abraham’s destiny. From the beginning, the line between insider and outsider was never meant to be a wall; it was meant to be a doorway through which the nations would eventually walk.

I am seeing that this is why Samuel’s instinct to trace Messianic prophecy is so fitting. When the prophet Micah names Bethlehem — “But you, Bethlehem Ephrathah, though you are little among the thousands of Judah, yet out of you shall come forth to Me the One to be Ruler in Israel” (Micah 5:2) — he is not merely giving an address. (Samuel and I fumbled on whether it was Malachi; the Spirit corrects gently, it was Micah.) The specificity of that prophecy testifies that the coming of Christ was no improvisation. The One born in that small town was destined, in Isaiah’s words, to be “a light to the Gentiles,” that God’s salvation would reach “to the ends of the earth” (Isaiah 49:6).

I notice that my brother could only think of one Gentile who honored Jesus — someone offering vinegar at the cross. But as the Spirit opens my memory, the Gospels are full of them. The Roman centurion of whom Jesus said, “I have not found such great faith, not even in Israel” (Matthew 8:10). The Syro-Phoenician woman who would not be turned away, whose faith Jesus praised (Mark 7:26-29). The centurion at the foot of the cross who confessed, “Truly this was the Son of God!” (Matthew 27:54). Far from being only persecutors, Gentiles keep appearing in the Gospels as the ones who see.

There is a tension here I cannot ignore. Samuel is right that it was Gentile hands — Roman hands — that drove the nails, because the occupying power alone held the authority to crucify. And yet it was also a Gentile centurion who first confessed the crucified One as the Son of God. The same nations that shed the blood are the nations the blood was shed for. This is the scandal and the glory of the cross: mercy poured out precisely upon those who spilled it.

The Greek word ἔθνη (ethnē, ETH-nay) — the nations, the Gentiles — carries this whole promise into the New Covenant. When the risen Christ commissions the eleven to “make disciples of all the nations” (Matthew 28:19), He uses this word. The Great Commission is not a new idea bolted onto an old religion; it is the flowering of the seed planted in Abram. Paul says it directly: “the Scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, preached the gospel to Abraham beforehand, saying, ‘In you all the nations shall be blessed’” (Galatians 3:8). The gospel was preached to a Gentile named Abram two thousand years before Bethlehem.

As I hold all of this, I feel the weight of what Samuel and I were circling. If God has always called Gentiles, then salvation has never been about bloodline or birthright. It has always been about the God who crosses lines to reach the far-off. Paul writes to the Ephesians that they were once “aliens from the commonwealth of Israel and strangers from the covenants of promise” — but now, “in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ” (Ephesians 2:12-13). Every Gentile believer is living proof that the covenant was always meant to overflow its banks.

And I confess the Spirit does not let me stand outside this. I am a Gentile. Samuel is a Gentile. When we laugh about delivering each other from spirits, when we plan our next study, when we say “God bless you” seven times over a phone line, we are the very fulfillment of the Abrahamic promise — the far-off brought near. The warm Christmas story my brother loves is warm precisely because it is our story too. The manger in Bethlehem holds a light lit for the nations, and we are among them.

I am also struck by how the Spirit hid this treasure in an ordinary evening. We were not in a sanctuary. We were tired, one of us drinking, both of us joking about jesters and exorcists. And yet the hunger to open the Word was real, and God honored it. This tells me something about how the Kingdom actually advances — not only in polished pulpits but in phone calls between two flawed brothers who keep dragging each other back to Scripture. “Where two or three are gathered together in My name, I am there in the midst of them” (Matthew 18:20). He was in the midst of Casselton and a living room, in the midst of us.

The final thing pressing on me is the way we ran out of time. Samuel needed sleep; we agreed to save the Gentile study for tomorrow. And I hear in that the patience of God with progressive revelation. He does not dump the whole ocean on us at once. He gives us Bethlehem tonight and the nations tomorrow. The unfinished study is itself a picture of the walk of faith — always more to open, always another reason to gather again over the Word.

## Implications and Challenges

Let me hold this gently, because it comes out of genuine friendship and genuine faith. Samuel’s love for the Nativity is a gift, and the Spirit clearly moves in our conversations.

**First**, this meditation challenges any quiet assumption that we are insiders by nature. Modern Western Christianity often carries an unspoken pride, as if faith were our heritage rather than a mercy extended to outsiders. Remembering that Abraham began as a Gentile keeps us humble and grateful.

**Second**, it confronts the tendency to reduce Bible study to warm feelings. Samuel honestly said the Christmas story “makes me happy,” and that is real — but the Spirit invited us deeper, from sentiment to substance, from the manger to the sweep of covenant. Warmth is a doorway, not a destination.

**Third**, it presses on how we handle Scripture casually. We misremembered which prophet named Bethlehem, and we spoke of “Luke going over Egypt” when that is actually Matthew’s account. This is not condemnation — it is an invitation to handle the Word with growing care, to be “diligent to present yourself approved to God, a worker who does not need to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth” (2 Timothy 2:15).

**Fourth**, it challenges the modern separation of the sacred and the ordinary. We treated demons lightly and mixed drink with Scripture, and while the Spirit still met us, the meditation invites sobriety of a deeper kind — a reverence that does not require perfection but does grow toward holiness.

**Fifth**, it calls us outward. If God has always sought the Gentile, then our faith cannot be a private comfort. The far-off are still far off, and we who were brought near are now sent to carry the light to the nations.

And I end where I began — in grace. That two imperfect brothers keep returning to the Word is itself evidence that the Abrahamic promise is alive in us.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

We grow when we stop reading Scripture for warm feeling alone and let it carry us into the whole counsel of God. Maturity is remembering that we are the outsiders God chose, and letting that gratitude burn out every trace of self-righteousness.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

If the covenant was always meant to overflow to the nations, then obedience means carrying the light outward. The Great Commission is not optional patriotism for the Kingdom; it is the fulfillment of a promise made to a Gentile named Abram, and we are the ones now entrusted with it.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

This trains us to see God’s fingerprints in ordinary hours — a train platform, a phone call, an unfinished study. It teaches us to wait, to save the deep things for tomorrow’s gathering, and to trust that the Spirit unfolds revelation in His own patient time. We watch, we return to the Word, and we keep the appointment.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I thank You that You have always been a God who crosses the line to reach the outsider — that You found Abram in a land of idols and made him the father of a promise, and that in that promise You already saw me, a Gentile, brought near by the blood of Your Son. I thank You for my brother Samuel, for the laughter and the hunger both, and for how You hid revelation in an ordinary evening. As one You have called from far off and set among Your servants, I stand between the heavens and the earth and make agreement with Your will: let the light lit in Bethlehem reach the ends of the earth, let the nations come home, and let us who were once strangers now be faithful heralds of the mercy that first found us. Teach us to handle Your Word with care, to reverence the holy, and to keep gathering over the Scriptures until Christ is all in all. In the name of Jesus, the Ruler born in little Bethlehem and the Light of the Gentiles, amen.
