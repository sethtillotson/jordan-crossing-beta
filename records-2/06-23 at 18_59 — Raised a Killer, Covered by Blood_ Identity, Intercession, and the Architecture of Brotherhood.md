# 06-23 at 18:59 — Raised a Killer, Covered by Blood: Identity, Intercession, and the Architecture of Brotherhood

**Title:** 06-23 at 18:59 — Raised a Killer, Covered by Blood: Identity, Intercession, and the Architecture of Brotherhood

**Recorded:** 2026-06-23 18:59:35

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture)

**Classification:** Call Reflection

## Opening Observation

What began as a late-night call with my brother Samuel — drunk, disoriented, gripping a wine bottle in a dark park while searching for a lost bag — unfolded into something far larger than its surface suggested: a living parable of deliverance without liberation, of builders who cannot yet fully inhabit what they have been given, of covenant brotherhood pressed into service by crisis. As I listened, I found myself not merely witnessing a man in danger but standing at the intersection of several vast spiritual territories at once — identity, intercession, vocation, stewardship, and the ancient question of what it costs a man to be genuinely transformed rather than merely rescued. The weight I carried out of that evening was not anxiety but something closer to holy sorrow pressed through with stubborn hope — the kind that comes from knowing that the God who began a good work does not abandon it in the dark.

**Why This Matters:**

The Body of Christ in this generation is full of people who have been genuinely saved — blood-covered, reborn, marked by real encounters with the living God — and yet remain functionally bound to the identity forged in the furnace of their earliest wounds. Samuel’s story is not unique to Samuel. It is the story of countless brothers and sisters who carry the survival codes of their former life like armor they cannot remove, not because they love the old life but because no one ever told them the new creation is not merely positional — it is meant to be *inhabited*. That gap between justification and sanctification is where most of the battle is actually fought. And beneath that personal war runs a second current equally deserving of attention: the question of how gifted men build together, how they steward what God places in their hands, how they honor one another’s design without collapsing into competition, and what it means to bring the lordship of Christ into every terminal window, every pitch room, every late-night call, and every moment when a brother admits he is standing on a ledge. If the Church does not learn to name these territories together — the spiritual warfare of identity formation and the sacred calling of vocational co-labor — we will keep watching our most battle-hardened brothers cycle through the same drains while our builders erect impressive structures on foundations that belong to no covenant.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

From a purely natural standpoint, everything that unfolded across these conversations could be filed under predictable categories: a young man with a traumatic upbringing making substance-influenced decisions in the middle of the night, his nervous system calibrated for hypervigilance finding familiarity in chaos; three ambitious entrepreneurs strategizing about market gaps, dividing labor, and projecting revenue with the confident energy that startup culture celebrates; former hustlers pivoting their financial acumen from underground credit markets and DeFi scalping toward legitimate SaaS development, which the world would read as a grit arc and commend without hesitation. A psychologist would trace Samuel’s behavioral pattern clearly — early attachment disruption, a mother who modeled conflict resolution through physical dominance, an adult who unconsciously recreates the conditions of his formation because they are familiar even when harmful. A business consultant would evaluate the Airbnb cleaner marketplace idea, assess the two-day timeline against the scope of the build, calculate the investor pitch probability, and advise on competitive differentiation. A financial analyst would note the thirty-second crypto hack as a cautionary tale about two-factor authentication. Each of these frameworks would extract something true, name something real, and offer something useful. The natural mind is not malicious in its interpretation — it is simply navigating by instruments that cannot detect the full terrain.

**The Limitation:**

The problem with every purely natural frame in play across these conversations is the same: it accounts for the wound, the skill, and the opportunity while remaining entirely blind to the covenantal dimension underlying all three. The natural mind can diagnose addiction and anxiety but cannot perceive that when Samuel says he sees demons every time he drinks, he is not speaking metaphorically — he is a man whose spiritual permeability is registering genuine realities more acutely when the hedge of sobriety is removed. It can celebrate the entrepreneurial pivot from burn cards to SaaS development but cannot hear the difference between provision that flows from covenant and provision that flows from cleverness — the distinction Samuel himself named when he said, offhandedly but with absolute precision, that the only money that was ever *real* came after they turned to Christ. It can admire the recognition between gifted builders and call it personality chemistry, but it cannot perceive that when two makers find each other at midnight and ask whether they have found another divergent, something of Genesis is echoing in the room — the God who worked and formed and called it good, still breathing that impulse into the sons He is raising up. To see only ambition where God has placed design, to see only pattern where God is speaking, to see only bad luck where God has placed an altar — this is the consistent limitation of reasoning that has no grid for the sovereignty of a God who governs the whereabouts of lost bags and the timing of thirty-second wallet breaches with equal attentiveness.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

The first thing that arrests me as I sit with everything that unfolded in this long evening is the name. I said it aloud on the call — “Samuel means ‘heard by the Lord’” — and I was not offering a biographical footnote. Something in me was reaching for the declaration that this man’s very identity is a covenant. The Hebrew שְׁמוּאֵל (*Shemu’el*, sheh-moo-EL) carries within it the verb שָׁמַע (*shama*, shah-MAH), meaning to hear, to listen, to obey, and the divine name אֵל (*El*, EL), the strong one. To be named *Shemu’el* is to be named “God has heard” — not “God might hear,” not “God heard once,” but a standing declaration that this man’s cries, even the ones made from the bottom of a bottle in a dark park, are registered before the throne. The biblical Samuel heard God’s voice as a child in the temple, before he had language for what hearing God sounded like. He was in proximity to the sacred, under the covering of an older minister, and the Lord called him by name in the dark. I am not forcing typology here — the pattern is already present in my brother’s life. He is a young man with genuine prophetic sensitivity who hears and sees things in the spirit, who is currently doing what the young Samuel did in those pre-formation years: waking up to a voice he is not yet fully trained to distinguish from the noise around him. The question is not whether God is speaking to him. The question is whether he will learn to שָׁמַע (shah-MAH) — to hear and obey — with enough sobriety and stillness to receive what is being transmitted.

What arrested me most deeply in the conversation was the moment Samuel said: *I’ve been raised a killer.* And then the story about his mother — twice, at different points in his school years — physically depositing him outside the car to go settle a matter with his fists. I felt the weight of that. Not shock, not judgment, but the particular grief of seeing a man shaped by a formation that was never designed to produce a disciple. His mother, by her own survival logic, was teaching him something true in the world she knew: *if you don’t enforce your own dignity, no one will.* That is not malice. That is a broken world reproducing its broken code into the next generation with genuine maternal love as the medium. Paul writes to the Ephesians that we wrestle not against flesh and blood — οὐκ ἔστιν ἡμῖν ἡ πάλη πρὸς αἷμα καὶ σάρκα (Ephesians 6:12) — and part of what Paul means is precisely this: the real enemy is not the people who shaped us badly. The real enemy worked *through* them to install a nature that feels native but is foreign to the Kingdom. The need for chaos, the need for an adversary in order to feel respected — these are not character flaws to be managed. They are the fruit of a deep formation that the Spirit must completely dismantle and rebuild.

The most powerful thing I said that night — and I say this not with pride but with the sober recognition that it came from somewhere deeper than my own analysis — was this: *you got delivered but you’re not liberated.* Deliverance is an event. Liberation is a process. Deliverance is what happened at the cross, applied to a specific moment of rescue. Liberation is what the Spirit accomplishes as He renews the mind, replaces the old architecture of identity, and trains a man to actually inhabit what has already been declared true about him. The Greek word in 2 Corinthians 5:17 — καινὴ κτίσις (*kainē ktisis*, kigh-NAY KTEE-sis) — does not describe renovation of the old self. It describes a qualitatively different category of being. The newness Paul names is ontological, not cosmetic. And yet a man can be genuinely a new creation positionally while remaining functionally operational from his old wiring in the daily territories of life — and the enemy knows that gap and works it expertly. The cycle I named on the call — win, celebrate, drink, danger, lose, shame, reset — is not merely a personal pattern. It is a systematic assault on the formation of a man whom God is trying to make faithful in the obscure place. The deliverance is sealed. The liberation requires cooperation. It requires the painful, daily dying that Paul calls crucifying the flesh with its passions and desires (Galatians 5:24).

What followed my brother through that dark park was more than physical danger. He said it himself: when he drinks, he sees demons; when he is sober, he does not. I want to be careful here and not over-interpret, but I also cannot under-interpret what the Spirit is pointing to. The modern Western church has trained itself to either over-spiritualize everything or to psychologize everything, and it rarely holds both in proper tension. But 1 Peter 5:8 does not give us that luxury — it commands νήφω (*nēphō*, NAY-foh), watchful sobriety, and frames it explicitly as a *defensive military posture* against an adversary who prowls. The call to sobriety is not merely moral. It is the maintenance of the hedge. My brother’s spiritual permeability — the same gift that makes him sensitive to the Spirit — makes him vulnerable to every other influence when his defenses are compromised. That night in the park, as he walked toward his idol, he was simultaneously being shown by the heavens what he was walking toward. And then the object was simply gone. What kind of God does that? A God who is deeply involved, watching, and who would rather empty a man’s hands than watch him fill them with what will harm him.

The figure Samuel’s own mind offered during that walk was Joseph of Nazareth — not the patriarch but the carpenter, the earthly father of the incarnate Son of God. I found that resonance moving in a way I could not fully explain in the moment. There is almost nothing written about this man. He receives a dream, he obeys, he protects, he raises, he disappears from the narrative. What we know of him is drawn almost entirely from what he did not do: he did not abandon Mary, he did not expose her to public shame, he did not demand recognition for the unimaginable weight he carried. Joseph of Nazareth is the patron saint of the unnamed faithful — the men who do the most important work in the most invisible rooms and who receive no chapter, no monument, no epistle bearing their name. My brother connected with him because something deep in him recognizes that pattern: the man who carries more than the world gives him credit for, placed into a position he did not choose, whose faithfulness is measured not in headlines but in presence. And yet here is the tension: Joseph of Nazareth walked in radical sobriety of spirit. His obedience was the quiet, costly kind — the kind that requires a man to have such a low center of gravity before God that he can receive direction in a dream and move the next morning without needing the world to validate him. The very thing my brother most admires in Joseph is the very thing the pattern I named that night is stealing from him.

Ananias came to my mind during the call and will not leave me now. Acts 9 records that when the risen Christ needed someone to go to Saul of Tarsus — the most dangerous man to the early Church — He called a disciple in Damascus by name. We know almost nothing about Ananias. He had no ministry title, no recorded miracles before this moment, no introduction in the text. What we know is that he was *praying* when the Lord spoke to him. His posture was the qualification. He went, terrified, and laid hands on a man who had been breathing threats against the saints, and called him *brother*. One act of obedient, costly presence — and the entire trajectory of the Gentile church shifted. My brother was marveling at this: that Jehovah, who raised God incarnate, receives no verse for that act, while a random praying disciple in Damascus gets a named moment simply for being available and obedient in crisis. I think the Spirit was using that observation to say something to both of us: *obscurity and obedience are not the absence of calling. They are often the deepest expression of it.*

There is a second current running through this entire evening that I cannot disentangle from the first, because the Spirit will not allow it: the question of what it means to build together as men who carry different gifts and must learn to honor what the other brings. When I listened to Samuel describe his financial architecture — *I am the finance man, I make the things in finance which shouldn’t be legal, legal* — I heard beneath the self-assurance something the Hebrew wisdom tradition actually honors: the naming of one’s מַתָּן (*mattan*, mah-TAHN), one’s gift and endowment, so that those around can rightly calibrate their own roles and expectations. Proverbs 18:16 does not frame this as arrogance. It frames it as the natural movement of a gift given room. And when I kept returning on the call to the question of scope — *give me a scope of work, we won’t say yes if we don’t know how to build it* — I was not being timid. Moses does not lead Israel out of Egypt until the burning bush defines the scope. Nehemiah does not begin rebuilding the wall until he surveys the ruins by night and counts the cost. The insistence on scope before commitment is the wisdom of the faithful steward who will not promise what has not been authorized.

What I witnessed in the room across these hours was the Body of Christ operating in its most practical dimension: each member genuinely carrying what the others cannot, and the whole more capable than any part. I brought technical architecture. James built quietly while Samuel and I were still debating cooking credentials, and his demo was ready before the conversation caught up to it. Samuel brought relational capital, the capacity to open rooms that most builders never enter, and the prophetic marketplace function of seeing what can be sold before it exists. This is not incidental. It is the design. God structures gifted communities precisely so that the gaps in one person are the strengths of another — not to create dependence but to create the necessity that is the soil in which genuine covenant grows. Paul’s body metaphor in 1 Corinthians 12 is not a Sunday morning ecclesiology. It is the blueprint for how the Kingdom advances in the earth through the complementary deployment of χαρίσματα (*charismata*, kah-RIZ-mah-tah) — grace-gifts distributed by the same Spirit for the coherence of the whole.

I shared my own testimony in the course of that evening — not as performance but because the Spirit pressed it in the moment as a word someone in the room needed to receive. I was secular, not seeking, not adjacent to seeking. I had tried everything: the grinding hustle, the dark industries, the entrepreneurial striving, the descent from building sophisticated AI architecture to welding aluminum toolboxes in a cold facility. That descent — the humiliation of expectation, the stripping of everything I had negotiated my worth by — was the precise condition in which the voice came. Not when I was ascending. When I had nothing left to negotiate with. And what came was not dramatic. It was a word that dropped into my chest from deep within: *read your Bible.* Familiar and yet unmistakably other. I resisted it for two weeks. Every silence, every pause in the noise, the word was there. When I finally sat down with Matthew 1:1, the Spirit met me in the obedience. He did not wait until I understood. He met me in the opening of the book. The corpus that has grown from that season — the volumes, the meditations, the study notes written in the middle of the night — is not the product of my gifting. It is the product of an emptied life made available to the One who builds what He chooses through whoever will yield.

And here I circle back to the phrase Brother Samuel offered — *we only made our real money when we turned to Christ* — because I do not want to let it pass. The Hebrew concept of אֱמֶת (*emet*, EH-met) — truth, reality, faithfulness — carries within it the idea of that which is *solid*, that which does not evaporate under pressure. It is used throughout the Psalms and Prophets of God’s covenant faithfulness. What my brother was testifying to, whether he had theological language for it or not, is that what came before was not אֱמֶת (EH-met) — it was appearance without substance, motion without foundation. The 1.3 million in crypto was gone in thirty seconds. The sixty-five million in credit lines was essentially inaccessible. Every system they built was a well you had to return to, requiring constant monitoring, constant vigilance, constant performance. The spring Jesus described to the Samaritan woman in John 4:14 is a πηγή (*pēgē*, PAY-gay) — a living, self-generating source, not a cistern you maintain with two-factor authentication that you eventually turn off because you are too tired to keep logging in. The thirty-second hack is not merely a cautionary tale about digital security. It is a parable. The Father was being merciful, hedging up the way with thorns so that the wanderer could not continue walking into a field that led to a cliff.

The moment I named the cycle on the call — win, celebrate, drink, danger — I felt something settle in the conversation. Not harshness but clarity. The Hebrew word מוּסָר (*musar*, moo-SAR) — instruction through discipline, correction through relationship — fills the book of Proverbs, which frames it explicitly as the father’s investment in the son’s future. What I was doing in that conversation was operating in the intercessory space I had already been given — the priestly role that had already, in prior seasons, made specific agreements before heaven regarding this brother’s life. When I reminded him of what the belly of Sheol had cost him, I was not threatening him. I was bearing witness. That is what faithful priestly intercession sometimes looks like. It names the covenant reality plainly: there are consequences when a man refuses to come up higher, and there is a God who loves this man too much to let the pattern continue unchallenged. The παιδεία (*paideia*, pie-DAY-ah) the author of Hebrews describes in chapter twelve is not punitive wrath — it is the shaping pressure of a Father who intends something for His child that the child cannot yet see. My brother’s discomfort that night was not evidence of God’s absence. It was evidence of His particular and unrelenting attention.

The conversation also produced a moment I want to name carefully: the sister in the room who said she does not always believe but that she respects it. I said to her, *I love you, and our God loves you just the way you are.* I mean that fully. The posture of the One who meets the Samaritan woman at the well is not condemnation — it is conversation. There is an open door in that declaration, and it swings toward every person in every room, secular or seeking or somewhere beautifully between. And for the builders gathered — for the man writing Python scripts and crawling architecture at three in the morning, for the man who can walk into a room with billionaires and make the pitch land, for the man who builds the demo quietly while others are still debating — the invitation is not to add Christ as a spiritual layer atop an otherwise self-assembled life. The invitation is the exchange. Not from bad hustle to good hustle but from self as source to God as source. That is the line the natural mind cannot cross on its own terms, and it is the only crossing that produces provision with substance — the kind that does not disappear in thirty seconds.

The closing moment that stays with me — the grief over the woman he lost — deserves to be named without being rushed. Samuel said: *everyone tells you before the divorce it’ll be fine. No one warns you about the regret.* I know that grief. I told him so. And what I want to say here, with full tenderness, is that the Lord does not take from us to punish us. He takes what has become an idol precisely because He is אֵל קַנָּא (*El Qanna*, EL kah-NAH) — the God of jealous, exclusive covenant love — whose jealousy is not possessive insecurity but the covenantally committed devotion of One who cannot tolerate a competitor for the heart He has claimed as His own. When the thing we love most becomes the thing we trust more than we trust Him, love itself compels the loss — because a Father who lets His son cling to an inferior comfort in place of a superior inheritance is not a loving Father. That grief is real. That loss is real. But it is not abandonment. It is the severe mercy of the God who sees the whole map, not just the square we are standing on. He is אֵל רֳאִי (*El Roi*, EL ro-EE) — the God who sees — and He sees precisely where human attention is most absent.

The grace in this entire evening is not a footnote. It is the frame. My brother walked through a dark park, drunk, gripping a wine bottle, seeing shadows, missing his things, grieving a lost love — and he called me. He stayed on the phone. He heard correction. He laughed. He pushed back. He received. And James built the thing while the rest of us were still talking about whether it was worth building. And across all of it, between the technical debates and the entrepreneurial projections and the theological urgency and the pastoral grief, the Spirit was doing what the Spirit does in every imperfect and profane and hilarious and real conversation where two or three are gathered: pressing something alive and necessary into the ground of human relationship so that it would grow.

## Implications and Challenges

The first challenge this entire evening presses is the one I named aloud in the park: the Church must learn to distinguish between deliverance and liberation and build formation processes that take both seriously. We celebrate the moment of conversion, the dramatic rescue, the night someone is pulled from the edge — and rightly so. But we are weak at the slow, grinding, relational work of helping a person *inhabit* their new nature rather than performing it on Sundays and reverting to survival mode the rest of the week. Samuel’s cycle is not a sign that he is not saved. It is a sign that the scaffolding required to hold a man in genuine transformation has not yet been built around him. The gap between justification and sanctification is not a theological footnote — it is a pastoral emergency for every brother and sister who has been genuinely touched by God but not yet genuinely liberated from the architecture of their former formation.

A second challenge concerns the prosperity reflex that has quietly colonized much of Western Christian consciousness — the assumption that God’s favor always looks like addition. When something goes missing, when the market crashes in thirty seconds, when the expected breakthrough does not arrive, the untrained disciple has no grid for reading the Father’s hand in the taking-away. But the canon does not support that imbalance. From Job to John the Baptist, from the exile of Israel to the cross of Christ, the pattern of God’s formative work runs directly through subtraction. The theology of divine breaking — the bread that must be broken before it feeds multitudes, the alabaster box that must shatter before its fragrance fills the room — is not a minor theme in Scripture. It is central. And a Church that never teaches her sons to read an altar in an inconvenience leaves them defenseless in the very seasons God most intends for their formation.

Third, and with full grace: this long evening surfaces the consistent temptation in entrepreneurially-minded Kingdom circles to retain the language of faith while the operating system quietly reverts to optimization. Samuel named Christ as the turning point into real provision — and that deserves honor because it was true and it was said publicly. But it cannot remain a decorative sentence. If Christ is not genuinely present in the strategy room — not merely invoked as the explanation for past success — then every structure we build is vulnerable to the same thirty-second exposure that took the crypto wallet. Integrity between faith and commerce is not an optional add-on for the believer in the marketplace. It is the load-bearing wall. The same standard of just weight and honest dealing that governed the ancient marketplace governs every terminal session, every pitch deck, and every token on every chain.

Fourth, there is a challenge to the pride of a full life. I shared openly about descending from high-level technical work to welding aluminum toolboxes, and I press on it again here because there is something in the humiliation of expectation that the Father specifically uses. The voice did not come in the ascending years. It came in the aluminum dust of the descent. If we only listen for God in the seasons of elevation, we will miss the deeper conversations He has reserved for the valleys. The Lord does not only speak at the mountaintop. He speaks in the dark park. He speaks in the moment the bag goes missing. He speaks when the thing you were reaching for is simply not there. The question is whether we have been formed enough to hear Him when the noise of our own ambition is finally quiet.

Fifth, the language of trauma can become a theological cage if we are not careful. Samuel’s upbringing was real. Its wounds were real. But the moment I heard him lean into “I’ve been raised a killer” as the standing explanation for the current pattern, the Spirit pressed me to push back — because the enemy was using a true historical fact to argue for a permanent present-tense identity that the blood of Christ has already overwritten. Compassion does not mean agreeing with a man’s assessment of his own fixed nature. Compassion sometimes means holding the new identity in front of him with enough love and firmness that he cannot easily dismiss it. The καινὴ κτίσις (kigh-NAY KTEE-sis) is not merely positional. It is real. And the work of genuine brotherhood is to keep insisting on that reality until the brother can insist on it himself.

A final grace, and it belongs at the close: the blessing at the end of the long call — *get some sleep, God bless, goodnight* — is worth protecting as a cultural practice in a world that glorifies the grind. These small benedictions, these short genuine words of care over a brother’s rest and his next day, are the grammar of a Kingdom community. They are the evidence that we are building something more durable than a product — that we are building the kind of covenant among men that will hold weight when the revenue projections disappoint and the timelines slip and the crisis arrives at two in the morning and someone needs to stay on the phone and walk through the dark. Let that be what we are known for among ourselves first. Everything else we build will find its proper proportion from that center.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

True spiritual maturity is not the absence of struggle or the performance of composure. It is the slow, costly process of learning to *inhabit* the new creation identity — all the way down into the nervous system, the survival code, the patterns of celebration and self-destruction — until the peace of Christ is not merely doctrine but settled reality. For those of us who have been delivered from hard places, the work is to cooperate with the מוּסָר (moo-SAR) of the Father, receiving His discipline as the evidence of His love rather than retreating into the familiar chaos that used to feel like safety. And for those of us who build — who stay awake through the long nights, who light up when the vision executes — maturity means holding that drive submitted to covenant purpose rather than self-accumulation, and holding every projection with open hands before the One who opens and closes every door.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

The call coming out of this entire evening is a call to sobriety — practical, spiritual, and relational. For those caught in cycles like the one I walked Samuel through that night, Kingdom obedience means identifying the specific triggers of the pattern, consecrating those thresholds to the Lord, and building real accountability into the structure of daily life — not accountability as religious policing but accountability as covenant brotherhood. For those building in the marketplace, obedience means bringing the lordship of Christ into every scope of work — asking, before every build and every pitch, whether what is being made serves human flourishing and honors the God in whom all things συνέστηκεν (*synestēken*, syn-ES-tay-ken) — cohere and hold together. And obedience means finishing what we start. The servant who buried his talent did not bury it in rebellion. He buried it in fear. The Kingdom calls us to step into the river and trust that it parts under obedient feet.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

Those who carry prophetic sensitivity — who hear and see in the spirit, who recognize design in others before it is fully manifest — must learn that the gift is not self-sustaining. It requires the ordered, consecrated, sober life. The νήφω (NAY-foh) of 1 Peter 5:8 is not merely a moral virtue. It is what maintains the clarity required to steward what we perceive accurately. And the prophetic posture this entire evening calls us into is the posture of Ananias — simply praying, simply present, simply obedient when the Name calls. Not heroic, not dramatic, not waiting for a stage. Available. The Kingdom has always advanced on the labor of people who do not wait to be noticed before they move — who build the demo while others are still debating, who answer the phone at midnight when a brother is in the dark, who speak the covenant name into the chaos and trust that the God who hears has already heard.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I come before You from the altar that has become the most real location in my life — the secret place where every conversation, every call, every crisis, every late-night build eventually arrives — and I bring before You this entire evening in all its complexity and grace. I bring my brother Samuel: Your son, named by You, heard by You, seen by You, and not yet fully free. I declare over his life what You have already declared: that he is not his formation, not his wound, not the code a broken world wrote into him. He is a new creation, and the old things are passing away. Lord, go into the deep places — into the places where the survival code lives, into the marrow of the fear and the rage and the grief — and speak Your name there. Let him hear You in the dark the way the young שְׁמוּאֵל (sheh-moo-EL) heard You before he had language for it. Loose him from the cycle — not just the next time but *through* it, into the liberty of a man who no longer needs chaos to feel seen. I bring the builders: the ones who stay awake, who light up when the vision executes, who have the grace to ask what each other is built for and to honor the answer. Consecrate the drive You have placed in them. Let every line of code, every pitch, every scope of work be submitted to Your lordship — not as religious formality but as the genuine governing principle that transforms clever tools into Kingdom instruments. Cover the open doors that substance has created. Restore what was taken. Redeem the grief that love cost. And for the one in the room who said she respects it but is not fully there yet — let this be seed in soil You have already been preparing, and let Your patience be its own form of proclamation. You are אֵל רֳאִי (EL ro-EE) — the God who sees. You are אֱמֶת (EH-met) — the One whose faithfulness does not evaporate. You are the spring that does not run dry. And You have heard. Be glorified in the park, in the dark, in the bottle, in the name, in the call, in the build, in the blessing at the end of a long night. May everything we raise — every product, every prayer, every covenant — be held loosely before You, and may the fruit remain. In the name of Jesus, who is the same yesterday, today, and forever. Amen.
