# 06-22 at 15:06 — Running on Fumes and Faith

\*\*Title:\*\*06-22 Call Reflection: Daily Faith, Empty Tanks, and Kingdom Obedience

**Recorded:** 2026-06-22 15:06:31

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture)

**Classification:** Call Reflection

## Opening Observation

I am sitting with what came out of my own mouth almost without thinking — *“Faith, my friend”* — spoken in answer to a simple, practical question about how I was going to get home. There was no gas money, no answer on the other end of the phone, no certainty about what the next hour held, and yet something in me reached past all of that and named the only real strategy available. What arrests me now, looking back, is that I said it lightly, almost humorously, and yet it was truer than I knew.

**Why This Matters:**

There is a category of faith that only gets activated when the natural options run dry. It is not the faith of abundance or preparation; it is the faith of the threshold moment, the faith that moves when the tank reads empty and the contact is not picking up and the evening is pressing on. This matters for discipleship because so much of modern Christian culture has been conditioned to call on faith as a supplement to natural sufficiency rather than as the very ground of movement when natural sufficiency is absent. What I stumbled into in this brief exchange — the willingness to drive on fumes toward the next town, trusting that the engine would hold and the station would be open — is precisely the kind of embodied, unremarkable, daily faith that the Kingdom is actually built on. It is not dramatic. It is not a stage moment. It is just a man with an empty tank choosing to move forward anyway, because what else are you going to do?

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

The natural mind looks at this exchange and sees a logistical problem with an incomplete solution. A man is stranded without gas money, dependent on a third party who is not responding, running low on fuel, and facing the very real possibility of being stuck. The reasonable response, from the worldly frame, is anxiety, frustration, or at minimum a kind of resigned helplessness — maybe some frustration at the person who hasn’t replied, maybe some stress about the hour and the closing gas station. The humor in the exchange would be read as a coping mechanism, a way of softening the edge of a genuinely uncomfortable situation. The plan — drive on fumes and hope — would be assessed as reckless or naïve, the kind of thing a person does when they have no better option, not something worth theological reflection.

**The Limitation:**

But the natural mind cannot perceive that there is a pattern woven through exactly these kinds of unremarkable, in-between moments — the ones that don’t feel like spiritual events at all — where the posture of the heart is actually being formed and revealed. What looks like a man making a casual joke about faith is actually a man demonstrating what faith looks like when it has been practiced long enough to become reflexive. The flesh calculates; the spirit moves. The flesh waits for certainty before committing to the next step; the spirit commits to the next step and receives what it needs along the way. The limitation of purely natural reasoning is that it cannot see that the engine running on fumes and the soul running on trust are speaking the same language — the language of a life that has learned, however imperfectly, to move when God says move, even when the gauge says stop.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

I keep coming back to that old GM 3800 engine. I said it would run on anything — that it would sputter for five miles down a highway and simply not give up. I said it with affection, almost admiration. And the Spirit is showing me now that what I love about that engine is what I am being invited to love about the kind of faith that actually carries a person through the long haul of a life given to God. It is not elegant. It is not new. It has mileage on it. But it does not quit when conditions are less than ideal, and it will take you somewhere even when by all visible measures it should have stopped long ago.

There is a moment in 1 Kings 17 that comes to mind — the prophet Elijah standing before the widow of Zarephath, asking her for bread when she has only enough flour and oil to make one last meal for herself and her son before they die. The natural logic of that moment is preservation: protect what little remains, do not give it away, hold on. But Elijah speaks a word from the LORD and says *“do not fear”* — and then gives her a specific instruction to act first, before the promise has been fulfilled. The jar of flour does not run out. The jug of oil does not run dry. But she had to move first. She had to begin making the bread before the miracle was in her hands. That is the structure of faith that the Spirit is pressing on me through this small, ordinary evening moment. Drive to the gas station before you know how you’re paying. Get to the next town over first. Move on the word, then watch what the LORD does with the jar.

Hebrews 11:1 says, *“Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen.”* The Greek word translated *substance* is ὑπόστασις (hypostasis, hoo-POS-tah-sis) — and it is a word that carries tremendous weight. It does not mean wishful thinking or optimistic feeling. In classical usage it referred to the actual foundation beneath a structure, the bedrock on which something stands. So faith, rightly understood, is not an emotional state I generate to feel better about an uncertain situation. It is a *foundation* — something I am standing on even when I cannot see it, something that holds even when the visible evidence suggests it shouldn’t. When I said *“faith, my friend”* and then put the car in drive, I was not being reckless. I was standing on ὑπόστασις (hoo-POS-tah-sis) — on something real, even though it was invisible.

What strikes me further is that this moment did not happen in prayer. It did not happen in a church building or during a Bible study. It happened in a parking lot, at twenty to nine in the evening, in a phone call with a brother who was doing his best to help me get sorted. And yet the Spirit was present in it, as He is present in all things for those who belong to Him. Romans 8:28 is not a verse about church services. It is a verse about *all things* — including empty gas tanks and unanswered phone calls and the minor pressures of an ordinary Tuesday evening. *“And we know that all things work together for good to those who love God, to those who are the called according to His purpose.”* The whole shape of a life surrendered to God becomes the curriculum of faith. Nothing is wasted. Even the inconvenience is instructive.

I think about the Israelites in the wilderness. Exodus 16 describes a people who were genuinely out of food, genuinely uncertain, genuinely facing the gap between where they were and where they needed to be. And what God gave them was manna — bread from heaven — but only enough for each day. They were not permitted to stockpile it, because the design of the provision was not to eliminate dependence; it was to *cultivate* it. Every single morning they had to wake up and trust that what they needed would be there. The λόγος (logos, LAW-goss) — the word, the governing wisdom of God — was building into them a daily posture of expectation and reliance. That is what I am recognizing in my own small way in this moment. The situation was not resolved in advance. The money was not already in my account. The contact was not picking up his phone. I was being invited into the daily-manna kind of faith: move toward the provision before the provision is visible, and trust that the LORD who has fed you before has not forgotten that you need to eat today.

Brother Samuel’s response is worth sitting with too. He heard me say *“faith”* and instead of dismissing it, he said *“that’s a solid strategy.”* There was recognition in that — the recognition that passes between people who have both spent time learning to trust something larger than their own arrangements. That is the fellowship of the Kingdom. It is not always a deep theological exchange. Sometimes it is two men on a phone call, one of whom has an empty tank, and the other of whom hears the word *faith* and knows exactly what it means because he has lived inside of it himself. The writer of Hebrews says we are surrounded by a *“cloud of witnesses”* — and I want to suggest that the cloud is not only the ancient saints. It includes the brothers and sisters alive right now who say *“that’s a solid strategy”* when you name faith as your plan, because they have watched it work.

There is something tender in the closing of this call that I do not want to pass over. *“Let me know if you get to the gas station safely.”* That is pastoral care in its most elemental form. It is not elaborate. It does not require a theology degree. It is one brother saying to another: *I am watching over you. Report back. I want to know you made it.* This is the ἀγάπη (agapē, ah-GAH-pay) — covenantal love — that Paul describes in 1 Corinthians 13 as the greatest of all things. Not sentimental warmth, but the committed, attentive, practical love that tracks with a person through the unglamorous details of their life and cares whether they get home. ἀγάπη (ah-GAH-pay) shows up in parking lots at twenty to nine. It shows up in a brother who keeps hitting up a contact on your behalf when you are stuck. It is the love that is not contingent on the situation being resolved.

And then — *“Do you want the meeting when we’re back?”* Even in the middle of the unresolved situation, with the gas question still hanging and the contact still not replying, the meeting was still on the table. The work of the Kingdom was still being organized. There is something in that small exchange that speaks to me about the posture of people who have decided not to let present difficulty become a reason to abandon present obedience. We were going to have the meeting. We were going to do the thing we came together to do. The empty tank was a logistical problem, not a theological catastrophe, and it was not going to cancel the gathering. Jesus says in Matthew 6:33, *“But seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things shall be added to you.”* The order matters. Kingdom first. The gas station is one of *these things*. It gets added. But only after the priority is honored.

I find myself sitting with the image of the car sputtering five miles down the highway and not giving up. There is a parable in that engine. I have been that engine in seasons of my life — running past what should have been the limit, held together by something I could not fully explain, arriving somewhere I should not have been able to reach by any reasonable calculation. The Spirit does not always give us a full tank before we leave the driveway. Sometimes He gives us just enough to reach the next provision point, and then just enough from there to reach the one after that. The journey of faith is structured this way on purpose. Not to exhaust us, but to keep us close — to ensure that every mile of the road has His fingerprints on it, because we were dependent on Him for every mile of it.

What I said almost without thinking — *“faith, my friend”* — I want to say now with full intention. That is the strategy. That has always been the strategy. Not the absence of practical effort, not a refusal to make phone calls or seek provision through legitimate means, but the bedrock conviction underneath all of it that the God who called me has resources I cannot see, and that He has committed Himself to the lives of those who are committed to Him. The car made it or it didn’t. But either way, I was not alone in the drive, and I am not alone now.

## Implications and Challenges

There is a version of Christian faith in contemporary Western culture that has been so thoroughly domesticated by comfort and convenience that it no longer knows how to function in the ordinary pressures of daily life. Faith has been relegated to the language of large declarations and altar moments, while the small, unremarkable acts of daily trust — driving to the gas station on fumes, waiting on a brother to reply, committing to the meeting even when the logistics are unresolved — are treated as secular and separate from spiritual life. This meditation invites us to reject that division entirely. Every moment of genuine trust is a spiritual act, and the Kingdom is built one such moment at a time.

The prosperity gospel and its many cultural cousins have taught a generation to interpret financial pressure and unresolved logistics as signs of spiritual failure or insufficient faith. But the witness of Scripture is precisely the opposite. The widow’s jar was empty when the miracle happened. The Red Sea was closed when God opened it. The manna had not yet fallen when Israel woke up hungry. God consistently works at the edge of human sufficiency, not because He enjoys difficulty, but because that is the precise location where genuine trust is formed. If we always have enough in our own reserves, we will never learn what it means to be carried.

There is a quiet challenge here to the individualism of modern Christian spirituality. I was not handling this situation alone. There was a brother on the phone, tracking with me, interceding on my behalf with a contact, offering to make another call, checking whether I got home safely. The Body of Christ is designed to function this way — with members genuinely involved in each other’s practical lives, not only in each other’s spiritual devotions. ἀγάπη (ah-GAH-pay) is not abstract; it is logistical. It shows up in the details.

We must also reckon with the temptation to spiritualize faith in ways that make it passive. *“Faith, my friend”* was not an excuse to sit still and wait for someone else to solve the problem. It was said from the seat of a car that was about to be put in drive. Biblical faith is almost always active — it moves toward the provision rather than waiting for the provision to arrive before moving. James 2:17 is not subtle: *“Faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead.”* The works are not earning the favor of God; they are the shape that living trust takes in the body, in the real world, on a real road, in a real car with a real gas problem.

Finally, the simplicity of this moment deserves to be honored rather than over-theologized. Sometimes the Spirit speaks not through elaborate vision or deep biblical unfolding, but through an offhand comment that turns out to be the truest thing said in a conversation. The Kingdom is not always manifested in dramatic events. It is often manifested in the quiet faithfulness of ordinary people making the next right move with what little they have, blessing each other as they go, and committing to the meeting when they get back.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

Maturity in faith is not the absence of pressure or uncertainty; it is the development of a reflexive trust that responds to pressure with forward movement rather than paralysis. When *“faith, my friend”* becomes the first thing out of our mouths rather than the last resort after all other options have failed, something genuine has been formed in us. Let us examine what we reach for first when the natural options are not there.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

The meeting is still on the table. Regardless of what logistical pressures are pressing on us today, the work of the Kingdom we have been called to participate in does not wait for perfect conditions. We commit to the gathering, we commit to the service, we commit to the obedience — and we trust that the God who gave us the assignment knows about the empty tank.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

A prophetic people learns to read their own ordinary experiences through the lens of what God is building in them. The car that runs on fumes is a word. The brother who says *“that’s a solid strategy”* when you name faith is a witness. The gas station that is still open when you arrive is a testimony. Let us develop eyes to see the testimony embedded in the texture of a surrendered daily life.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I come before You from the position of a co-heir of this Kingdom — one who has been written into Your purposes, who stands between heaven and earth as a servant of the Word, in agreement with Your will and with what You are doing in the earth. I thank You for the small moments. I thank You for empty tanks and unanswered calls and brothers who say *“let me know when you get there safely.”* I thank You that You are not only the God of the altar but the God of the parking lot, the God of the twenty-to-nine phone call, the God of the sputtering engine that does not quit. I ask that You would form in Your people a faith that is reflexive — not manufactured under pressure, but released from a place of genuine rest in Your faithfulness. I intercede for every brother and sister who is running on fumes right now, in finances, in strength, in hope. Let them feel the ground of ὑπόστασις (hoo-POS-tah-sis) beneath them. Let them drive forward. Let the jar not run dry. And let us keep committing to the meeting, to the gathering, to the work, because You are worthy of our best obedience even in the middle of the unresolved thing. Be glorified in the ordinary. Be magnified in the daily. Let every mile of the road belong to You. In the name of Jesus Christ, who is the same yesterday, today, and forever — Amen.
