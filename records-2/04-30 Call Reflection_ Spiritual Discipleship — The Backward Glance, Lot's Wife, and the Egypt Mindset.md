# 04-30 Call Reflection: Spiritual Discipleship — The Backward Glance, Lot’s Wife, and the Egypt Mindset

**Title:** 04-30 at 18:52 — Come Out of Her: The Backward Glance and the Pillar of Salt

**Recorded:** 2026-04-30 18:52:03

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture / Call Reflection)

**Classification:** Call Reflection

## Opening Observation

As I listened to my brother pour out his grief, his rage, his insecurity, and his honest confessions across that call, something in me recognized the ancient terrain we were walking together — the threshold between Egypt and the wilderness, between Sodom and the mountains beyond. The pain in his voice was real, the temptations were real, and the moment when the Spirit pressed a word through me about Lot’s wife and the Exodus generation was equally real. What I am seeing now, sitting with this, is that this conversation was not merely pastoral; it was itself a kind of kairos event — a moment where the eternal broke through the temporal, where the living God inserted His Word into raw human suffering, and where the cost of looking backward was being demonstrated in real time.

**Why This Matters:**

The Body of Christ is full of people who are technically free — free from relationships, free from seasons, free from old structures — yet who remain spiritually stationary because they have never truly crossed the threshold. They stand at the doorway with one foot still reaching back toward what was. This matters immensely because that posture — what the Scripture names and what I have come to call the Egypt mindset — is not merely a phase of grief; it is a spiritual condition that, left unchecked, becomes a defining orientation of the soul. My brother’s anguish was not unusual. It is, in fact, the universal anguish of every soul that has been called out of something and has not yet decided, in the deepest room of the heart, to actually leave. This is not a small pastoral problem. This is a matter of whether the journey toward the Promised Land will ever be completed — or whether the wilderness itself becomes the grave of an entire generation that was freed but never truly free.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

The natural mind, encountering a conversation like this one, would frame everything through the lens of psychological pain management. It would name what my brother is experiencing as a breakup — a relational wound in need of emotional processing, perhaps a rebound, perhaps therapy, perhaps the passage of time. It would nod sympathetically at his anger, validate his sense of betrayal, encourage him to feel his feelings, and perhaps gently suggest he stay off Instagram. It might even celebrate the vulnerability — the admitting of insecurity, the rare honesty about violent ideation, the acknowledgment of longing — as emotional progress. The worldly lens is not entirely wrong to value honesty; it is not entirely wrong to recognize that grief needs space. But the worldly mind can only see the surface layer of what is happening. It sees a man in pain processing a loss. It does not see a man standing at the edge of a burning city with his eyes beginning to drift backward over his shoulder.

**The Limitation:**

What the natural mind cannot perceive is that grief, when it becomes a sustained orientation of the heart, is not merely a wound to be managed — it is a posture that either turns toward God or calcifies into something else entirely. The natural mind has no category for the kind of spiritual diagnosis that the Spirit was pressing through this conversation: that looking back in longing is not just emotionally understandable but spiritually dangerous, not because God is harsh toward the grieving, but because the backward gaze is evidence that the soul has not yet released what God has already called it to release. The natural man cannot receive this because it sounds, from the outside, like religious cruelty — like telling a bleeding man to stop thinking about the wound. But what the Spirit knows, and what I was pressing my brother toward, is that the bleeding is prolonged precisely by the returning gaze. The natural mind has no remedy for that. It can only offer distraction or validation. What the Spirit offers is a threshold — a definitive crossing — and that crossing requires a different kind of courage than emotional expression alone can supply.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

As I sit with the weight of this conversation, the first thing that arrests me is the phrase that came through me almost unbidden: *Come out of her, my people.* I had not planned to say it. It arrived. And looking at it now through the lens of Scripture, I see why the Spirit pressed it into that moment. The call in Revelation 18:4 — “Come out of her, my people, lest you share in her sins, and lest you receive of her plagues” — is not merely a call about external Babylon, though it is certainly that. It is also a call about internal architecture: the way Babylon can be internalized, carried within the soul, so that even after the outward departure has occurred, the city still has a resident in the heart. My brother had left the relationship. He had not yet left what the relationship had come to represent in his inner world — his insecurities, his identity anchors, his measuring of himself against another man’s wealth and appearance. The Babylon he needed to come out of was not the woman. It was the idol he had unknowingly constructed from the relationship’s rubble.

The Spirit then drew me immediately to Lot’s wife, and I want to sit with this longer than I did in the call itself. In Genesis 19, the angels give Lot’s family a stark and specific command: *“Escape for your life! Do not look behind you nor stay anywhere in the plain. Escape to the mountains, lest you be destroyed”* (Genesis 19:17, NKJV). And yet Lot’s wife looked back — וַתַּבֵּט (wattabbet, vat-tah-BET), a sharp, quick backward glance — and she became a pillar of salt. Modern readers often pity her, or dismiss the story as ancient mythology. But the Spirit is pressing something much more precise. The Hebrew root of the verb carries the idea of a gaze fixed with attention, not a casual glance. She was not distracted; she was invested. She was watching. And the very substance she became — salt — is itself deeply symbolic. Salt preserves. It holds things in their former state. She did not merely look at Sodom; she became, in her very body, a monument to the impulse to preserve what God had already judged. She became what she longed for: a static thing, crystallized in the moment of departure, going nowhere.

This is what I was trying to name for my brother, and I want to name it more fully here. When he said, *“I know she’s gone, but I suppose I’m still grasping it”* — he was describing the pillar of salt in his own soul. The factual knowledge of the ending had not yet become an inward release. And this is one of the most important distinctions the Spirit teaches in the life of discipleship: there is a difference between knowing something in the mind and releasing it from the will. The Israelites in the wilderness knew they were free. They had watched Pharaoh’s army drown in the Red Sea. But their will — their longing, their appetite, their memory — remained in Egypt. Numbers 11:5 is devastating in its honesty: *“We remember the fish which we ate freely in Egypt, the cucumbers, the melons, the leeks, the onions, and the garlic.”* Slaves, cataloguing the cuisine of their captivity with nostalgia. This is the Egypt mindset: romanticizing the very chains God broke because the present provision does not satisfy the flesh.

And yet — and this is where the Spirit presses further than my brother may have been ready to receive in that moment — the manna they were dismissing was extraordinary. They called it מָן (man, MAHN), meaning literally *“What is it?”* — the very name is a confession of incomprehension. Exodus 16:31 tells us it tasted like wafers made with honey. Their clothes did not wear out through the entire wilderness journey (Deuteronomy 29:5). The pillar of cloud by day and fire by night was their constant canopy. But because they were looking backward, they could not see what was already raining down upon them. The provision was miraculous. The eyes were pointing the wrong direction. I said this to my brother, and I feel the weight of it even more now: the measure of your gratitude is the measure of your focus, and the measure of your focus is the measure of what you believe God has actually given you.

Now I want to address something my brother said that I touched on only briefly but that the Spirit will not let me pass over lightly: his anger — his raw, uncensored, violent ideation toward this other man. He was honest enough to say that he was not truly angry at the man; the man owed him nothing. The anger was directed at the woman, and beneath the anger was something even more vulnerable: shame. He said, *“This guy is noticeably more attractive than me.”* He said she had gone straight for his insecurities. And in that moment, I heard beneath the profanity and the rage the voice of a man who had placed the architecture of his identity on something other than what God says about him. This is not condemnation — this is simply the precise location of the wound. The worldly identity structure says: your worth is measured by comparison — height, wealth, attractiveness, the quality of your replacement. And when someone else seems to score higher on those metrics, the entire edifice trembles. But the kingdom identity structure, which the Scripture presses from Genesis to Revelation, says something entirely different.

In Zechariah 3, the Lord addresses the high priest Joshua — יְהוֹשֻׁעַ (Yehoshua, yeh-hoh-SHOO-ah) — who is standing before the angel of the Lord in filthy garments, with the adversary standing at his right hand to oppose him. And the Lord does not say, *“Improve yourself and come back.”* He says: *“I have removed your iniquity from you, and I will clothe you with rich garments”* (Zechariah 3:4, NKJV). The verb there is עָבַר (avar, ah-VAR) — to cause to pass over, to remove as if by transit. The iniquity is not merely forgiven in a legal sense; it is relocated. It is taken away. And in its place, Joshua is clothed with rich garments — not because of what Joshua has accumulated or how he compares to others, but because of what the Lord has spoken over him. This is what I wanted my brother to hear beneath all the spiritual counsel: your identity is not a score. It is a garment that the Lord Himself puts on you. And no photograph on Instagram can take it away.

I then pressed something else into the conversation that I want to unfold more carefully: the matter of focus as a discipline of the Kingdom. There is a thread that runs from Deuteronomy 8 — *“that he might humble you and test you, to do you good in the end”* — through Isaiah 26:3 — *“You will keep him in perfect peace, whose mind is stayed on You”* — through Philippians 4:8 — *“whatever things are true, whatever things are noble… meditate on these things”* — and it is this: what you habitually look at will shape what you habitually become. The Greek verb in Philippians 4:8 translated “meditate” is λογίζομαι (logizomai, lo-GEED-zo-my), which carries the sense of a deliberate, reasoned, ongoing calculation. This is not passive daydreaming. It is an act of the will — a sustained, intentional direction of the mind’s attention. My brother’s habit of returning to Instagram — even through a browser, even after deleting the app — was not a small failure. It was a pattern of spiritual looking-back, a repeated choosing to see what the heart was already aching toward. And the Spirit was not condemning him for it; the Spirit was naming it, because what is named can be surrendered.

I want to honor something beautiful that emerged in the conversation even through the chaos of substances and grief and midnight profanity: my brother’s willingness to be honest. He did not perform spiritual composure. He did not pretend. He wept in his own way, raged in his own way, and then kept asking real questions. *“Do you ever feel like we’re psychos?”* That question, half-joking and half-terrified, is actually one of the most important questions a person on the threshold of genuine encounter with God can ask. Because yes — from the outside, the language of encountering holiness, of the Lord removing iniquity, of being clothed with rich garments, of manna that falls from heaven — it sounds like the language of madness. And the Scripture itself acknowledges this tension. The disciples were accused of being filled with new wine on the Day of Pentecost (Acts 2:13). Paul told Festus, *“I am not mad, most excellent Festus, but speak the words of truth and reason”* (Acts 26:25, NKJV). The encounter with the living God has always appeared as foolishness to those whose epistemological framework is bounded by the natural order. But as I said to my brother — and I mean it with everything in me — when you have genuinely stood in the presence of holiness, the question resolves itself. You do not argue yourself into certainty about God from that moment. You simply know, from depths of yourself you did not know existed, that what you encountered was not manufactured.

And this brings me to the phrase I want to close this meditation’s central unfolding with: the joy that is not the same as happiness. I said it to my brother in the conversation, and I want to say it again here with more fullness. I have joy. I do not always have happiness. There are seasons of weeping, of intercession so heavy it feels like carrying nations, of frustration and lament and the spiritual weariness of a long march through wilderness terrain. But beneath all of that — beneath the happiness and unhappiness alike — there is a settled, unshakeable thing that does not depend on circumstances. The Greek word for peace, εἰρήνη (eirēnē, ay-RAY-nay), carries in its background the Hebrew שָׁלוֹם (shalom, shah-LOME) — not merely the absence of conflict but the presence of wholeness, completeness, a life held together by covenant fidelity. This is the peace that *“surpasses all understanding”* (Philippians 4:7, NKJV). It cannot be reverse-engineered from the outside. It cannot be produced by substances or by the silencing of grief. It is only given by the One who is Himself the Prince of שָׁלוֹם (shah-LOME). And it is only received in the posture of surrender — not the surrender of resignation, but the surrender of trust: *His way is better.*

I said that to my brother near the end of the call, and I believe it with all that I am. His way is better. Not easier. Not faster. Not more immediately satisfying to the flesh. But better — better in the way that the Promised Land is better than Egypt, better in the way that resurrection is better than the long grief of the tomb, better in the way that being clothed in rich garments by the Lord Himself is better than dressing yourself in the approval of a comparison that will never finally settle.

## Implications and Challenges

The first and perhaps sharpest challenge this call reflects is the modern Christian habit of grieving without surrendering. We have learned, rightly, to allow ourselves to feel grief. We have learned, rightly, that suppression is not holiness. But somewhere in that healthy reclaiming of emotional honesty, a distortion crept in: the idea that feeling the grief is the same as processing it before God. My brother said, *“I feel like the only way through it is to feel it.”* And there is truth in that. But feeling it without bringing it to the Lord — without naming it before Him, without asking Him to rebuke the backward pull — is not healing. It is, in the language of the Scripture, like holding salt water in your eyes. My brother himself almost stumbled into the metaphor: I told him, *“You hold all your tears in, and you turn into a pillar of salt. You’ve got to get that salt water out.”* The grief must flow — but toward God, not back toward what was left behind. The modern Western grief culture has space for tears without the altar. Scripture insists both are necessary.

The second challenge is the one my brother named himself and perhaps didn’t fully realize he was naming: the idol of comparative identity. The rage he felt was not merely about the woman or the man. It was about having measured his worth by metrics he did not choose and cannot control — height, attractiveness, wealth. These are metrics the culture installs quietly, and the church has far too often left them unaddressed. Men in the Body of Christ are suffering under a weight of comparative shame that the prosperity-adjacent, image-conscious strains of modern Christianity actually amplify rather than heal. The gospel’s answer is not to tell men they are more attractive or wealthier than their rivals. The answer is to dismantle the entire measuring system and replace it with the identity that comes from being clothed by God Himself. That is a slower, deeper, more radical work. But it is the only one that actually holds.

Third, the conversation surfaces the ongoing pastoral reality of people using substances to access spiritual experience — and the seductive logic behind it. My brother made a sincere case: he felt more broadly spiritual on the joint than at any other time. And I want to handle this with both honesty and grace, because the experience of expanded perception is real — the substances do something to the mind’s gatekeeping mechanisms that produces what feels like openness. But this is precisely the deception: what feels like a door opened to the divine is more often a door opened to the unfiltered output of the self. True lamenting, I said to him, is not done through substances. And I stand by that. The Spirit does not require chemical assistance to reach the human soul; the moments when people believe they have encountered God through substances are often encounters with their own unguarded interiority — which can be profound, but should not be mistaken for revelation. The corrective is not shame. The corrective is exactly what I offered him: *“You cannot manipulate your way into a spiritual experience.”*

Fourth, there is a challenge here for those of us who serve in pastoral or fraternal roles. I was tired in that call. I was hungry. My phone or connection dropped multiple times. And yet the Spirit still worked. This is both an encouragement and a caution: the vessel does not have to be at its best for God to speak through it, but the vessel also must remain alert to when genuine discernment is needed versus when the conversation has drifted into territory where both parties are simply too depleted to press further into the deep things. There were moments in this call where I needed to say, *“Let’s pick this back up when you’re clearer.”* The grace to know when to press in and when to give space is itself a form of Spirit-led pastoral wisdom that does not come from technique but from proximity to God.

Finally, and perhaps most broadly: the double-mindedness my brother was living in — not fully departing from the old relationship, not yet fully embracing the season ahead — is not unique to him. It is the defining spiritual condition of a large portion of the Body of Christ in this hour. The church is full of people who have said the prayer, walked the aisle, been baptized, and yet remain in a chronic state of looking-back: back at former identities, former sins they half-miss, former relationships, former doctrines they found more comfortable. James 1:8 says the double-minded man is unstable in all his ways, tossed like a wave driven and blown by the wind. The invitation of the Spirit, in this conversation and in this hour, is not complexity but clarity: choose this day. Cross the threshold. Stop arguing with the temptation and surrender it. As soon as you surrender it, you stop wrestling with it.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

Spiritual maturity is not the absence of grief, anger, insecurity, or temptation. It is the consistent, practiced discipline of bringing all of those things to the threshold — to the Lord — rather than allowing them to turn the gaze backward. The mature believer has learned that the backward glance is always available, always seductive, and always costly. And they have also learned that the forward horizon, though it requires faith to look toward, is the only direction in which the Promised Land actually lies. We grow into this not by suppressing grief but by directing it: toward the altar, toward the One who can receive it, toward the God who promises that the mourning will be comforted (Matthew 5:4) and that weeping may endure for the night but joy comes in the morning (Psalm 30:5).

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

This call is a summons to the specific obedience of the threshold moment — the decision to not look back, not because the past was not real, not because the pain is not real, but because God has spoken a word of forward motion and obedience means trusting His word above our longing. For my brother, the specific obedience is straightforward: close the browser, bring the grief to God rather than to the screen, and ask the Lord in the name of Jesus to rebuke the pull of the backward gaze. For all who read this, the specific obedience may look different in form but is identical in substance: what has God called you to leave that you are still, in the hidden rooms of your heart, holding?

**For Prophetic Posture:**

The prophetic posture this meditation cultivates is the posture of a people who trust the God who provides in the wilderness — not despite the wilderness, but *within* it. The manna falls in the wilderness. The water comes from the rock in the wilderness. The cloud and the fire are visible in the wilderness. The prophetic posture is not the one that insists on reaching the Promised Land by sundown; it is the one that learns to see the miraculous provision already present in the season of formation. It is the posture that says: *I will praise Him in this* — not because this is easy, but because He is faithful, because His promises are sure, because the oil in the lamp is enough for the journey, and because the garments He has given me will not wear out before I arrive.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I come before You now as one who has stood at that threshold myself — who has heard the command to not look back, who has felt the pull of Egypt even after the sea closed behind me, who has had to learn again and again that Your provision in the wilderness is not lesser than what was left behind but is, in truth, a more miraculous expression of Your covenant faithfulness than anything the old season contained. I thank You for my brother, for his honesty, for the raw and unfiltered way he opened his heart even in the midst of substances and grief and confusion — because You, Lord, are not frightened by that. You are not diminished by the profanity of the broken-hearted. You are the God who met Jacob at the ford of the Jabbok, who named Peter from the boat, who called Gideon a mighty man of valor while he was hiding in a winepress. I ask, as one of Your servants standing between the heavens and the earth, that You would meet my brother at his specific threshold — that the pull backward would be rebuked by the power of the name of Jesus, that the garments of his identity would be settled in You alone, that the shame of the comparison would be swallowed up in the truth that You have removed his iniquity and clothed him with rich garments — not because of what he has accomplished, but because of what You have spoken. And Father, let this word go beyond this one conversation, beyond this one brother, into the ears of every soul in the Body of Christ who is standing at the doorway, one foot still reaching back toward the burning city. Let them hear it clearly: *Come out. Do not look back. The manna is already falling. The cloud is already moving. His way is better.* To You, Lord Jesus — the Pioneer and Perfecter of faith who, for the joy set before Him, endured the cross and despised the shame — be all the glory, honor, and power, now and into the ages of ages. Amen.
