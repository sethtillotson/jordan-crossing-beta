# 07-01 Call Reflection: Faithfulness, Discipleship, and Marketplace Stewardship **Title:** 07-01 at 17:32 — When the System Becomes the Scaffolding: Faithfulness, Leadership, and the Courage to Step Into the Jordan **Recorded:** 2026-07-01 17:32:37 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to this conversation unfold with my brother Samuel, something beneath the surface of the business talk kept pressing on me — the way ordinary labor, systems, and human formation carry the fingerprints of covenant whether we name them that way or not. There is a kind of faithfulness embedded in building something from scratch, in discipling a young man toward mastery, in choosing to do the unglamorous infrastructure work before the harvest ever arrives, and in the willingness to step into a flooded Jordan before the waters have given any indication they intend to part. What arrested me most was a line I dropped almost in passing — *“You’ve got to step into the Jordan before the waters are held back”* — because that is not a sales maxim; it is a word of faith that fell into the middle of a commerce conversation, and I believe the Spirit means for us to hear it as such. I am sitting with the question: what does it mean to be genuinely useful, genuinely faithful, and genuinely present in a season that does not yet look like what it is becoming? **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ is not exempt from the disciplines of faithful stewardship in the practical world — and that practical world is not spiritually neutral. The way we approach the building of systems, the training of those entrusted to us, the quiet unsexy infrastructure work of any endeavor, the integrity of our counsel to those we serve, and the posture with which we enter uncertain waters all reveal the deeper formation of our character before God. When the saints are careless with ordinary things — with craft, with clarity, with the slow foundational work of laying ground others will stand upon — they tend to be careless in the spiritual as well. What I witnessed in this conversation was a portrait of real-world discipleship, marketplace stewardship, and faith operating under pressure that carries theological weight precisely because it is so unadorned. Faithfulness in small things is never merely practical. It is always, in some sense, covenantal. And the God who parts rivers does so in response to the feet of those who are willing to get them wet. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural perspective, this conversation is a straightforward entrepreneurial strategy session between two business partners — one of whom, my brother Samuel, is clearly the more architecturally minded strategist, sketching out a consulting model built on referral commissions, high-ticket retainer structures, placement-first hiring, and appointment-setting infrastructure. The natural mind watching this conversation would see exactly what it appears to be on the surface: two entrepreneurs in the early and messy stages of building a company, discussing product offerings, technology infrastructure, employee management, leadership philosophy, and cost structures. It would celebrate the hustle, admire the clarity of the offer stack, affirm the psychological savvy in how Samuel is managing a new hire named Kane, and measure success in dollars per booked meeting, commission percentages, and month-over-month revenue growth. It would frame my own contribution as a technical asset being priced and deployed, frame Kane’s development in purely transactional terms, and celebrate the advisory model as smart positioning. The natural mind is not wrong that these things are happening. It simply cannot see through them to what they signify about stewardship, discipleship, the formation of character under the weight of real responsibility, and the kind of faith that moves before conditions look favorable. **The Limitation:** What the natural mind cannot perceive is that every season of building carries a hidden curriculum, and that curriculum is always about the person doing the building more than the thing being built. It has ears that register the word “Jordan” as motivational metaphor and move on. It cannot hear that a fleeting Scripture reference dropped mid-conversation by a man whose mind is simultaneously tracking business models and domestic interruptions is actually a kairos moment — a time-saturated word falling into a time-sensitive decision. The flesh looks at infrastructure and sees cost; the Spirit looks at the same infrastructure and sees the hidden conditions under which trust is established, character is forged, and genuine authority is later released. The flesh calculates the ROI of pouring yourself into a team member before it gives; the Spirit gives because giving is the nature of the One who gives. The natural mind maps the pipeline but not the Providence, sees the funnel but not the faith, and measures leadership by retention rates rather than by the depth of what was genuinely transferred. There is a dimension to this conversation — a depth beneath the depth — that only the Spirit can illuminate, and it is precisely into that depth that I now want to press. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I want to begin with the word I almost let pass. I said it mid-conversation, almost offhand, almost buried beneath talk of sales funnels and commission percentages: *“You’ve got to step into the Jordan before the waters are held back.”* That sentence arrested me later, because it is Joshua 3. It is the moment when the priests carrying the Ark of the Covenant — the very presence of God enshrined in acacia wood and gold — were commanded to walk forward until the soles of their feet touched the edge of the Jordan at flood stage. The text is precise and the precision matters: the Jordan was not receding. The miracle was not given and then entered; the miracle was released by the entering. The Kingdom of God does not operate on the principle of preparation before obedience — it operates on the principle of obedience as preparation. The tension between “I need more infrastructure before I launch” and “step in and watch God hold back the water” is not merely a business tension. It is the ancient tension between the calculating mind of Egypt and the trusting stride of a covenant people crossing on dry ground. And the Spirit was pressing it into the middle of our conversation not as decoration but as the governing word of the season. What strikes me as I sit with the Jordan image is that the priests were not empty-handed risk-takers stepping into flood waters on sheer willpower. They were carrying the Ark — the manifest weight of God’s presence enshrined among His people. The miracle at the Jordan was accomplished by carried presence, not by clever strategy or optimal timing. This matters for how I understand the work I am doing and what my brother and I are building together. If we are building in the Spirit, then what we carry into the work is not merely skill or network or strategic intelligence. We carry the רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — the Spirit, breath, and wind of God — the same presence that brooded over the face of the waters at creation and hovered over the Jordan as Israel crossed. That presence has a history of making impossible waters give way. The question is not whether we are capable. The question is whether we are carrying what the Lord has given us to carry, and whether we are willing to get our feet wet before the miracle is visible. As I listened to Samuel walk me through the tiered structure of what he is building — from entry-level setter packages all the way to a full internal revenue department — what I noticed was not just the business logic, which is genuinely sound, but the shape of the thing. He is constructing a ladder. And ladders, in the scriptural imagination, are never incidental. Jacob’s ladder was not a metaphor for ambition; it was the revelation that the ordinary ground of a man’s sleeping place can become the very house of God, the gate of heaven — בֵּית אֱלֹהִים (beit Elohim, BAYT eh-lo-HEEM) — as Genesis 28:17 declares. I am not suggesting that Samuel’s sales platform is Bethel. I am saying that the Spirit does not partition life so cleanly as we do, and that the instinct to build something that allows people to climb — to enter at one level and ascend to the next through faithfulness and developed capacity — carries an echo of a design principle that runs through the entire canon. The structure rewards demonstrated faithfulness with expanded responsibility. This is not accidental. It is woven into the created order. The conversation about Kane arrested me perhaps more than any other portion of what I heard. My brother is doing something that, when I strip away the business language, looks remarkably like mentorship in its most intentional form. He has identified a young man, placed him inside a defined boundary of focus, given him repetitive foundational work, protected him from the distraction of every shiny alternative, and told him plainly: prove yourself faithful here, and I will expand your territory. This is the Parable of the Talents dressed in contemporary clothes — τάλαντον (talanton, TAH-lan-ton) in Matthew 25:14–30 — where the one who is given five talents is not told to go explore all the possible investment strategies available in the ancient near east, but is trusted with something specific and expected to demonstrate faithfulness within that specific assignment. The increase comes from the fidelity, not from the diversification. And the one who buries the talent — who hedges, who waits, who neither risks nor builds — loses even what he was given. Something in me recognizes the wisdom of what Samuel is doing with the noise-to-signal language he is using with Kane. The young man is surrounded by the loud competing voices of every online entrepreneurial path imaginable — every social platform screaming that visible fast success is available to anyone with a camera and a willingness to hustle. Samuel is essentially doing what a good father does: he is narrowing the field of vision not to impoverish his son but to protect him from the paralysis of infinite options long enough to build a real skill in a real domain. The Hebrew word that surfaces for me here is אָמַן (aman, ah-MAHN) — to be firm, to be established, to be faithful or trustworthy — the root from which we receive the word “Amen.” Faithfulness is not the opposite of ambition. It is the ground from which genuine authority grows. Kane is being trained toward אָמַן (ah-MAHN) before he is released toward leadership, and that sequence is not arbitrary — it is the sequence of the Kingdom. I am also sitting with something that Samuel said almost in passing — that leadership always has to be a hundred percent, even “crash and burn.” He was talking about sales teams and commission-chasing employees, but what landed in my spirit was the unmistakable fingerprint of a Kingdom principle embedded in the created order of human relationship: you cannot produce depth in another person with shallow investment. The Spirit pressed me here toward Matthew 13 and the parable of the sower. The sower does not audit the soil before he throws the seed. He goes out and he sows — on the path, among the rocks, amid the thorns, and on good ground. The return varies wildly. Some seed yields nothing. Some yields a hundredfold. But the sower’s posture does not change between casts. The pattern Samuel is describing — give your full leadership, build the pipeline beneath the current person, and trust that when one walks away there is someone ready — is functionally identical. You do not withhold the seed because the last field was rocky. The hundred-percent posture is not recklessness. It is trained trust in the sovereignty of the One who raises up laborers for the harvest. When Samuel said, also almost in passing, that he was fine with the possibility that Kane might one day walk away and do his own thing — that peace, the peace of holding people loosely while pouring into them fully — arrested me as something not natural but cultivated. ἀγάπη (agapē, ah-GAH-pay), the covenantal love Paul describes in 1 Corinthians 13:5, “does not seek its own.” The leader who can train someone up, give his hundred percent, and then release that person to their own calling without resentment or collapse has understood something about covenantal generosity that most institutions — including many local churches — have not. You do not own the people in your care. You steward them for a season. And the measure of your leadership is not how long they stayed but how ready they were when they left. This is not business philosophy. This is the shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep and does not hold the sacrifice against them when they scatter. I pressed gently in the conversation toward the question of whether Samuel is managing Kane or genuinely giving him something of himself. Samuel heard me begin to go there, and the conversation shifted before we could fully unpack it. But the instinct was right, and I want to linger here because it is the deepest implication. The Apostle Paul makes the distinction explicit in his letter to the Thessalonians: “We were gentle among you, just as a nursing mother cherishes her own children. So, affectionately longing for you, we were well pleased to impart to you not only the gospel of God, but also our own lives, because you had become dear to us” (1 Thessalonians 2:7–8). The word for “cherishes” is θάλπω (thalpō, THAL-po) — to warm, to brood over, to keep gently warm with body heat. It is the image of total, unhurried investment in the fragile life of another. Samuel doesn’t yet have the full theological vocabulary for what he is describing when he says he “develops” people, that it is guidance and alignment but also something like friendship. But the Holy Spirit recognizes the shape of it. There is a mode of leadership that gives people opportunity and pays them well but never actually gives them the leader’s own life. Paul distinguishes between those two things. The gospel-shaped leader gives both. What I find pressing in the advisory model Samuel described — where the consultant earns commission by connecting clients to solutions rather than directly selling services — is the integrity architecture that this model requires. It only works if the diagnosis is honest. If he steers a client toward a vendor not because it is the right fit but because the commission is higher, the model collapses — and worse, the client is harmed. The Apostle Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 2:17 that he is not, “as so many, peddling the word of God; but as of sincerity, as from God, we speak in the sight of God in Christ.” The word for “peddling” is καπηλεύοντες (kapēleuontes, kah-pay-LOO-on-tes) — the image of a tavern-keeper who waters down the wine for profit. The minister of the Word, like the true advisor in the marketplace, must be one whose counsel is not diluted by personal gain. Samuel himself flagged this implicitly when he said he needed to tell the Thailand clients not to settle for cheap solutions when they have the capital to invest in what actually works. That is prophetic clarity — the willingness to tell a harder truth because the relationship is built on genuine care for the other’s outcome. The Hebrew word תָּמִים (tamim, TAH-meem) — complete, whole, without defect — is the word Scripture uses for integrity, and it is worth noting that integrity in this sense is not merely moral uprightness; it is wholeness, the absence of a gap between what is built and what is presented. Show them the infrastructure, not just the pitch. Let the substance exceed the presentation. The conversation about what I would need to build — the backend infrastructure, the multitenancy, the authorization layers, the Stripe integration, the SQL queries — was simultaneously a practical exchange and, underneath it, a small parable about the invisibility of foundational work. Nobody celebrates the server. Nobody frames the database schema and hangs it on the wall. The beautiful front end gets the attention; the infrastructure that makes it function never receives the credit. But without the backend, the frontend is theater. I am thinking of the intercessors who hold up arms in the valley while the battle plays out on the mountain in Exodus 17, the unnamed saints who pray and fast and lay foundations that visible builders will later stand upon without remembering who prepared the ground. Samuel said plainly that these systems are “out of his range” and that he hates to admit it but it is true. That confession — honest, undefended — is its own form of faithfulness. Knowing what you are not is as important as knowing what you are. And Paul tells the Corinthians that the members of the body that seem less honorable receive greater honor, and that God has so arranged the σῶμα (sōma, SOH-mah) — the body, the assembled whole — that there should be no division (1 Corinthians 12:23–25). The infrastructure builder is the body member who rarely gets the celebration. But without them, the visible body cannot function. There is a moment in the conversation where Samuel said plainly, “I’ve got faith.” That phrase, spoken in the middle of a negotiation about hosting costs and AI-assisted code generation, carries more freight than the conversational register acknowledges. Faith in this context means willingness to commit resources before certainty of outcome, extending trust in the direction of a person’s demonstrated character rather than waiting for guaranteed returns. That is, in the language of the writer of Hebrews, the very definition: πίστις (pistis, PIS-tis) — the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not yet seen (Hebrews 11:1). Samuel’s πίστις (PIS-tis) in this context is not theological in the formal sense, but it is structurally identical to the posture Scripture commends. He is acting in advance of certainty because he has assessed the character of the person and the nature of the work and decided to commit. That is how genuine partnerships are born. That is how the Kingdom advances. I want to sit for a moment with my own contribution in this conversation, because it is easy for me to undervalue it — and I told Samuel exactly that, that I felt like I had nothing to work on, that the time was being wasted. Samuel’s response was immediate and clear: he is willing to sacrifice saved revenue just to get me building this infrastructure, because without it he cannot sell anything else. I heard that. And I want to receive it not as flattery but as honest recognition that genuine skill — particularly the kind that others cannot replicate without years of investment — carries real weight in the economy of building. The modern Western Christian often has a distorted relationship with worth — either inflating it through self-promotional performance or depreciating it through false humility. True humility is not the belief that I have little to offer. True humility is the accurate assessment of what I carry and the willingness to offer it in service without demanding recognition. I am being invited to stand in that more accurate posture. There is also something worth noting in the brief domestic interruption mid-call — I stepped away to attend to my wife. Samuel’s response was to call me back to the business conversation. My response was to go to my wife first. This is small, but it is not nothing. A man who governs his household well is a man the Spirit can trust with larger territory. The order of priority — covenant before commerce — is not a distraction from the work. It is the qualification for it. “He who is faithful in what is least is faithful also in much” (Luke 16:10). The willingness to pause a promising business call to tend to the covenant of marriage is itself a form of obedience that the natural mind would call inefficient and the Spirit would call foundational. You do not outsource your marriage to pursue your market and expect the Spirit to honor that trade. Finally, I am drawn to the sentence I spoke at the close of the conversation, observing the pattern of the call itself: *“Execute. Revision. Adjust. Execute again.”* This was not merely frustration with a dropped connection. This is the rhythm of faith in real time. The ancient Greek verb ζωοποιέω (zōopoieō, ZOH-oh-poy-EH-oh) — to quicken, to make alive — is used throughout the New Testament for the life-giving work of the Spirit. A “quickened word” is a word that comes alive, a kairos moment, a Spirit-timed utterance. And what I observed is that at the precise moment when something living breaks through into a conversation, resistance comes. Disconnection. Interruption. The enemy of revelation is not ignorance — it is disruption timed to the moment of breakthrough. But the response of a mature builder is not despair or extended spiritual lament. It is Nehemiah with a trowel in one hand and a sword in the other. Execute. Revise. Adjust. Execute again. The Spirit of God is not derailed by a dropped call. He simply awaits the callback. ## Implications and Challenges This meditation carries a grace that must be acknowledged first: Kingdom principles are not quarantined inside church walls. God’s wisdom about people, stewardship, covenant, and leadership bleeds through into the marketplace through the consciences of those He is forming, even when they do not yet have the full scriptural vocabulary for it. This should produce humility in us, not pride — a readiness to recognize the shape of Kingdom truth wherever it surfaces, and to honor it there without requiring it to wear Sunday clothes before we acknowledge its weight. The conversation about Kane and the noise-to-signal ratio carries a direct implication for discipleship in the church. We live in an era of infinite spiritual content — podcasts, reels, sermons, prophetic voices, revival reports — and the sheer volume creates a generation of believers who are perpetually stimulated but rarely formed. Samuel’s instinct to narrow the focus, to say “master this one thing before you reach for the next,” is a corrective to the restless spiritual consumerism that passes for discipleship in many contemporary environments. Formation requires sustained attention within a bounded field of faithfulness. The saints who cannot focus long enough to master one domain of their calling will remain perpetually excited and perpetually shallow. אָמַן (ah-MAHN) — the kind of faithfulness that produces genuine authority — is never the fruit of endless variety. It is the fruit of sustained fidelity. The ethics of how we frame opportunity to those under our influence deserve sustained reflection. Samuel is aware — and I pressed into this — that framing and psychology are at work in how he has positioned Kane’s onboarding. This is not inherently wrong; all communication involves framing. But the distinction between framing that serves the leader’s interest and formation that genuinely serves the person being formed is not always obvious, and it requires ongoing examination. The scriptural test is not whether the framing benefits the leader but whether it genuinely advances the flourishing and maturity of the one being formed. Paul operated with radical transparency in his relationships, saying in 2 Corinthians 1:12 that his conduct in the world was “in simplicity and godly sincerity, not with fleshly wisdom.” The question worth holding is: would I be comfortable if this person could see my full internal reasoning? Am I building with this person, or am I building through them? There is a difference, and the one being formed can eventually feel it. The challenge embedded in the commission model is one worth naming clearly rather than spiritually papering over. Any advisory structure that depends on advisor integrity will be tested precisely at the points where personal gain is highest — the moment when the higher-commission vendor is not the better-fit vendor, when the honest diagnosis costs more than the comfortable one. The temptation is real, quiet, and dressed as pragmatism. When our counsel is shaped more by our commission than by our client’s genuine need, we have become the very thing the world despises about salespeople, and worse, we have preached a false gospel with our professional life. The integrity of our counsel is our witness, not merely our ethics. The church’s current energy flows disproportionately toward the visible and the celebrated — the platform, the audience, the public ministry expression — while the structural and intercessory and administrative work that makes visible ministry possible goes chronically underresourced and undervalued. The backend of the Kingdom deserves better than the status of support staff for the real ministers. The saints who build the systems, handle the finances, maintain the relational infrastructure of a community — these are the body members Paul says receive the greater honor precisely because they receive the lesser recognition. The eye cannot say to the hand, “I have no need of you” (1 Corinthians 12:21). And yet in practice, much of what we call Christian community has made exactly that statement through the allocation of honor, attention, and resources. There is a caution embedded here for the believer who mistakes endless preparation for faithful obedience. Samuel is not waiting for the perfect training video before he launches his placement program. He is selling on placement, building as he goes, trusting that the infrastructure will be legible when the clients need to see it. This is not irresponsibility — this is the Jordan River posture. The waters do not part before you step in. They part because you step in. The Spirit is pressing: how many things has He been calling us to begin, that we have held back under the banner of “not yet ready”? Discern the difference between the door the Spirit has closed and the connection that simply dropped because of a poor signal. And then make the callback. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This conversation calls me — and us — toward a more grounded and less romantic understanding of what it means to be useful in a season of building. Maturity is demonstrated in the willingness to build infrastructure that no one will applaud, to confess the limits of our competence without shame, to receive our own value without either inflating or dismissing it, to hold people loosely while pouring into them fully, and to remain focused on the assignment in front of us rather than scattering after every adjacent opportunity. It also means developing what might be called integrated attentiveness — the capacity to remain spiritually awake in the middle of thoroughly practical engagement, to hear a kairos word in the middle of a business call, to recognize the prophetic shape of a Jordan River analogy, and to carry the presence of God consciously into the marketplace without compartmentalizing. Nothing is secular. Every domain is a field of the Spirit’s activity, and we are called to have ears to hear it. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The specific obedience this meditation calls forth is twofold: the obedience of the present task — whatever the Lord has placed in our hands, to do it with full diligence and theological seriousness, knowing that faithfulness in the small foundational work is not a stepping stone to the real work but is the real work — and the obedience of entry before certainty. Whatever Jordan-stage body of water stands between where we are and where He has spoken, the call is to step in. Not recklessly, not without the Ark and the presence and the covenant covering, but to step. To stop rehearsing the water temperature and to actually place the sole of a priestly foot on the edge of the impossible, trusting that the God who parted the waters for Joshua will part them again for those who carry His רוּחַ (ROO-akh) into their assignment. **For Prophetic Posture:** Those of us who are learning to move prophetically in our generation must learn to hold revelation without either forcing it prematurely or burying it under practicality. The prophetic posture this conversation models is one of embedded witness — dropping a word of faith into a commerce conversation not as performance but as genuine overflow of what we carry, remaining awake in the mundane long enough to recognize the Kingdom principle embedded in the commission conversation, the coaching relationship, the dropped call. God is always speaking. The question is whether we are paying the kind of attention that can hear Him through a conversation about SQL queries and sales funnels. The prophetic community does not only speak into the extraordinary. It discerns the extraordinary hidden within the ordinary and names it with reverence, clarity, and the full weight of what it actually is. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as Your servant, as one who stands between what is and what You have declared shall be, and I make agreement with Your will over the work that has been laid in these hands. You are the One who parts waters when feet of covenant obedience touch their edge, who prepares the ground before the harvest, who lays the foundation before the house can stand, and who forms the man before expanding the territory. I thank You for this season of building — for the backend work that no audience will celebrate, for the skills deposited in me that carry real value even when I cannot feel it, for the brothers You have placed around me who speak clarity over me when I have lost my bearings, and for the Jordan-word You hid inside an ordinary conversation about commission structures and dropped connections. I ask You to cover the work we are building together — that the systems would serve righteousness and not merely commerce, that the young men being formed in this season would be formed toward genuine character and not merely productivity, and that every relationship in this circle would be marked by the θάλπω (THAL-po) warmth that Paul described rather than the mere exchange of mutual benefit. Guard us from using each other and from diluting our counsel for personal gain. Make us people who give our full hundred percent with open hands, who build with integrity rather than angle, and who carry Your presence into every room we enter as the priests carried the Ark into the flood-stage Jordan — not because the conditions were favorable, but because You said go. Let every quickened word find its mark. Let every dropped connection be reestablished in Your timing. And let the evidence of Your Kingdom — righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit — be visible in the way we work, the way we speak, the way we keep covenant, and the way we step in before the waters have given us any reason to believe they will part. To You be all glory in the visible and in the invisible, in the celebrated and in the hidden, in the harvest season and in the long quiet days of building that precede it. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Joshua 3:13–17 | — | The Jordan parted only after the priests’ feet touched the water — obedience precedes the miracle, and carried presence is what releases the impossible | | Genesis 28:17 — “This is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven!” | בֵּית אֱלֹהִים (beit Elohim, BAYT eh-lo-HEEM) — house of God | Ordinary ground becomes sacred threshold;
