# 07-07 Call Reflection: Covenant Brotherhood, Spiritual Warfare, and Romans 8 **Title:** 07-07 at 18:40 — The Rod, the Well, and the Covenant That Holds Through the Storm **Recorded:** 2026-07-07 18:40:43 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I was in Proverbs 23 when my brother called — not vaguely in Scripture, but in that specific chapter, pressing through the vivid portrait of the drunkard, the woe, the wine sparkling in the cup, the serpent that bites at the last. What unfolded across the next hours was one of the most layered conversations I can remember: a faith crisis, a business dispute, a spiritual attack named out loud, a sermon found at the right moment, an unmarked page discovered in the middle of a chapter I have read many times, and two men standing in covenant against a pressure neither of us manufactured alone. By the time the call ended, I was sitting with the settled weight of something I can only describe as the Lord’s sovereign preparation — the Word already open before the test arrived, the passage reserved, the covenant holding where neither of us could have held it on our own. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ is saturated with men and women who are simultaneously reaching for Scripture and reaching for something that deadens the ache — not only literally, but spiritually. They sit in Bible studies and feel the worst of themselves rise to the surface. They build Kingdom businesses with covenant brothers and find the enemy sliding in through legitimate grievance. They hear Romans 8 for the first time and are undone by its weight. They disclose that the devil spoke to them by name, and they need a brother who will not pathologize the disclosure or perform polite deflection. What happened on this call is not merely a story about two imperfect men navigating a hard evening. It is a living demonstration of what the Spirit produces in those who abide — the Word arriving ahead of the need, the covenant holding in the storm, the glory set against the suffering in such a way that the suffering loses its power to be the final word. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind looking at this conversation would see several overlapping situations, each with its own clinical frame. It would see a young man having a rough night — embarrassed by impulsive messages sent from a bar, emotionally raw from a Bible study that surfaced unwanted interior noise, possibly still nursing the grief of a former relationship, venting to a sober friend who happened to be reading Proverbs at the right moment. It would see a textbook business dispute between two partners who failed to formalize expectations clearly before entering a shared financial arrangement, with one man under fresh domestic pressure and the other operating from a financial anxiety shaped by history he carries. It would see stress-induced spiritual language — a man saying the enemy spoke to him by name, which a natural framework would diagnose as projection or dissociative religious metaphor. And it would see a pleasant coincidence in the unmarked page of Romans 8, framed as confirmation bias meeting emotional need. The prescriptions would be sensible: rest, hydration, clearer written partnership agreements, emotional regulation tools, professional support for intrusive thoughts during prayer, and perhaps better communication habits around financial terminology. **The Limitation:** What the natural mind simply cannot perceive is the protagonist behind the convergence. It can name the emotions but not what is feeding them. It can observe behavior but cannot see the spiritual layer beneath behavior. When my brother named a spirit pressing against him — when he said he felt the wrath of something rising in him, attaching to the wounds of his life, arriving precisely on a night when faith was beginning to stir again — the natural framework reaches for a diagnostic category. The Spirit-trained mind reaches for Ephesians 6 and the armor that was already waiting. When the sermon on Romans 8 was found, and the very same passage was sitting in my Bible untouched amid a chapter otherwise full of my markings, the natural eye calls it coincidence. What the natural eye cannot perceive is that the God who inspired Romans 8 is the same Spirit who inhabits the believer, who abides in the keeping of specific pages for specific evenings, who plants the Word in the servant’s mouth before the test arrives, and who does not send His own into battle unarmed. Reason can explain nearly everything that happened on this call as psychology and circumstance. What reason cannot explain is why the preparation was already complete before the need was known. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I want to begin where the Spirit had me before the phone rang. I was in Proverbs 23 — not browsing, not scanning, but pressing slowly through the father’s words to the son, feeling the particular gravity of verses 29 through 35. *Who has woe? Who has sorrow? Who has contentions? Who has complaints? Who has wounds without cause? Who has redness of eyes? Those who linger long at the wine.* What the Spirit was pressing into me in that quiet hour was not moralism about alcohol. It was a diagnostic question about the heart — about what we turn to when the ache is too loud, and what the turning costs us at the last. The passage ends with the drunkard waking and immediately planning his return to the cup: *“When shall I awake, that I may seek another drink?”* And Solomon’s portrait is not cruel — it is almost tender in its sadness — because the man in this passage does not know he is in the portrait. He genuinely does not feel the blows. He says *they struck me, but I was not hurt.* The blindness is the condition, not a symptom of it. When my brother called, the woe and the sorrow were already present in his voice before I knew what the call was about. He had been at a Bible study — a room full of genuine believers, prayers being lifted, Scripture being opened — and what had risen in him during that time was not peace but the full interior inventory he described with startling honesty: anger, lust, greed, intrusive thoughts flooding in precisely when holiness was being invoked. He was shaken by this. He asked whether this was just him — whether everyone else in that room was somehow cleaner on the inside. And I told him what I believe is simply true: no. That is not just you. What my brother described is well-attested in the interior history of the saints across fifteen centuries of the Church’s reflection. The closer a soul gets to the light, the more visible the shadow becomes. The flesh does not advertise its corruption until something holy begins to press against it. When the Spirit moves over the waters, the chaos beneath the surface becomes visible. My brother mistook the exposure of corruption for the arrival of corruption — but the corruption was already there. The light simply revealed it, and that revelation, rightly understood, is mercy, not condemnation. The Proverbs 23 passage I was holding opens with instruction: *“Apply your heart to instruction, and your ears to words of knowledge”* (v. 12). The Hebrew לֵב (lev, LEV) — heart — encompasses the whole inner person: intellect, will, and emotion. The father is not speaking to a son who has failed and is being corrected as punishment. He is speaking to a son who is still being formed, still learning to apply himself to the instruction that will make him wise. And this is what I saw in my brother that night: not a man who had abandoned faith, but a man whose heart was in the process of being formed — and who had enough honesty to say so aloud, which is itself a sign of formation. A heart that has hardened does not grieve its own condition. A heart still soft enough to be troubled by what it finds inside itself is a heart the Spirit has not abandoned. The wine conversation did not stay in the realm of Proverbs alone, and I felt the Spirit drawing a line between the text in my hands and the invitation waiting in the Gospel. Solomon’s warning ends with the drunkard seeking another drink upon waking. But Jesus in John 4 reframes the entire grammar of thirst. He tells the woman at the well: *“Whoever drinks of this water will thirst again, but whoever drinks of the water that I shall give him will never thirst”* (John 4:13–14, NKJV). The ὕδωρ ζῶν (hudor zon, HOO-dor ZONE) — living water — is not a metaphor for emotional wellness. It is the indwelling Spirit as inexhaustible source, the only supply that addresses the thirst rather than temporarily satisfying it. The addiction and the worship are structurally identical: both are thirsts seeking a source. The only question Proverbs and John together are pressing is which source the thirst gets turned toward. I said to my brother: *Why are you still playing in the mud?* Not as condemnation — as the next question the Proverbs father would ask. You know the love that is coming. You know what the wine costs at the last. So when you awake, what will you seek? Then the conversation shifted, and what I can only describe as the leaven of Herod began to work in our exchange. We moved into business — net revenue, profit margins, financial terms — and the tension was real. He was pressing for a reinvestment that would have delayed payment to me in a season where, with a child on the way, I needed to hold the line on provision. Jesus issued His warning in Mark 8:15 about the ζύμη (zymē, ZOO-may) — leaven — of the Pharisees and of Herod. He gave that warning immediately after the disciples were arguing about literal bread on a boat, immediately after two miraculous feedings they had not yet understood. The leaven of the Pharisees is the leaven of performative unbelief — the appearance of faith without the substance of trust. The leaven of Herod is the leaven of worldly fear, the instinct to protect position at the expense of covenant. And as I sit with that call now, I can see both leavens were working in both of us. The fear about provision, the hardening around money, the impulse to protect myself by pointing to what was agreed upon — that leaven was in me too. And my brother felt it. The conversation sharpened until we were both pressing against something that was not the other person. What I noticed was that I had already been given the framework for the test before the test arrived. Proverbs 24:5–6 says: *“A wise man is strong, yes, a man of knowledge increases strength; for by wise counsel you will wage your own war, and in a multitude of counselors there is safety.”* The Hebrew תַּחְבֻּלוֹת (tachbulot, takh-boo-LOHT) — wise counsel — carries the image of a ship’s steering ropes: counsel is navigational, not decorative. The Word I had been given before the call turned out to be precisely the Word the moment required. But what struck me equally was the declaration of what I said in response to the business argument: *We are stewards.* That word — οἰκονόμος (oikonomos, oy-kon-OH-mos) in the Greek, the household manager who holds authority over what belongs to another — is not merely a sound financial principle. It is a Kingdom theology of money. Jesus Himself said in Luke 16:11 that faithfulness in unrighteous mammon and trustworthiness with true riches are not separate examinations. Liabilities come first. What I owe, I pay. What is not mine, I do not spend. This is not financial discipline abstracted from faith; it is an expression of the character of the God I represent. Then the call cracked open into something else entirely. My brother told me the devil had spoken to him that night — by name — and had made declarations: separation, fear, death. And something in me did not flinch. I did not reach for a therapeutic reframe. I stood. And what I stood on was Romans 8:38–39: *“For I am persuaded that neither death nor life, nor angels nor principalities nor powers, nor things present nor things to come, nor height nor depth, nor any other created thing, shall be able to separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord.”* The enemy’s threat, measured against that text, is not merely insufficient — it is absurd in the most literal sense: ab-surd, away from sound, disconnected from reality. Because the reality in which we live as covenant children is a reality in which the separation the enemy promises has already been ruled impossible by the One who holds the covenant together. What moved me to my core was what I said next, because it was not calculated: *What makes me tremble is knowing that my God hears that, trembling for them.* The יִרְאַת יְהוָה (yir’at YHWH, yeer-AHT yeh-HOH-vah) — the fear of the Lord — is not the same animal as fear of the enemy. To fear God is to stand correctly oriented. But I found myself in that moment trembling *for* the powers of darkness — aware, with a sudden weight I did not manufacture, of what it means for a defeated creature to make declarations against the children of God while God Himself is listening. Psalm 2:4 says: *“He who sits in the heavens shall laugh; the Lord shall hold them in derision.”* The enemy’s bold threats are not received by God as threatening. They are received as the declarations of something that has already lost. And the Greek perfect tense of John 16:33 — νενίκηκα (nenikēka, neh-NEE-kay-kah) — *I have overcome* — is the grammar of completed action with ongoing present effect. Jesus has overcome. The enemy operates in a post-defeat condition. Every activity of the adversary in this present age is the activity of a legal trespasser who knows his time is short. I declared over my brother in that moment that no unclean thing has rightful dwelling in a covenant son of God — that the name and blood and covering of the Lord Jesus Christ are not a ritual formula but an invocation of the full authority, nature, and covenantal standing of the One before whom every knee bows, as Philippians 2:10 declares. I said: it will not be diminished; it will be extinguished. And then something happened that I want to be honest about, because it was real and I do not want to flatten it into religious language: I felt the heavens move. I said on the call — almost echoing Paul’s language from Romans 9:1 — *I tell the truth in Christ. I am not lying. My conscience also bearing me witness in the Holy Spirit.* The Spirit bears witness with our spirit (Romans 8:16), and when that witnessing occurs in the middle of prayer, it is not sentiment. It is confirmation. Something in the room of that call shifted, and we both felt it. In the second portion of the call, my brother found a sermon on Romans 8:18, and he described hearing it with a freshness that only comes when a passage lands for the first time on a heart that is finally ready. He called me while it was still resonating. I turned to that same verse in my Bible — the one that sits at the pivot of the chapter, where Paul moves from the indwelling Spirit to the cosmic scope of redemption — and found it entirely unmarked. The entire chapter surrounding it glows with my notes and underlining. That single verse had been preserved untouched. I am still sitting with that. The Spirit does not waste these moments. A passage held in a state of untouched readiness is not an oversight. It is a keeping. Paul’s word in Romans 8:18 is λογίζομαι (logizomai, loh-GEE-dzoh-my) — to reckon, to calculate, to credit to an account. This is the language of a ledger. Paul is not offering wishful comfort. He is making a reasoned declaration from inside the affliction he is describing: the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us. The suffering is real. The glory is simply incomparable by weight. And then the passage opens into something cosmological — verses 19 through 22 reveal that κτίσις (ktisis, KTEE-sis), the entire created order, groans and labors in birth pangs, waiting for the revelation of the sons of God. This is not a personal pep talk. This is the entire created order positioned as a participant in the drama of suffering and glory, and what creation is waiting for is the children of God entering the fullness of their inheritance. The labor is not incidental. It is the birth canal of the new creation. My brother received this with wonder — *What a gift that I’ve been invited into that* — and I felt that response was exactly right. Not entitlement. Not spiritual consumerism. Just wonder. Because the invitation into the fellowship of Christ’s sufferings is simultaneously the invitation into the fellowship of His glory, and Romans 8:17 makes the condition explicit: joint heirs with Christ, *if indeed we suffer with Him, that we may also be glorified together.* The golden chain of verses 29 through 30 carries this into its most stunning form: those He foreknew, He predestined; those He predestined, He called; those He called, He justified; those He justified, He glorified — ἐδόξασεν (edoksasen, eh-DOH-ksa-sen), aorist tense, past, accomplished. The glorification of the called is already done in the purposes of God. We walk through suffering in time, but we are already glorified in Christ outside of time. This is not triumphalism. This is the anchor that makes suffering endurable without requiring it to be denied. My brother had also been wrestling with something about forgiveness — with the sheer breadth of human darkness, and with how God could judge righteously a species so thoroughly confused about itself. He said: *The only way You could judge us righteously would be to know each other.* And then he remembered the cross. He thought about Pontius Pilate — could even a man who participated in the execution of the Son of God be forgiven? And he anchored it in Jesus’ own words: *Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do* (Luke 23:34). The ἄφες (aphes, AH-fes) there is an aorist imperative — Christ issuing forgiveness as a command to the Father, in real time, from the cross, for those actively executing Him. This is not retrospective pardon. This is active priestly intercession at the moment of maximum injustice. Paul echoes this in 1 Corinthians 2:8 — had the ἄρχοντες τοῦ αἰῶνος τούτου (archontes tou aiōnos toutou, ar-KHON-tes too eye-OH-nos TOO-too) — the rulers of this present age — known, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory. The blindness was real. Into that blindness, grace moved. The cross does not trivialize injustice. It absorbs it, and transforms it into the instrument of redemption. My brother also told me something that I want to preserve, because it is the kind of testimony that the Lord gives not for public consumption but for the formation of faith in the one who receives it — what I recognized immediately as Gilgal theology. He described the consistent, small, personal confirmations of God’s presence in his daily life: specific attentive signs that only a Father who knows the particular interior of His child could provide. A robin prayed for in a quiet moment, appearing exactly. He said: *I have nothing but evidence — not for YouTube or Twitter — but evidence enough for a man to dedicate his life to it.* The stones the people pulled from the Jordan at Gilgal in Joshua 4 were not monuments to human achievement. They were witnesses to divine action, set up so that when children asked what the stones meant, the answer was already prepared: the Lord was here. He did this. We saw it. Personal testimony is Gilgal theology — the stacking of memorial stones, not to impress, but to establish a record of God’s fidelity for the one who must walk forward in faith when the feeling fades and the evidence of the moment has grown quiet. Near the end of the call, after the spirit had been addressed and the covenant had spoken louder than the threat, my brother made a pivot that stopped me. He stopped talking about money entirely. He said: *Our brotherhood was not started by agreement but by disagreement. Every truth you have ever shown me has come through prayer. I need help, not an enemy.* That is not the voice of a man still under the influence of what had been pressing him. That is the voice of a man who had just experienced something real — deliverance through the name of Christ, the ministry of the Word spoken in real time, and the activation of genuine covenant love. The enemy lost his grip in that moment. And what was left was achingly beautiful: two brothers quoting Proverbs over a phone call, speaking of dying for friends, reminding each other who holds the covenant together. What I said then — and meant entirely — is this: *The one who holds our covenant together overcame the world.* That is not sentiment. That is the most stable ground there is. And it is not durable because of the quality of the men standing in it. It is durable because the One who initiated it is incapable of losing. Before the call ended, my brother told me something I had not expected: that at his Bible study that night, in the middle of his own unraveling, he had prayed aloud for me — that I would be the best father I could be to my son Elijah. He told his whole church. He said it was an honor to watch from the outside and to be the friend who could say *it’s going to be okay* when the weight of what is coming presses. Here was a man at his lowest — faith shaking, thoughts during prayer frightening him, his own inner walls under repair — and he spent part of his Bible study lifting me before God. That is not the behavior of a faithless man. That is the behavior of a man whose love is more real than his doubt. James 5:16 says the effective, fervent prayer of a righteous man avails much — and righteous here does not mean sinless. It means aligned. Samuel, in his love for me and for my son, was aligned with something real in that room. I carry that prayer. And I said it on the call: *I felt the heavens move.* I still feel it now. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this call surfaces is the performance of holiness in communal settings. My brother felt worse in a Bible study than he would have felt alone, because the group environment activated comparison rather than communion — a kind of ambient spiritual competition where it felt safer to seem clean than to bring the full weight of interior struggle. This is a structural failure in how we often build Christian community. If we want men and women to experience genuine formation rather than skilled performance, we need communities trained to receive darkness without flinching — where the honest confession of interior chaos is met with recognition rather than correction: *we are all here because we are all broken, and the light we are gathering around is not ours.* The second challenge is the tendency to treat intrusive thoughts during prayer as disqualifying. The Desert Fathers catalogued this exact phenomenon fifteen centuries ago. John Climacus wrote of thoughts that arise precisely at the threshold of prayer — because the adversary knows the threshold is where the most important business happens. The man who is too far from prayer to be interrupted is in far more danger than the man who shows up and finds his mind under assault. The thoughts that surface during prayer are not evidence of unfitness. They are evidence of proximity to something real. The enemy does not press against men who pose no threat. The third challenge sits at the intersection of covenant and commerce. This call demonstrates that the sacred and the secular are not separable domains. The same Spirit who governed the spiritual warfare in this conversation was already at work in the financial exchange that preceded it. When we say *we are stewards*, we are making a theological statement with real economic implications — implications that include precision in language, the payment of what is owed, the refusal to spend what belongs to another, and the submission of every financial decision to the Lord of the household. The church has too often permitted a Gnostic partition between spiritual life and material stewardship, as though the promises of God exist in a dimension safely removed from actual finances. Jesus multiplied physical bread. The incarnation is the definitive statement that God enters and redeems material existence, and discipleship includes the balance sheet. The fourth challenge is the church’s capacity — or incapacity — to take spiritual warfare seriously without either sensationalizing it or spiritualizing it into abstraction. When my brother disclosed that the devil had spoken to him by name, the correct response was not to reach for a diagnostic category or to perform polite deflection. The correct response was to stand. And I was able to stand because the Word was already alive in me — not as theological data stored for retrieval, but as a living reality the Spirit had been pressing into me in the hour before the call arrived. The church must recover the capacity to stand, in real time, when a brother discloses attack — not in spiritual bravado, not in lone-practitioner authority, but in the sober, covenant-grounded, Scripture-anchored steadfastness that Paul describes in Ephesians 6. That standing requires formation, not performance. The fifth challenge is the invitation to look for God in the small and ordinary. I said on this call: the graduation of miracles is not from miracle to absence — it is from spectacle to intimacy. Elijah, after the confrontation on Carmel, found God not in the wind or earthquake or fire but in the קוֹל דְּמָמָה דַקָּה (qol demamah daqah, kohl deh-mah-MAH dah-KAH) — the voice of fine, silent stillness. The maturity the Spirit produces in those who abide is not the capacity to command more spectacular outcomes. It is the sensitivity to find God’s handwriting in the robin at the window, the unmarked page, the sermon found at exactly the right hour. A culture addicted to spiritual spectacle will miss Him in the still small voice, and will misread the long quiet seasons of endurance as absence rather than formation. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity, as I saw it demonstrated and pressed in this call, is not the absence of interior chaos during prayer. It is the continued showing up despite it. It is the capacity to name what is real — in the business conversation and in the spiritual warfare — without losing love for the person through whom the pressure is coming. It is the willingness to say, in the middle of the attack: *I need help, not an enemy.* And it is the practiced sensitivity to recognize when a passage has been held in reserve for exactly this evening — and to receive it as such, rather than normalizing it as coincidence. Maturity is also the slowness to remain in the Word long enough for the Word to remain in us: not racing through Scripture as content to be consumed, but sitting with it the way one sits with a person one is genuinely listening to. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this call demands is integrated and unsegmented. It means holding the line on stewardship — paying what is owed, defining terms with precision, refusing to let covenant language substitute for practical financial clarity — while simultaneously standing in the gap when the enemy makes declarations against a brother. It means λογίζομαι (loh-GEE-dzoh-my) — the deliberate reckoning that Paul names in Romans 8:18 — applied to suffering, to business pressure, to the fear of provision, and to the weight of what is coming: reckoning that the glory is incomparable, that the One who holds the covenant has overcome the world, and that the memorial stones we stack in faithfulness now are the evidence our children will point to later. And it means honoring the intercession of broken friends: Samuel prayed for me at his lowest. That prayer was received. I carry it, and I owe him the same. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this call presses me into is the posture of one who abides deeply enough in the Word that when the Spirit needs to speak through me, the vocabulary and the Scripture are already alive in my mouth. I was in Proverbs 23 before Samuel called. I was not searching for a relevant passage when the test arrived — I was already living inside the one the Spirit had assigned. That kind of preparation is not accidental and it is not guaranteed simply by being a believer. It is the fruit of John 15 abiding — the branch remaining in the Vine so that when fruit is needed, the Vine has already been flowing. The prophetic community must also learn to hold the Gilgal stones of personal testimony — the specific, attentive confirmations of God’s fidelity — not as emotional crutches but as the record of a covenant-keeping God that faith requires in order to walk forward when the feeling fades. We watch. We wait. We remain faithful in the secret place. And we are ready when the phone rings and a brother says his faith is at an all-time low. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one You called and equipped before I knew the call was coming — as a steward, a covenant brother, a soon-to-be father, and a servant learning to abide deeply enough in the Word that I am armed before the test arrives. I thank You that You are the גֹּאֵל (go-EL, go-EL) — the Redeemer who is mighty, who pleads the cause of those who cannot plead for themselves — and I thank You that You were already in Proverbs 23, already in the golden chain of Romans 8, already in the image of ὕδωρ ζῶν (HOO-dor ZONE), before my brother ever called. Lord, meet him where the woe is. Meet him where the heartbreak and the crumbling inner walls are. Let the wine lose its appeal not through willpower but through a thirst You have awakened for the living water that never runs dry. I declare under the name, blood, and eternal authority of the Lord Jesus Christ that no unclean thing has rightful dwelling in a covenant son of God — that the enemy’s declarations against my brother are the declarations of a defeated creature whose end is already written, and that the love declared in Romans 8:38–39 is not moveable by any created thing. I ask You to send him the gathered community, the elders, the laying on of hands that the Spirit is pointing him toward, and to bring the fullness of the deliverance You already purchased at the cross. Guard little Elijah. Keep our covenant in Your hands. Let our finances and our friendship both be governed by the character of the God we represent. Let us be men who stack Gilgal stones in faithfulness, who look for Your handwriting in the small and ordinary, who graduate from spectacle to intimacy and find You present in the still small voice. And in the hour when suffering presses hardest, let us λογίζομαι (loh-GEE-dzoh-my) rightly — weighing the glory that is coming against the pain that is present, and declaring with Paul’s certainty that they do not compare. You have overcome the world. νενίκηκα (neh-NEE-kay-kah) — the tense is perfect, the victory is accomplished, and we walk in it now. To the Father, to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit — all glory, in the brotherhood and in the warfare, in the business and in the intercession, now and in the age to come. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Proverbs 23:12 — “Apply your heart to instruction, and your ears to words of knowledge” | לֵב (lev, LEV) — heart / inner self / will | The Hebrew lev encompasses the whole inner person — intellect, will, and emotion.
