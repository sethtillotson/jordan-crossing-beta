# 03-28 Theological Dialogue: Suffering, Intercession, and the Fully Human Christ **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation (live theological dialogue between Seth and a brother named Samuel, with integrated personal reflection woven throughout Seth’s spoken contributions) **Reflection: Walking Into the Already-Finished — On Suffering, Intercession, and the Fully Human Christ** ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother Samuel work through what the Spirit had spoken to him through a recent word I had shared, something began to sharpen in me — not just about his situation, but about a pattern I keep seeing in the body of Christ at large. We have been trained, often without knowing it, to pray as though God has not yet moved, as though His work is still pending our petition, and as though our suffering is evidence of His absence rather than evidence of His trust. What arose in that late-night conversation was not merely pastoral comfort — it was a confrontation with a deeply rooted theological error that is costing the remnant their authority, their peace, and their forward motion in the Kingdom. **Why This Matters:** The stakes here are not small. If the body of Christ continues to approach prayer as a mechanism for persuading God to do what He has already finished or to take from us what He has sovereignly entrusted to us, then entire generations of believers will remain spiritually paralyzed — not because God is withholding, but because they are standing still, waiting for Him to move first when He is, in fact, waiting for them. This is not a peripheral theological footnote. It touches the very nature of co-laboring with Christ, the meaning of suffering in the Kingdom, and the incarnational reality of Jesus as a fully human Savior who bore the full weight of what we carry. To get this wrong is to misunderstand both the cross and the commission. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, when it encounters pain, defaults immediately to removal. The psychological framework we inherit from a therapeutic culture tells us that suffering is a problem to be solved, a symptom to be treated, a wound to be closed as quickly as possible. When prayer is filtered through this framework, it becomes essentially a spiritual version of pain management — we bring our discomfort to God the way we bring a prescription to a pharmacist, expecting Him to dispense relief on demand. When the relief does not come, the natural conclusion is either that God is not listening, that we have not prayed correctly, or that faith itself has failed us. In this framework, unanswered prayer is a crisis of either divine attention or personal worthiness. The idea that God might be not only aware of the pain but actively trusting us with it — that the suffering itself might be a doorway into deeper partnership with Christ — does not register in the natural mind because it violates everything the flesh understands about a good God. **The Limitation:** But the flesh-mind, for all its sophistication, cannot perceive what is happening in the unseen architecture of the Kingdom. It reads God’s silence as abandonment when it is actually commission. It reads suffering as punishment when it is actually appointment. It reads the door that will not open as rejection when it is actually a threshold that requires *our* step to cross. The Spirit reveals what natural reasoning cannot access: that God does not withhold out of absence but out of intention, and that what He calls us to walk through, He has already walked through ahead of us — and is now waiting, with nail-scarred hands extended, not to carry us past the pain but to walk with us *through* it. This is not cruelty. It is covenant. And no amount of emotional intelligence or therapeutic wisdom can perceive that distinction without the illumination of the Holy Spirit. ## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation As I sat with what emerged in this conversation with Samuel, the first thing that arrested me was the simplicity of a truth that costs so much to receive: **the work is already complete, and He is waiting for us to step into it.** This is not the language of passivity misread as activity. It is the language of a finished redemption calling for a living response. When Samuel described praying for God to take away his pain — *“Lord, take this away, take this pain away”* — I recognized in his words something I have heard echoed across countless conversations, and something I have spoken myself in seasons of crushing. It is the prayer of a man who does not yet know that he has been entrusted with what he is carrying. The Greek word that rises in me here is δύναμις (dynamis, DOO-nah-mees) — power, capacity, the inherent ability placed within a vessel for a purpose. When Paul writes in Philippians 4:13 that he can do all things through Christ who strengthens him, the word translated “strengthens” is ἐνδυναμοῦντι (endynamounti) — literally, the One who infuses δύναμις (DOO-nah-mees) into me. The suffering Paul endures, the thorn he carries, the chains, the shipwrecks, the beatings — none of these prompt God to simply remove the hardship. Instead, God says, *“My grace is sufficient for you, for My strength is made perfect in weakness”* (2 Corinthians 12:9, NKJV). This is not a consolation prize. This is the revelation that the suffering itself becomes the site of divine δύναμις (DOO-nah-mees) — not despite the pain, but within it. Samuel put it beautifully, and it stopped me: *“You have to empty your heart to refill it.”* That is not a therapeutic platitude — that is a kenotic principle embedded in the very life of Christ. The Greek word κένωσις (kenosis, keh-NO-sis) — the self-emptying — is the theological heart of Philippians 2:7, where Paul writes that Christ “made Himself of no reputation,” literally *emptied* Himself, taking the form of a servant. The emptying precedes the exaltation. The death precedes the resurrection. And for us, the hollow ache of grief, loss, or spiritual desolation is not the end of the story — it is the shape of the vessel being formed to hold more of Him. But we must not rush that emptying. We must not pray it closed before it has finished its work. I then found myself returning to something I told Samuel about prayer going unanswered — and I want to be precise here, because this is easily misunderstood. I am not saying God never supernaturally removes suffering. He does. He healed the sick, raised the dead, and calmed the storm. But there is a category of prayer that goes unanswered because the one praying is asking God to do what God has already commissioned *them* to do. Jesus sent out the Twelve with authority to heal the sick, cast out demons, and proclaim the Kingdom (Matthew 10:7–8, NKJV). He did not say, “Pray and wait for Me to come do it.” He said, *“Go.”* The commission was given. The authority was delegated. And when the disciples failed to cast out the demon in Matthew 17, Jesus did not say, “You should have prayed harder for Me to do it.” He pointed to the disciples’ own posture — *“because of your unbelief”* (Matthew 17:20, NKJV). The capacity was in them. The faith to exercise it had faltered. This is why I described something that surprised even me during that conversation: the recognition that, for the first time, I was not functioning primarily as an intercessor bringing the people’s cry to God, but as a prophet bringing God’s word to the people. The Hebrew concept at work here is נָבִיא (nabi, naw-BEE) — one who is called to speak, to pour forth, to bubble up what has been placed within. A נָבִיא (naw-BEE) does not manufacture the word; he receives it and releases it. What I described to Samuel — the outline drawn from six recent meditations gathered between March 26 and March 27, 2026 — I had not assembled with him consciously in mind. And yet when the word landed on his precise situation, that was not coincidence. That was the Spirit ordering revelation ahead of the need, the way God always works: *“Before they call, I will answer; and while they are still speaking, I will hear”* (Isaiah 65:24, NKJV). The word was prepared before the wound was expressed. What Samuel described next humbled me deeply. He spoke of God standing at the edge of the forest, hand extended, saying: *“I will guide you through the pain, but you have to hold My hand and walk with Me.”* That image is a living echo of Psalm 23 — not the sentimental version we recite at funerals, but the raw original: *“Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil; for You are with me”* (Psalm 23:4, NKJV). The Psalmist does not say God removes the valley. He says he *walks through* it — and the Shepherd is there. The Hebrew word for “walk” here is אֵלֵךְ (elekh, ay-LEKH) — active, progressive, forward motion. It is not sitting. It is not waiting in place. It is movement *into* the darkness with the Shepherd beside you. Samuel’s image was not poetic decoration. It was biblical cartography. I think about Peter walking on the water in Matthew 14, and the moment that always stops me is not the moment he sinks — it is the moment *before* that. Jesus did not walk the water for him. Jesus said, *“Come”* (Matthew 14:29, NKJV). That single word contains the entire theology of co-laboring. The water was dangerous. The wind was real. Peter’s terror was legitimate. But the invitation was still *“Come”* — not *“Wait while I carry you over.”* The miracle was not that Jesus walked on water. The miracle was that a human man, sustained by his gaze upon Christ, also walked on water. And the lesson of the sinking is not failure — it is instruction. When the gaze broke, the capacity broke. *“O you of little faith, why did you doubt?”* (Matthew 14:31, NKJV). The doubt was directional — it shifted Peter’s attention from the person of Christ to the size of the storm. The pain had not changed. The wind had not stopped. But the sufficiency of Christ was sufficient to hold him *through* it, not exempt him *from* it. Then we arrived at Paul — and I said something to Samuel that I want to sit with longer here. Paul, who wrote more of the New Testament than any other single voice, who was caught up into the third heaven and heard *“inexpressible words”* (2 Corinthians 12:4, NKJV), who planted churches across the known world and faced death so many times it ceased to alarm him — even this man carried the σκόλοψ (skolops, SKOH-lops), the thorn, the stake in the flesh. Three times he asked for its removal (2 Corinthians 12:8, NKJV). Three times the answer was not removal but sufficiency. I am pressing on something here: if Paul’s spiritual stature did not exempt him from sustained, unrelenting suffering, then the expectation that spiritual growth will eventually produce a pain-free life is not a biblical expectation. It is a Western, comfort-conditioned theological fantasy. Maturity in Christ does not purchase exemption from the σκόλοψ (SKOH-lops). It teaches you to carry it differently — pressed into His chest rather than straining away from it. Samuel made a remark that landed hard: *“Because he’s human, and so was Christ.”* This was not a throwaway line. This was, in its simplicity, a confession of the most contested doctrine in the history of the Church. The full humanity of Jesus Christ is not a minor footnote to His deity. It is the *mechanism* of our salvation. Hebrews 2:17 says He was made *“like His brethren, in all things”* — fully human in every dimension — *“that He might be a merciful and faithful High Priest”* (NKJV). If Jesus was not fully human, He cannot be a faithful High Priest for humans. If He did not actually feel the weight of what we carry, His sympathy is performance, not reality. But Hebrews 4:15 declares He *“was in all points tempted as we are, yet without sin”* (NKJV). The Greek ὁμοιότητα (homoioteta, hoh-moy-OH-teh-tah) — likeness, sameness — is the word behind “in all points.” He was not *similar* to us in a general way. He was the same as us in specificity — hunger, grief, exhaustion, temptation, the weight of injustice, the silence of God in the darkest hour. This leads me to the Johannine test I mentioned to Samuel, which I want to unfold fully here, because it is not merely historical — it is present and pressing. The Apostle John writes in 1 John 4:2 (NKJV): *“By this you know the Spirit of God: every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is of God.”* The Greek perfect tense in the phrase ἐληλυθότα ἐν σαρκί (elēlythota en sarki, el-ay-LYOO-thoh-tah en sar-KEE) — literally “having come in flesh” — is not a past event with no present consequence. The perfect tense in Greek implies a past action with continuing present result. Jesus has come in the flesh, and He remains the Word made flesh, even now bearing the wounds of the cross in the resurrection body that Thomas touched (John 20:27, NKJV). The Gnostics tried to spiritualize Him away — to suggest the Christ-spirit departed before the suffering was complete. John’s first epistle is a frontal assault on that lie. Jesus did not vacate the body at the cross. He bore the full weight of it in the full reality of human flesh, and He rose bearing those wounds still — because those wounds are not scars of defeat. They are the credentials of His authority. This is why Revelation 5 is so staggering. John sees the scroll — sealed with seven seals, no one found worthy to open it — and he weeps. Then one of the elders says: *“Do not weep. Behold, the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has prevailed to open the scroll”* (Revelation 5:5, NKJV). John turns to see the Lion — and what he sees is a Lamb, *“as though it had been slain”* (Revelation 5:6, NKJV). The wounds are still present. The credentials of suffering are still visible. The authority to open the sealed mysteries of history — to break the seals and release the final movements of the Kingdom — belongs to the One who suffered in human flesh and rose in human flesh and still bears the marks of that suffering. This is the Christ that Samuel and I were confessing in the small hours of the morning. Not an abstract theological concept. Not a spiritual force. A Person, bearing wounds, worthy to open everything that is sealed. The conversation closed quietly — Samuel needing rest, the weight of the hour pressing down — but something had been accomplished that had nothing to do with the clock. A word had been planted. The kind of word that does not simply inform; it *roots*. Samuel said it himself: *“I see my path a lot clearer than I did.”* That clarity is not the product of intellectual comprehension. It is the fruit of revelation rightly received — the kind that settles into the bones and begins to reorganize the way one walks forward. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation presses against is the **prosperity-adjacent theology that equates spiritual maturity with the removal of suffering.** It is everywhere in modern Western Christianity — packaged in the language of breakthrough, blessing, and declaring victory. And while none of those categories are false in themselves, when they become the primary lens through which we evaluate our relationship with God, they produce a catastrophic misreading of seasons where God is not removing the hardship but *trusting us with it*. Paul did not lack faith. He lacked a theology that said faith means no pain. We must recover the theology of the σκόλοψ (SKOH-lops) — not to glorify suffering for its own sake, but to recognize that some things God assigns rather than merely allows, and to stop praying for removal when we are being called to endurance. The second challenge is the **passive prayer posture that asks God to do what He has already commissioned us to do.** This is one of the most widespread sources of frustrated faith in the body of Christ. We pray for God to open doors He has told us to knock on. We pray for God to feed the hungry when He said, *“You give them something to eat”* (Matthew 14:16, NKJV). We pray for boldness without stepping into the situation that would require it. The commission is not pending divine initiation — the commission has been issued. The question is whether we will step into what is already finished in the heavens and work it out in the earth. The third challenge is the **failure to properly guard the full humanity of Christ as doctrinal bedrock.** This is not merely a historical problem with ancient Gnostics. The impulse to spiritualize Jesus — to thin Him into a concept, a force, a divine frequency — is alive and accelerating in streams of mystical Christianity, New Age syncretism, and even well-meaning progressive theology. But when Jesus is reduced to a spiritual metaphor, the incarnation loses its power, the resurrection becomes symbolic, and the wounds He still bears become decorative rather than authoritative. Every teacher, every movement, every voice that subtly diminishes the born-of-woman, flesh-and-blood, truly-died-and-truly-rose humanity of Jesus Christ must be discerned carefully — not with hostility, but with the Johannine test firmly in hand. The fourth challenge is the **false dichotomy between knowing and doing** — the fine line Samuel named himself: *“It’s stuff you already know. It’s a fine line between what you know and what you do.”* This is the application gap that James addresses with almost painful directness: *“But be doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving yourselves”* (James 1:22, NKJV). The Greek word for “doers” is ποιηταί (poiētai, poy-ay-TIE) — makers, performers, those who bring something into being through action. The knowledge of Scripture, without the embodied obedience that Scripture calls forth, produces a particular kind of self-deception — the comfort of familiarity without the transformation of encounter. We must resist the temptation to let theological depth become a substitute for Kingdom action. The fifth challenge touches on **the sacred alone time as a non-negotiable formation practice.** I mentioned to Samuel that reading theological commentary — even good commentary — should not replace the discipline of waiting before God in personal encounter with Scripture. There is a real danger in a generation of believers who are deeply literate in what others have said about Scripture and yet have never learned to sit alone with the text and wait for the Spirit to speak directly into their specific situation. Commentary, teaching, and theological meditation — including everything I write and record — is meant to *supplement* that sacred space, never to colonize it. The remnant must learn to wait. And waiting is not passive. It is its own form of walking. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This meditation calls me — and those who walk alongside me — to fundamentally reframe what maturity looks like in the Kingdom. It does not look like the absence of pain. It looks like the capacity to carry pain without breaking eye contact with Christ. Maturity is not arriving at a place of comfort; it is arriving at a place of rootedness deep enough that the wind cannot uproot you. This requires a willingness to stop praying for God to do what He has already told us to do, and to start stepping — even when the water is dark, even when the wind is real, even when the shore feels impossibly far. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The call here is concrete: step into the role. Not wait for God to step into it for you. The commission has been issued. The authority has been delegated. The δύναμις (DOO-nah-mees) has been deposited. What remains is the choice to act on it — to hold the hand that is already extended and begin walking, one step at a time, through the forest rather than standing frozen at its edge praying for it to disappear. **For Prophetic Posture:** The remnant is being called right now to function as a נָבִיא (naw-BEE) people — those who carry and release what the Spirit has placed within them, not for their own benefit but for the building up of the body. This means investing in the sacred alone time, storing up revelation through disciplined meditation, and being willing to speak what has been received even when the full application is not yet visible. The word I shared with Samuel was gathered across six meditations I had not consciously assembled for him. That is the nature of prophetic preparation — we fill up in secret, and the Spirit deploys it in the moment of need. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I stand before You as one of Your servants — a steward of the mysteries You have entrusted to Your servants the prophets, a co-heir of the Kingdom established in the blood and resurrection of Jesus Christ, Your Son, fully divine and fully human, born of woman, crucified in flesh, raised in body, bearing still the wounds that qualify Him as the only One worthy to open every sealed thing. I thank You that the work is already finished — that You have not left us to wait on something yet to come, but have invited us to step into what You have already completed in the heavens. I thank You for the suffering You trust us with, for the thorns that keep us low, for the pain that hollows us into vessels large enough to hold more of You. I ask, on behalf of every brother and sister who has grown weary in the waiting, who has prayed for removal when You were calling them to endurance, who has looked away from Your face toward the size of the storm — restore their gaze. Let them see You, greater than the pain, walking ahead of them through the forest, hand extended, voice saying *“Come.”* And for those who will read these words after the conversation that sparked them, let the word do what You intended it to do before any of us knew it was needed. You are faithful. You are worthy. Your Kingdom is unshakeable. And we are walking into it — one Spirit-ordered step at a time. To the Lamb who was slain and lives forevermore: all dominion, all honor, and all glory.
