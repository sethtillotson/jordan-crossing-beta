# 08-21 Reflection: Spiritual Deliverance and a Father’s Cancer Diagnosis **Title:** 08-21 at 22:23 — The Seat I Had No Right to Sit In and the Breath That Emptied the Room **Recorded:** 2026-08-21 22:23:45 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation My father asked me what was going on, and I heard my own voice fill the phone line with the new welding job — the certifications, the burns on my skin, the Bug-O machine, the crane that nearly crushed a man — and then, almost as an afterthought, I said the words *I also had another weird spiritual experience*, and everything else on that call became prelude. For one full hour the Lord let me stand in a room full of legion and did not deliver on my timeline, and the terror was not the demons but Christ’s stillness, which pressed on a seam between what I believe and how I behaved. Then, before I could set down the weight of that war, my father told me he has cancer — in his prostate, his ribs, his glands, his lower abdomen — and the whole call turned into a son learning his father’s flesh is under siege. Somewhere between the demonic pressure and the radiation machine waiting in Rochester, the Spirit was pressing a single thread through all of it: that suffering is not the absence of God but often the very doorway into seeing Him. **Why This Matters:** This matters because I have to be honest about what the Lord exposed, not what I wish had happened. I went into that intercession still carrying fragments of a works-based salvation I thought I had already surrendered — little pockets of ego that believed *I* had to play the part, *I* had to win the argument, *I* had to be the deliverer. The Spirit did not rebuke that pride with thunder; He let a legion press on it until it cracked open in front of me, and I discovered I had sat down in a seat that was never mine to occupy. For the Body of Christ this is not a thrilling war story; it is a sober word about how deeply self can hide inside even our most spiritual acts, and how a spirit can name the name of Jesus and still refuse to confess Him as God come in the flesh — which means discernment is not optional decoration on the Christian life; it is survival. And it matters because the same Body is being trained, right now, in how to suffer without being destroyed and how to intercede without going insane — because the front line of deliverance and the radiation room are both places where God seems silent on our timetable, and the whole of discipleship turns on whether we will run to Him or run from Him. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind has a tidy explanation ready for the whole night, and for the whole call. A young man drank himself into a stupor — a full bottle of chardonnay, a bottle of Jack, ciders, beer — became belligerent, frightened his parents, and in his intoxication produced a dissociative episode that a religiously primed friend then interpreted as demonic. It would note the exhaustion, the twelve-hour welding shifts, the sleepless nights spent managing a failing business overseas, the stress of stolen commissions and an unborn child on the way, and conclude that a mind under that much pressure hears voices and assigns them meaning; the “voices naming themselves Gabriel and Jacob” become dissociation or roleplay, the “growl” becomes a drunk man’s theatrics, and the relief the next morning becomes ordinary hangover recovery plus emotional catharsis. When the call turned to cancer, the same mind reaches for the obvious frames: a stressed co-owner building operational handbooks, an aging man juggling PET scans, radiation schedules, ivermectin, and the humiliating prospect of hormone therapy, and a son and father exchanging bad news across a breaking phone signal. It would counsel therapy for the intercessory weeping, medical compliance for the cancer, and perhaps a gentle suggestion that all this talk of demons and healers is a coping mechanism against fear. Compassion is due here, because these are real bodies breaking and real grief pressing in. **The Limitation:** That reading can account for the body but not for the boundary that was crossed. It cannot explain a voice that will freely say *Jesus* and then, in the same breath, sneer *who is Jesus Christ?* — the one line the flesh has no motive to invent, because it costs the speaker nothing to simply confess and end the encounter. It cannot weigh the moment a voice in a brother’s throat used plural pronouns it should not have used, named a welding job it had no way of knowing, or quoted sentences from a manuscript no living soul has read. The natural mind assumes that if a thing can be partly explained by pressure, it is wholly explained by pressure — but Scripture never treats inebriation and invasion as rivals; it treats the first as the open door to the second. And when it hears “cancer” it reasons cause and effect but cannot perceive a war fought by principalities through people who break their vows, nor a repentance that formed a man just in time to shepherd real souls, nor a suffering that draws a man nearer to God than comfort ever could. The flesh mind flattens mystery into pathology because it has no category for it — not from cruelty, but because these things are apprehended only by the Spirit. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I keep going back to the sound of my father’s ordinary question — *what’s going on?* — and how I answered with steel: twelve-hour shifts, four-to-four-thirty, mandatory overtime, twenty-eight dollars an hour with the night differential, the three-hundred-dollar boot stipend, the metatarsal guard I had to borrow because I could only afford the cheapest steel toes at Walmart. And the burns: my wrists, my hands, my upper arms, my chest, my neck, my lower face, all scorched by an arc whose ultraviolet radiation is fiercer than anything aluminum ever threw at me. I told him it hurts worse than a sunburn, that it’s swollen, that my skin isn’t used to it yet the way the veterans’ skin is — they know how far to stand, how to cover their eyes. I did not know at the time how much that whole conversation was preaching to me: a man newly exposed to a fire he had never welded under before, not yet knowing how close he could stand without being burned. Because that is exactly the man who walked into the deliverance. I have done this before — I told my dad about Abigail, how that one was *exciting and smooth*, how I felt I had played a part in something. And there it is, the very phrase the Spirit came to burn out of me: *I felt like I played a part*. This time the Lord withdrew the smoothness. For an hour He let the legion sit there, confusing me, torturing me, prodding at my theology and my Christology, and the horror of it was not their presence but His stillness. I knew He was with me. The deliverance simply was not happening on my clock, and in that silence I discovered what I thought was already gone: pride, ego, the quiet heresy that my performance was load-bearing. What arrests me now is not the terror but the seam they found — the space between what I believe and how I behaved. I confused my role. I thought my task was to stand and argue and hold the line until Jesus arrived, when in truth my calling was to step aside and worship so the glory of God could enter the atmosphere and do what only He can do. The distinction between conviction and conduct is not academic; it is the very seam through which the enemy pressed for sixty minutes. The demons found the seam in me the way I find the seam of a weld, and they pressed the arc right into it. I keep returning, too, to the phone call that started it — the one where I never even suggested the drink be poured out. My brother, mid-intercession, asked whether Jesus might be telling him to pour the rest down the sink, and all I said was that the choice was his. He did it himself. Then his parents told him the last thing he remembered was that call, and the weight of it settled: the pouring out was the last clear moment before the voices came, because that act of obedience was the exact seam the enemy chose to exploit. He had cleaned the house, but the house was empty. A cleaned house is not a safe house. A house is only safe when it is filled. The Lord’s own words rise up to meet this. Jesus warned that when an unclean spirit goes out of a man and finds no rest, it returns and finds the house “empty, swept, and put in order,” and then brings seven others more wicked than itself, “and the last state of that man is worse than the first” (Matthew 12:43–45, NKJV). I had read that passage many times, but that night I watched it. The reformation of behavior without the habitation of God is not deliverance; it is preparation for a heavier occupation. And this dismantles a comfortable doctrine, because my brother is in the Body of Christ and he was fully possessed. We like to believe the saved are hermetically sealed, but the man from whom Legion was cast in the Gerasenes was set free and then “sitting at the feet of Jesus, clothed and in his right mind” (Luke 8:35, NKJV) — deliverance and belonging are not always simultaneous, and the drinking was not merely a vice but a hinge the enemy swung on. The first Scripture the enemy weaponized against me was the very test John gives: “Beloved, do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits, whether they are of God… every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is of God” (1 John 4:1–3, NKJV). I had always been taught the shorthand that an unclean spirit cannot say the name of Jesus. That night proved the shorthand false and the Scripture exact. These ones said *Jesus*. What they would not do — could not do — was confess Him as God come in the flesh. The Greek sharpens it: the word John uses is ὁμολογέω (homologeō, ho-mo-lo-GEH-oh) — to say the same thing, to speak in agreement, to confess. The demons could *pronounce* the name, but they could not ὁμολογέω (ho-mo-lo-GEH-oh) — they could not *agree* with heaven about who He is. And this was the very trap they set for me: over and over they tried to get me to make an agreement, to “confess” with them into their reality, to say the same thing they were saying. The entire battle was over ὁμολογέω (ho-mo-lo-GEH-oh) — they wanted my agreement transferred out of heaven and into their lie, one small concession at a time. This is why the accusation about righteousness was so subtle. The voice calling itself Jacob said, *Do you see him, Seth? Standing there in his own righteousness. He is on the throne of heaven, standing in his own righteousness.* It was an inversion so clever it almost sounds pious. But Christ never clutched His own righteousness as a possession — “He made Himself of no reputation, taking the form of a bondservant… He humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death, even the death of the cross” (Philippians 2:7–8, NKJV). The demon painted Jesus as a self-exalting throne-sitter precisely because that is what Satan is; it was Lucifer who said, “I will exalt my throne above the stars of God… I will be like the Most High” (Isaiah 14:13–14, NKJV). They were projecting their own father’s ambition onto the Son who came to serve. My answer — *the only one I see is the Holy One* — was the Spirit refusing their agreement through my mouth. The seam they pressed was the space between my conviction and my conduct, and my error was discussing anything with them at all. They used my brother’s own voice before they revealed themselves, framing me into a defensive posture from the very first exchange, and that same slip repeated for an hour in deeper forms. This is the ancient pattern — the serpent in the garden did not seize; he asked, “Has God indeed said?” (Genesis 3:1, NKJV). Every temptation began as a question, an invitation to negotiate, and I answered as myself instead of stepping aside for the One who alone has standing to speak. Even the sober vigilance Scripture commands failed me here — the word behind “sober” is νήφω (nēphō, NAY-foh) — to be clear-headed, unintoxicated, vigilant — bound directly to warfare: “Be sober, be vigilant; because your adversary the devil walks about like a roaring lion, seeking whom he may devour” (1 Peter 5:8, NKJV). I had been awake, but conversation was the drink the enemy poured for me. What undid me was not their power but their knowledge, because the higher spirits knew me by name, knew I had just come from a new welding job, threw back errors from my own Christology, sentences from the third volume of meditations I am writing that no living soul has read. That is how I know this was no random haunting but a premeditated, studied attack — I had seen something skulking in the shadows of my own house two days before and commanded it out by the blood of Christ, and it left, but it had been watching. They begged me to pray for them — “pray for us, Seth” — and in that plural I heard the legion, for there is no place in Scripture where a demon is prayed for; they are already judged, awaiting only their execution (Matthew 25:41; Jude 6). They offered me their false liturgy — three black dots in the mirror, Satan standing behind a thumb, “do you see him?” — because if I had agreed to see what I did not see, their reality would have broken through. Agreement is the currency of the spirit realm. Even when I attempted to send angels, the answer was chilling: “Test me all you want, Seth. My brother Jacob is here and he will defend me.” A war was raging over authority I kept trying to wield as though it were mine. The Kingdom mystery the natural mind would miss entirely is *how the hour actually ended*. It did not end when I out-argued them. It ended when I stopped defending myself as myself. As long as I answered *as Seth*, they had a handle on me — “I’ll defend myself as myself; I don’t defend myself as myself, Christ is my defense.” The turn came when the fear of God fell on me and I understood I had sat down in Christ’s seat, that I was in His way. So I did the only right thing left: I repented mid-intercession, renounced everything the unclean spirits had spoken over my brother, his family, his lineage, my own, and then simply began to worship: *My Father in heaven, I see You, I love You, thank You for Your Son.* When they said “Satan has claimed you,” I did not answer “he cannot have me” as Seth, because Seth has no power. Only when I laid down my ego, my flesh, my ministry-identity, and said *Your turn, Lord — You do the work* did the glory come and finish it. And here is the mercy I cannot stop thanking Him for: He kept my eyes closed in the spirit. The voice told me Satan himself was standing there with the legion, waiting for me, and the Lord did not open my sight to it. “As for me, I will see Your face in righteousness; I shall be satisfied when I awake in Your likeness” (Psalm 17:15, NKJV) — He is jealous over what my eyes behold. What natural courage would have craved as proof, His grace withheld as protection. Then came the breath. When I worshipped and cried out for His presence and glory, the thing screamed louder than I thought a human throat could go — *stop making the volume so loud* — and I blew into the microphone and said, *feel the breath of God*. And it recoiled, and I said, *oh, you like the breath of God?* — and it hung up. I did not plan that. That was the Spirit reaching for the oldest image in Scripture, when “the LORD God… breathed into his nostrils the breath of life” (Genesis 2:7, NKJV), the נְשָׁמָה (neshamah, ne-sha-MAH), and the Risen Christ who “breathed on them, and said… Receive the Holy Spirit” (John 20:22, NKJV). The רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — Spirit, breath, wind — is unbearable to what is dead. My breath meant nothing; His breath emptied the room. And then the tongues, which was not “hala-bara-bara, power-of-God” performance — that is bullseye theater, and I said so. When the Spirit gave me Hebrew that night, it was Him supplying “the words that you need to say in order to make the prayer and agreement between heaven and earth.” It was ὁμολογέω (ho-mo-lo-GEH-oh) again — but now heaven’s agreement flowing *out* of me instead of the demons’ agreement being pried out of me. “The Spirit… makes intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered” (Romans 8:26, NKJV). I do not even remember most of what was prayed; I only have the recording, and the fruit. Because the fruit came the next morning, almost embarrassingly ordinary: my brother called and said he could not believe how great he felt, “thirty pounds lighter,” and his parents thanked me for getting him to pour out all his alcohol. But I never told him to pour it out. Someone poured that bottle of chardonnay into the sink in the night, and it was not me and, in the deepest sense, it was not him. *Don’t thank me, that was Jesus.* I record everything because the demons “are really good at confusion and getting to break up the koinonia,” the κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-no-NEE-ah), the shared-life fellowship of the brethren — and by morning I could not even remember the prayer the Spirit prayed. That poured-out wine is the last thing the Spirit set before me from that night, and it will not let me go. My brother had been drinking wine to drown; in the morning it was poured out onto the ground like an offering, and I cannot help but hear it against the other wine — “This cup is the new covenant in My blood, which is shed for you” (Luke 22:20, NKJV). The wine of self-medication, poured out in the dark, is the shadow; the blood of Christ, poured out on the cross, is the substance that actually delivers. He does not merely make a man pour out his liquor; He pours out Himself, and a man wakes up thirty pounds lighter without knowing whose hands emptied his bottle. And what strikes me is the ordinariness of the vessel hell raged against — not a mega-church general but a welder in Fargo, exhausted, sweat-soaked, black with steel, writing about how to sanctify an ordinary Tuesday. That the kingdom of darkness studies an unpublished manuscript by an unknown welder and threatens his unborn child over it is itself the sermon. But the call did not end with the war. Before I could set that weight down, my father began listing the places the cancer had gone — prostate, then the second and fifth ribs, then a gland on his left side, then a whole cluster wrapped toward the back of his lower abdomen. He said the ribs were probably benign, trying to hold the diagnosis at arm’s length even as it colonized his body, and then, almost in the same breath, the small mercy: they scanned again and the spots on the prostate weren’t as big as they thought, they had shrunk. I sat there aching and burning in my own flesh, listening to a man I love narrate the geography of his mortality, and I could do nothing but offer to drive out and lay hands on him. That offer — “Can I come and lay hands on you and pray?” — is the only real thing I had to give. What arrests me is that this conversation began with me confessing brokenness, not his. I had just spoken of people who make oaths and then get shocked-drunk against their own vows, and how I myself slip and must run back to God rather than drown in condemnation. And now here is my father, facing a treatment that would give him menstrual cramps and swelling in his chest to starve the cancer, saying plainly he does not want to war against his own created design for two years. The same thread runs through both moments: the brokenness of the flesh, the integrity we cannot perfectly keep, the created goodness now perverted by decay. We are both men under repair, and neither of us can fix ourselves from the inside out. The word of the crucified Christ opens over this — “Nevertheless not My will, but Yours, be done” (Luke 22:42, NKJV) — and I told my father I always have to end my prayers there, because my own θέλημα (thelēma, THEL-ay-mah), my will and desire, gets in the way. That word came from Gethsemane, from a Man sweating great drops of blood; when I told him I suffer as Christ suffered and am learning to count it joy, I was not spiritualizing away his cancer. I was standing beside him in the garden. The same רוּחַ (ROO-akh) surfaces under all of this in a different key. My friend Andy lies in a medically induced coma because they say he cannot breathe on his own; my father is up ten times a night, barely able to pass water, his breath thinning under disease. The breath God breathed into Adam’s nostrils is not ours to manufacture, and when a man cannot breathe on his own we are confronted with the nakedest truth of creaturehood — that our next breath is a gift, not a possession. This is why I could tell my father the answer to “God’s not doing this in my life” is almost always another question: did you ask Him? When was the last time you stood before Him alone? The canon carries this from shadow into fulfillment in Job, who lost everything and sat in ashes while his friends became little theologians explaining his suffering. God did not vindicate the explainers; He vindicated the wrestler. I told my father he has the right to argue with God, to say “this was for no purpose, You have to tell me,” just as Job did — and the answer God gave was not a syllabus but a Presence: “Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?” (Job 38:4, NKJV). The end of that wrestling was not information but sight: “I have heard of You by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees You” (Job 42:5, NKJV). Job did not get his questions answered; he got God. Here the theological tension opens sharply, and I want to be careful with it. I said in that call that cancer, and disease generally, is spiritually rooted in the perversion of created good — that there was never meant to be disease — and this is true to Scripture, that death entered through sin (Romans 5:12) and creation groans in bondage to φθορά (phthora, fthor-AH), corruption and decay (Romans 8:21–22). Yet I was equally careful to say not every disease is a direct demonic assault, and I will not overstate what I do not know. There is a discipline in refusing to become the little mini-theologian who reads the Bible and suddenly knows everything. I hold loosely, too, what I told my father about hell and forgetting — that the second death is finally non-existence, and that just as God chose to forget our sins, casting them behind His back, so the redeemed will not carry the finally-lost as an eternal wound. The deeper thing the Spirit was pressing is that forgetting is an act of divine will and love: “I, even I, am He who blots out your transgressions for My own sake; and I will not remember your sins” (Isaiah 43:25, NKJV). A God who can choose to forget is a God whose mercy is not passive but deliberate — covenant-grade grace, not sentimental erasure. And then there is the weeping. My father, in his blunt fatherly way, said daily intercession and tears did not sound very healthy. I told him it is a gift — that not many are given the weight of the Father’s grief, and that some who prayed to see in the spirit went insane when they saw the demons hanging off buildings and the chains dragging behind people. To be entrusted with a fragment of God’s own sorrow over a broken world is not a disorder to be medicated; it is a portion of fellowship with “a Man of sorrows and acquainted with grief” (Isaiah 53:3, NKJV). Yet even here the discipline holds — I always end at *not my will* — because the weight would crush me if I bore it in my own strength. The tears are priestly, not pathological, but they must be laid on the altar and not hoarded. And in the fractures we named — families divided over politics, a wife whose husband is not on the same spiritual level, a son watching his mother sleep herself toward death through years of depression — Christ Himself foretold this cutting: “I did not come to bring peace but a sword” (Matthew 10:34, NKJV). I told my father, half in dark humor, that people are divided over Trump but Jesus started the dividing. The loneliness of the genuinely praying soul in an unbelieving household is not a sign of failure; it is the μάχαιρα (machaira, MAH-khai-rah) landing exactly where He said it would. I feel my own repentance being drawn out even now. I confessed to my father that I too am off social media, that I cannot bear the arguing, the way the one place built to connect the world holds no real community. But the sharper conviction is that I knew the man who first introduced me to Christ seven years ago, and I only truly met Jesus myself recently. I was near the fire a long time before I was warmed by it — the same welder’s fire, the same gap between knowing about Him and knowing Him, the whole distance of Job’s confession, and I have only lately crossed it. So I have no room to boast. I have only room to lay hands on my father and drive the hell of a drive to Rochester because I have finally seen with my eye what I long only heard with my ear. For the Body, this whole call is a template of how the saints must attend one another in the last stretch — not a debate stage but an altar, not a solution but a presence. Whether it is a black woman in sales who has had to scream to be heard, or an old man deciding whether to submit to two years of hormone suppression, or a fully-possessed brother the household never even heard growl, the Kingdom demands we treat image-bearers as people and never as problems to be solved. ## Implications and Challenges **On the doctrine that the saved cannot be oppressed, and the empty house.** I have to speak plainly, and gently, because I know it costs comfort. My brother was in the Body of Christ and was fully possessed, and much of Western teaching has no shelf to put that on. But the Gerasene demoniac was delivered *before* he sat clothed at Jesus’ feet (Luke 8:35), and the drinking in my brother’s house was a literal hinge the enemy used. Worse, a house merely cleaned is a house made ready for a heavier occupation — Matthew 12:43–45 is not metaphor but the pattern I watched unfold. Grace does not mean vigilance is optional; it means the Deliverer is available, and the house must be not merely swept but filled with the רוּחַ (ROO-akh) that no cleaned emptiness can invite worse than itself. Let us love the oppressed among us enough to stop pretending they cannot exist. **On the shorthand doctrines we inherit and never test.** I was taught that no unclean spirit can say the name of Jesus, and I believed it until a legion said it to my face and then asked *who is Jesus Christ?* The real test is not pronunciation but confession — ὁμολογέω (ho-mo-lo-GEH-oh), agreement with heaven that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh and is God (1 John 4:2–3). This challenges how casually we handle the deep water of the spirit, armed with slogans instead of Scripture. If I had leaned on the shorthand that night, the moment it failed I would have concluded I was safe when I was most exposed. Test the spirits by the exact words the Spirit gave, not the ones tradition rounded off. **On the hidden theology of works that survives in our spiritual highlight reels — and the seat that is not ours.** The most sobering correction is aimed at me. I called the Abigail deliverance “exciting and smooth” and said I “felt like I played a part,” and that sentence was the tumor the Lord came to cut out. So much of our charismatic culture trains us to measure ministry by the thrill and by our role in it, which is works-salvation wearing spiritual clothes — and the deepest form of it is sitting down in Christ’s own seat, defending ourselves as ourselves. The hour of terror was mercy precisely because it stripped my performance out of the equation until only worship was left, and my error the whole hour was that I discussed anything with them at all. Even Michael the archangel “dared not bring against him a reviling accusation, but said, ‘The Lord rebuke you!’” (Jude 9). If an archangel refused to trade words with the accuser, how much more should a welder from Fargo — the seat of interrogation is not our seat. **On the counterfeit of tongues and the charismatic distortion of sobriety.** I said tongues is not “hala-bara-bara, power-of-God” theater, and I stand by the sharpness of it; when the Spirit gave me Hebrew, it was the concrete supply of “the words you need to say to make agreement between heaven and earth,” an echo of Romans 8:26, not self-generated ecstasy. Much of what passes for tongues in our gatherings is emotional display detached from any intercessory burden. And the same distortion mistakes loss of self-control for the fullness of the Spirit — but the fruit of the Spirit includes self-control (Galatians 5:22–23), and νήφω (NAY-foh), clear-headed vigilance, is the posture Scripture commands for warfare (1 Peter 5:8). To be filled is to be more awake, not less. Let us hunger for the genuine and repent of the show. **On the theology that knows too much, and the discipline of leaving mystery unforced.** I said it bluntly to my father — people study the Bible, become little mini-theologians, and now think they know everything, and no, they do not. Job’s friends had impeccable doctrine and God’s wrath was kindled against them; He vindicated the wrestler, not the explainers. This is why I hold my words about hell and forgetting loosely, and why I would not overstate that every disease is a direct demonic assault. The things deliberately left out of Scripture are not gaps to be filled by our cleverness but doorways to drive us back to the Author for revelation He alone can give (Isaiah 43:25). The invitation is to trade the arrogance of certainty for the intimacy of asking. **On weeping as gift, the war against created design, and presence as the ministry of the saints.** My father gently suggested that daily tears in intercession are not healthy, and I understand the concern — the flesh can counterfeit spiritual grief with mere depression, as we saw in the mother sleeping herself to death. But we must not let a therapeutic culture reclassify every spiritual weight as a symptom to be flattened; to carry a fragment of the Father’s grief is fellowship with the Man of Sorrows (Isaiah 53:3), dangerous only when carried in our own strength rather than surrendered at *not my will*. So too when my father recoiled at testosterone suppression swelling his chest, he felt something true beneath the medical calculus — a grief at warring against his created maleness, for cancer itself is the perversion of created good (Romans 8:21–22) — and the church must recover reverence for the body as God’s handiwork even while showing compassion for the suffering flesh. In the end I offered my father not a solution but a drive, a prayer, and my hands laid on his failing body, and that incarnational presence — leaving our screens and scheduled shifts to make the hell of a drive to Rochester — is how the world will know we are His. When they thank us, the only faithful answer is *that was Jesus*. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity is not the disappearance of the enemy’s pressure but the relocation of my defense, and it is measured not by how eloquently I explain suffering but by how faithfully I wrestle with God inside it. As long as I stood in that hour defending *Seth* — my theology, my competence, my track record from Abigail — I had a handle the demons could grip, and the turn to worship was the turn from self-righteousness to Christ’s righteousness, the same turn Philippians 2 traces in the Lord Himself. My father is being invited into that same journey, from hearing of God by the ear to seeing Him with the eye, and that journey passes directly through the radiation room, not around it. We grow up not by becoming better arguers but by becoming smaller defendants, learning to end every prayer at *not my will but Yours*, until Christ alone is our defense and our worship, not our winning, ends the war. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience here means testing every spirit and every inherited slogan by the exact confession of Scripture — does it ὁμολογέω (ho-mo-lo-GEH-oh) that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh and is God? — and refusing every subtle invitation to make agreement with a false reality, no matter how pious it sounds dressed up as “righteousness on the throne.” It means guarding the house, keeping it not merely swept but filled, and naming honestly the open doors in our own lives before the enemy studies them the way he studied me. It means driving the long road, laying on hands, and speaking the hard true word in love — telling a suffering man he has both the right to argue with God and the duty to actually ask Him — and building twelve hours of systems so that a woman is no longer forced to scream to be heard, because the Kingdom treats every person as an image-bearer and never as a problem to be solved. And when the deliverance or the healing comes, obedience is deflecting the thanks: *that was Jesus.* **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture I am being trained in is patience under the Lord’s timing, reverence for what He withholds, and worship as warfare. He let an hour pass without deliverance to expose what was in me, and He kept my spiritual eyes closed to Satan as a mercy, so I learn to watch, wait, and yield rather than demand sight or speed. To abide is to stay νήφω (NAY-foh) — clear-headed and watchful — seeing in the spirit without gazing where the enemy points, opening the gate for the King and stepping out of His way, then trusting the recording and the fruit more than my own feelings. It calls us to carry the Father’s grief without being destroyed by it, to hold revelation loosely and guard the mysteries He has deliberately kept, to stand in the locked room and truly believe He hears, and to bless the divisions that following the true Light must bring — for the whole matter turns on whether we believe the One who suffered for us will also answer us. ## A Closing Prayer Father in heaven, I come as Your child and Your servant, still trembling from an hour I did not control and aching in my own flesh even as I lift my father’s failing body before Your throne. I thank You that You are the Deliverer and I am not, that when I could not remember my own prayers Your Spirit remembered them for me and prayed with groanings I could not utter. Forgive me for the pride I carried into that room, the quiet lie that I played a part, the seat I sat in that was never mine, and thank You for loving me enough to let the enemy press on that seam until only worship was left. I confess with all of heaven that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh, that He is God, that He did not grasp His own righteousness but poured Himself out unto death, and I make agreement with You against every spirit that will say His name but will not bow to who He is. Fill every swept and empty place in me and in my brother’s house with Your רוּחַ (ROO-akh), seal every doorway the bottle ever opened, and keep him thirty pounds lighter for the rest of his days. I stand as a co-heir under the New Covenant to ask boldly that You would shrink every spot of cancer in my father as You have already begun, that You would guard his breath, his rest, and the very design the enemy seeks to pervert, and teach him to stand alone before You and truly believe You hear. I make agreement with Your will that his ear-knowledge of You would become eye-sight, that in the radiation room he would meet the Man of Sorrows who bore his griefs; I pray too for Andy in his silence, that Your breath would move where no man can. I lay my daily tears on Your altar and surrender the weight of Your grief back into Your hands, for I cannot carry it alone; keep me from the arrogance of the little theologian and preserve the reverent trust that leaves Your mysteries unforced. Guard the koinonia of Your saints from the accuser’s confusion, let every deliverance and every healing point past me to the pierced hands that actually poured out the wine, and in all of it, whether the answer be healing or homegoing, I say with You in the garden — not my will, but Yours be done. To You alone belongs the credit, the kingdom, and the power, forever, in the name of Jesus Christ come in the flesh, crucified, risen, and reigning — for that was Jesus, and Jesus is Yours, and I am His.
