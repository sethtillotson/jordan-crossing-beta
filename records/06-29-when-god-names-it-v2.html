# 06-29 Teaching Reflection: Mercy, Divine Testing, and God’s Dominion Over Love **Title:** 06-29 at 15:06 — When God Names It, He Lords It: Mercy, Dominion, and the Anatomy of a Test **Recorded:** 2026-06-29 15:06:24 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Teaching Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to this teaching unfold in the room around me, something began to press on me that I could not dismiss. The thread being pulled was deceptively simple — mercy, love, testing — but beneath it ran a current of revelation that I am still sitting with. The question being raised was not merely theological but deeply confrontational: do I extend what I so desperately want to receive? And beneath even that question, a deeper one emerged — does God claim jurisdiction over the very things I consider most intimately mine? **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Body of Christ is presently in a season where the language of grace and mercy has been retained while the practice of it has quietly eroded. Saints who have tasted the kindness of God are often the most surprising hoarders of judgment toward others — particularly toward those still in the middle of what they themselves were delivered from. At the same time, the teaching that surfaced about the distinction between temptation and test carries extraordinary weight for disciples who are suffering and interpreting that suffering as either abandonment or divine punishment. If we do not understand what God is doing when He goes silent during an exam, we will either abandon the cross or misread the silence as hatred. And if we do not understand that love itself is something God names and therefore lords, we will treat our affections as sovereign territory that belongs to us alone — a posture Scripture does not support. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, hearing a teaching like this, tends to receive the mercy portion as social commentary — a critique of hypocrisy that resonates because it confirms what most people already suspect about religious communities. The world applauds the exposure of double standards in the Church because it vindicates the skeptic. The part about Abraham and Isaac gets filtered through a psychological or literary lens: it becomes a story about the evolution of religion away from human sacrifice, or a portrait of a father’s devotion, or a cultural artifact from an ancient Near Eastern context that no longer bears direct application. The distinction between test and temptation gets collapsed entirely, because in secular thought, suffering is suffering — there is no meaningful difference between what the devil sends and what God permits. The natural mind cannot conceive of a God who would withhold His voice during the most crucial moment of a son’s formation and call that silence *love*. **The Limitation:** The difficulty is not merely intellectual. It is covenantal. The natural mind has no framework for a God who names things in order to claim dominion over them — who, by the very act of calling something love, establishes Himself as its Lord and therefore its Examiner. To the mind operating apart from the Spirit’s illumination, the idea that God would test what He has taught — and remain deliberately silent during the examination — feels like cruelty dressed in theology. But this perception is bounded by a fundamental misunderstanding of what divine testing actually is: not cruelty, but fidelity to a pedagogy that is older and wiser than any human educational system. The silence of the Father during the test is not the absence of care; it is the fullest expression of it — the refusal to rob the student of the weight-bearing moment that produces the strength only testing can generate. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I listened to the teaching move from the woman turned away at the door — told to go home and change her clothes before she could enter — I found myself genuinely arrested. Not simply by the indictment of religious hypocrisy, but by how precisely it mirrors what James says in chapter two: that judgment without mercy will be rendered to the one who showed no mercy. The brother teaching quoted James 2:13 directly, and I want to sit inside that verse for a moment before moving anywhere else. The Greek word for judgment here is κρίσις (krisis, KREE-sis) — a decisive separation, a moment of final determination. And the word for mercy is ἔλεος (eleos, EH-leh-os) — covenantal lovingkindness, the New Testament echo of the Hebrew חֶסֶד (hesed, HEH-sed). What James is saying is not merely that unmerciful people will receive harsh treatment. He is saying that κρίσις (KREE-sis) is calibrated by ἔλεος (EH-leh-os), and that those who withhold ἔλεος (EH-leh-os) have effectively removed the buffer between themselves and the full weight of divine evaluation. What struck me further was the observation that people who condemn others often end up doing worse than the thing they condemned. The teaching framed this not as cosmic irony but as something God deliberately permits — that from the moment of condemnation, He removes the hedge. And this connects directly to the principle stated in the sermon: that the reason a particular struggle did not come your way is not because you are stronger or more righteous, but because God weighed the burden and determined it would have consumed you. That is a deeply humbling word. The addict on the street is not proof of your superiority; he is proof of God’s precise calibration of what each vessel can bear. Paul says in 1 Corinthians 10:13 that God will not allow us to be tempted beyond what we are able — but the implication running beneath the teaching is that this same principle means the struggles assigned to others were assigned because those people had a way of escape that you, frankly, might not have had. Now the teaching pivots — and this is where I feel the Spirit pressing hardest — to the first occurrence of the word *love* in all of Scripture. Genesis 22:2. Not between a man and a woman. Not between friends. Not even in a direct statement of God’s love for humanity. It appears in God’s address to Abraham: *“Take now your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love.”* The Hebrew word here is אָהַב (ahav, ah-HAHV) — to love, to have deep affection for, to be attached to. And what arrests me is the observation made in the teaching: it was *God* who named it love. Abraham did not label his feeling. God named it. And in naming it, He claimed jurisdiction over it. This is not a small thing. The teaching rightly connected it to the creation narrative, where God forms everything and then calls Adam to name the animals — because naming establishes dominion. What you name, you govern. So when God names Abraham’s attachment to Isaac as אָהַב (ah-HAHV), He is not merely acknowledging a tender emotion. He is asserting sovereign authority over that love. He is saying: *this thing you feel — I define it, and because I define it, I can test it.* And the test was not arbitrary. It was pedagogically precise. To understand the test, we must understand what God had already taught. In Genesis 17, God revealed Himself to Abraham as אֵל שַׁדַּי (El Shaddai, el shah-DYE) — the Almighty, traditionally rendered “God Almighty,” though the root שַׁדַּי (Shaddai, shah-DYE) carries the connotation of the one who is abundantly sufficient, the life-giver. The teaching offered the translation “the double-breasted one” or “the one who gives life,” and while that rendering is contested among scholars, it points toward the same theological reality: El Shaddai is the God who brings forth life where life should be impossible. Abraham had already received this revelation. And in Genesis 17, God had promised that in Isaac — specifically in Isaac — the nations would be blessed. So when God commands the death of Isaac in Genesis 22, a believer who had been paying attention in class should have been able to complete the theological equation: *if Isaac must die, and if nations must be blessed through Isaac, then Isaac must rise.* The writer of Hebrews confirms this explicitly in chapter eleven — Abraham reckoned that God was able to raise Isaac from the dead. This is the architecture of a test. God does not test without first teaching. The examination only follows the curriculum. And this produces in me a searching question: when I enter a season of silence, of pressure, of apparent contradiction — do I have enough of the Word in me to hold the equation together when it looks like the promise is being destroyed? The teaching made this painfully practical: if you could not send away Ishmael in Genesis 21, you could not have killed Isaac in Genesis 22. The lesser obedience — the revision — precedes the greater. God does not give the final exam before the coursework. And He does not speak during the exam, not because He has abandoned the student, but because the teacher’s voice during an examination would invalidate the very assessment the teacher designed. Here I must pause and hold what I offered briefly during the conversation — that this pattern extends to Jesus Himself. The cross was simultaneously a test and the site of a temptation. Those passing by mocked Him, calling Him to come down, to demonstrate power, to prove His identity by the exercise of force — that was the temptation, the enemy’s pressure point designed to pull Him away from the Father’s stated will. But the test was something else entirely. The test was whether He would hold to what He had already prayed in Gethsemane: *“Not My will, but Yours, be done”* (Luke 22:42). The temptation came from outside. The test was the one He had already agreed to from within. And in the moment of deepest testing, Jesus cried from the cross: *“My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?”* (Matthew 27:46). This is a quotation of Psalm 22:1, and what I feel the Spirit pressing here is that this cry was not the language of theological confusion — it was the language of tested covenant. The Son of God, having become sin on behalf of the world (2 Corinthians 5:21), endured the separation that sin produces, the κρίσις (KREE-sis) that sin deserves. The Father’s silence in that moment was not the silence of indifference. It was the silence of the examination room. The test was the test of will — would the Son remain in the Father’s plan even when the Father’s voice had gone quiet? He did. And resurrection was the answer that proved the teaching had been absorbed. What I also feel stirring is the invitation I mentioned during the conversation — connecting this to a question about why Jesus said, *“I go to prepare a place for you”* (John 14:2). That question and this teaching are more connected than they first appear. Jesus going to prepare a place is not merely a logistical statement about heavenly real estate. It is the language of a bridegroom who must endure the period of separation — tested by absence, faithful in the unseen, completing the work the Father assigned before the reunion can occur. The going away is part of the covenant architecture. And His silence between the ascension and the return is not abandonment. It is the faithful completion of a test that holds the promise of every nation blessed in the seed. I want to return to the mercy thread, because it does not stand apart from the testing thread — it is woven through it. The reason we cannot extend mercy is often that we have not yet been brought low enough to recognize that our own standing before God is entirely a product of ἔλεος (EH-leh-os) and nothing else. When God removes the hedge and allows the person who condemned others to face the very thing they judged, He is not being cruel. He is being merciful to them — because the fastest path from condemnation to compassion is experience. The Pharisee does not become the Good Samaritan through more theology. He becomes the Good Samaritan when he has been left on the side of the road himself. Paul’s word in Romans 11:32 comes alive here: *“For God has committed them all to disobedience, that He might have mercy on all.”* The grand design of God is not merely that some receive mercy while others receive condemnation. It is that every vessel passes through the place of recognized need — so that the mercy shown can be extended rather than hoarded. We were not saved so that we could manage a spiritual class system. We were saved so that we could extend what we received to the one still standing outside the door — even in the clothes she came in. ## Implications and Challenges There is a pattern in Western Christianity where mercy is preached loudly in the direction of personal struggles — addiction, mental health, relational failure, financial ruin — but withheld quietly in the direction of theological disagreement, doctrinal immaturity, or lifestyle difference. We have mercy for the man who fell but not for the man who still believes differently about a secondary doctrine. This is inconsistent, and James 2:13 does not grant exemptions based on the category of need. The teaching’s observation that David is everyone’s favorite biblical character — until David is your actual pastor — is a genuine challenge to the performative nature of much modern Bible engagement. We consume Scripture as narrative entertainment while remaining wholly unwilling to apply its most uncomfortable implications to our own relational and ecclesial realities. Familiarity with biblical heroes at a literary distance does not constitute the transformation that only comes from encountering the same God who tested them. The distinction between test and temptation is one of the most practically neglected doctrines in the contemporary Church. When believers cannot distinguish between what the devil is sending and what God is using, they respond to seasons of silence with spiritual panic, theological revision, or outright abandonment of their post. The result is a generation of disciples who are perpetually beginning the race but never crossing into the maturity that only the completed examination can produce. We must also reckon with what the teaching implies about unsolicited comparison of our struggles to others’. To look at someone mired in addiction, poverty, relational chaos, or public failure and infer that their condition reflects their inferior standing before God is not discernment — it is the precise error that Paul addresses in Romans 2:1. You who judge are doing the same things. The only honest response to another’s visible struggle is the quiet acknowledgment that God calibrated that struggle to that vessel with a way of escape included — and your own clean hands reflect His mercy to you, not your merit before Him. Finally, the principle that God names what He lords — and tests what He names — has direct implications for how we hold our closest affections. If God claimed jurisdiction over Abraham’s love for Isaac by naming it, then our loves — our children, our callings, our communities, our dreams — are not exempt from divine examination. This is not a threat. It is an invitation into the kind of faith that can hold a knife above the thing it loves most and know, because of the Word already received, that El Shaddai raises the dead. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity in the Kingdom is not the absence of struggle — it is the capacity to hold the promise and the command in tension long enough for God’s answer to emerge. The mature believer has enough Word stored that when the test comes and the Teacher goes silent, the curriculum carries them through. We grow toward that maturity by treating the Word not as devotional decoration but as active preparation for the examination that is coming. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this calls forth is double: to extend without measure the mercy we have received, and to remain at our posts when the silence of testing feels indistinguishable from abandonment. Neither is passive. Both require the deliberate decision to trust what the Word says over what the moment feels like. The woman at the door deserves the mercy she came looking for, still in the clothes she was wearing when she arrived. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture formed here is one of watchful endurance — staying on the cross when the temptation is to come down, staying with Isaac when the temptation is to find a cheaper substitute, staying in the silence when the temptation is to demand that God speak before the exam is complete. This is the posture of Hebrews 12:1-2 — running with endurance, looking to Jesus, who for the joy set before Him endured the cross and despised the shame. The prophetic community holds that posture together, bearing one another’s burdens, extending the ἔλεος (EH-leh-os) that turns κρίσις (KREE-sis) from terror into grace. ## A Closing Prayer Father, we stand before You as those who have received mercy we did not earn and endured tests we did not fully understand — and we are grateful. You are אֵל שַׁדַּי (el shah-DYE), the God who gives life where life has no business appearing, and every promise still standing in our mouths is standing because You named it and You lord it. We repent, genuinely and without reserve, for every door we have posted a dress code outside of — for every person we turned away because they had not yet been changed when You sent them to us still in need of the change. Forgive us, and give us the mercy-extended heart of those who know they are only standing because the hedge remained. We agree with Your Word over every brother and sister in examination right now — those who hear silence and are reading it as rejection: the silence is the room, and You are the Teacher, and the curriculum You have already given them is sufficient to carry them through. Complete in us the work You began. Let the things You have named in us — the loves, the callings, the covenants — come fully under Your dominion, tested and proven true. We receive Your Word as preparation. We receive Your silence as trust. And we receive Your resurrection as the final answer to every test that looked, for a moment, like the end. To the Father, through the Son, in the power of the Spirit — all authority, all honor, and all glory, now and into the age to come.
