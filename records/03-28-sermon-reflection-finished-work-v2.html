# 03-28 Sermon Reflection: Finished Work of Christ — Faith, Righteousness, and the True Gospel in James 2 -Johnny Chang **Source Discernment:** E — Recorded Sermon/Teaching with live audience interaction and brief post-reflection segments **Personal Meditation: The Work Is Already Finished — Faith, Nakedness, and the Gospel That Pharisees Cannot Preach** ## Opening Observation Something has been pressing on me as I have sat with this teaching — a deep, almost uncomfortable confrontation between what we have been told the Christian life looks like and what the Word of God actually says. The question at the heart of it is deceptively simple: *Who does the work?* I have watched the Body of Christ labor under a misreading of James chapter two for generations, building soup kitchens when the text was calling us to preach resurrection, clothing the outer man when the Scripture was speaking of the nakedness of sin. What I am seeing, the longer I sit with this, is that the confusion is not merely intellectual — it is spiritual, and it runs very deep. **Why This Matters:** This matters profoundly because the gospel itself is at stake. When the saints misread James 2 as a call to social performance and charitable output, they do not merely misapply a text — they subtly reshape the entire architecture of salvation into something that can be earned, measured, and paraded. A Christianity built on works-production rather than Christ’s completed work will inevitably produce either pride or despair: pride in those who perform well, and crushing despair in those who know they fall short. More dangerously still, it produces people who are convinced they are saved because they serve — people who may hear on that final day, *“I never knew you”* (Matthew 7:21, NKJV). The stakes here are eternal, not merely theological, and that is why I cannot treat this lightly. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the vantage point of natural human reasoning, the interpretation of James 2:14–17 as a call to tangible charitable action seems not only sensible but noble. After all, the world respects visible results. A faith that feeds the hungry, clothes the unhoused, and opens soup kitchens on Skid Row is a faith that can be photographed, validated, and shared. The natural mind gravitates toward transactional religion because it already understands cause and effect: *if you do this, you will get that.* It reads “naked” and thinks bare skin. It reads “destitute of daily food” and thinks empty stomachs. This reading feels compassionate, actionable, and socially responsible — and that is precisely what makes it so seductive. The flesh wants a gospel it can produce by effort, because a gospel it cannot produce by effort demands something far more threatening: surrender. **The Limitation:** But there is a ceiling the natural mind simply cannot penetrate on its own. It cannot see past the surface of language into the symbolic architecture that the Holy Spirit has embedded across the 66-book canon. When the natural mind reads “naked,” it does not instinctively travel back to the garden, to Genesis 3:7, where nakedness first entered the human story as the mark of shame, separation, and the knowledge of sin. When it reads “bread,” it does not hear the echo of John 6:35, where Jesus announces, *“I am the bread of life”* (NKJV). The natural mind reads each verse in isolation, as a self-contained instruction manual, and misses the grand symphonic unity the Spirit has woven from Genesis to Revelation. It takes the illumination of the Holy Spirit — not a more sophisticated intellect — to see that Scripture interprets Scripture, and that what sounds like a social program is actually a gospel imperative. ## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation As I listened to this teaching unfold, something arrested me early — a methodological declaration that became a kind of governing principle for everything that followed: *we are not going to say “in my opinion,” “in my perspective,” “what I feel,” or “what I think.” We are going to go to Scripture.* I find myself returning to that anchoring commitment again and again, because it is not merely a rhetorical preference. It is an act of epistemic submission. It is the recognition that my feelings change by the hour, my thoughts are shaped by my wounds and my culture, and my opinions are the product of a mind that Scripture itself describes as needing renewal (Romans 12:2, NKJV). The discipline of letting the Word interpret the Word is not a limitation; it is liberation. The journey into James chapter two begins, as all good scriptural exegesis must, not with the verse in question but with the context that frames it. Verses 15 and 16 introduce a *naked* brother or sister and a person *destitute of daily food.* I had always read this and pictured material poverty — a man in rags, a child without dinner. But the Spirit pressed me to follow the canonical thread, and it led me to Genesis 3:7, NKJV: *“Then the eyes of both of them were opened, and they knew that they were naked; and they sewed fig leaves together and made themselves coverings.”* This is the first appearance of nakedness in the human story, and it is unmistakable in its theological weight. Nakedness here is not a wardrobe malfunction; it is the exposure of the soul before a holy God. It is the recognition of sin, and the immediate, futile human response: *cover it with something you made yourself.* Isaiah 47:3 deepens the canonical confirmation: *“Your nakedness shall be uncovered, yes, your shame shall be seen.”* The connection between nakedness and sin, between exposure and judgment, is not a clever interpretive leap — it is the consistent language of the covenant. When James 2:15 introduces a *naked* brother or sister, it is introducing someone whose sin is not yet covered by the righteousness of Christ. It is a person still wearing fig leaves, still attempting the self-made apron of their own religious performance. And suddenly the whole texture of the passage shifts. James is not writing a social welfare program. He is describing a pastoral and evangelistic failure — the failure to bring the naked sinner the covering that only the gospel provides. Then the second image: *destitute of daily food.* And here the Lord led me to John 6:35, NKJV, where Jesus declares, *“I am the bread of life. He who comes to Me shall never hunger, and he who believes in Me shall never thirst.”* When James speaks of someone without daily food, he is speaking — in the symbolic grammar of Scripture — of someone without Jesus. Someone without the living bread that descended from heaven. Someone who may have religion, may have moral effort, may even have membership in a congregation, but who does not have Christ in the deepest, life-sustaining sense. The hunger James describes is the hunger of a soul not yet nourished by genuine union with the risen Lord. Now I return to verse 16, and I am reading with entirely different eyes. *“And one of you says to them, ‘Depart in peace, be warmed and filled,’ but you do not give them the things which are needed for the body, what does it profit?”* (James 2:16, NKJV). Who is the *“one of you”* James addresses? Look at James 1:1 — James writes to *“the twelve tribes which are scattered abroad”* (NKJV). He is writing into a Jewish context, addressing people who are religious by inheritance, steeped in the traditions of the law, who know the language of righteousness but who — crucially — do not preach Jesus as Messiah. The *“one of you”* who sends the naked and hungry away with a blessing but without the gospel is the ancient Pharisee and his modern equivalent: the religious leader who speaks of repentance without resurrection, who says *“do better”* without offering the One who has already done it perfectly. I am struck by the particular wickedness — if I may call it that with grief rather than condemnation — of a religion that *identifies* the problem without offering the solution. The Pharisee sees the sinner. He knows the commandments. He can articulate where the person falls short. He might even offer kind words, a warm blessing, a *“depart in peace.”* But he does not give them the bread of life. He does not cover their nakedness with the righteousness of Christ. He tells them to stop sinning without showing them the One who has already conquered sin. And so, as the teaching made plain, he saves the body in appearance while leaving the soul to perish. This is the *“works”* James is condemning — not charitable work performed in Christ’s name out of love, but the hollow performance of a religion that has not been animated by genuine faith in the risen Lord. Faith without *these* works is dead — the works being the proclamation of the gospel, the covering of the naked with righteousness, the feeding of the hungry with the bread of life. A religion that produces social programs without resurrection preaching has made the same error as the one who says *“be warmed and filled”* while withholding the thing that actually warms and fills the soul eternally. And now I come to the place where the teaching stopped me entirely — John 17:4, NKJV: *“I have glorified You on the earth. I have finished the work which You have given Me to do.”* Red letters. Jesus speaking. *I have finished the work.* Not *we have begun the work.* Not *you and I together shall complete the work.* He — alone, unassisted, without addition from our performance — has finished it. And the work, as John 17:3 immediately clarifies, was this: *“that they may know You, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent”* — the giving of eternal life through knowledge of the Father and the Son. The work is the gospel. The work is the revelation of the Father through the Son. And Jesus says: finished. Complete. Done. It follows, then, with a kind of holy inevitability, that Hebrews 12:2 would call Jesus *“the author and finisher of our faith”* (NKJV). He is not a co-author awaiting our contribution. He is not a finisher waiting on us to start it. He is both the genesis and the completion of faith itself. This is not a passive observation — it is a radical reorientation. The question is no longer *“what must I produce to demonstrate my faith?”* The question becomes *“do I actually believe that He finished it?”* And the honest answer, when I sit with it quietly, is that much of what passes for Christian living in our age is an implicit denial of this — a life structured around the assumption that the work is ongoing and that our performance is the decisive variable. The treatment of Romans 4:25 here opens something vital: *“who was delivered up because of our offenses, and was raised because of our justification”* (NKJV). Note the precision. He was delivered for our *offenses* — the debt of sin demanded His death. He was raised for our *justification* — the resurrection is not merely proof of His divinity; it is the declaration that the account is settled, that the righteousness has been credited, that the one who believes is now counted just before God. This is the gospel in its most crystalline form. The death addresses the problem; the resurrection delivers the verdict. And the verdict is: *justified.* This is what must be given to the naked and hungry. Not a commandment. Not a moral program. The verdict. What, then, is repentance in this light? The teaching offered an answer that I have been turning over carefully: real repentance is the distrusting of one’s own thoughts and the acceptance of the Word of God. The Greek word μετάνοια (metanoia, meh-TAH-noy-ah) — a change of mind, a turning of the whole interior faculty — is precisely this. It is not primarily an emotional experience of remorse, though grief over sin may accompany it. It is a cognitive and volitional reorientation: I stop trusting what my broken mind produces and I begin trusting what the living Word declares. True sin, understood in this framework, is the exaltation of one’s thoughts above the testimony of God. And true repentance is the reversal — the dethroning of self-generated certainty and the enthronement of divine revelation. The man who stands before God and says *“I am a sinner”* while the blood of Jesus has already washed him clean is, in a very real sense, calling God a liar. The saint’s confession should be what God has declared: *“I am justified. I am righteous. Not because I performed, but because He finished.”* The question of how to judge righteously without hypocrisy — raised in the conversation — finds its answer here as well. Righteous judgment, the teaching clarified, is not the assessment of another’s moral behavior. It is the discernment of whether a person knows Jesus — whether they stand in the righteousness of Christ or are still trying to establish their own. The standard by which we judge is not our own moral performance, which would make us instant hypocrites, but the gospel itself, which stands independent of us. We can say with confidence: *here is what Scripture declares about sin, about the cross, about righteousness* — and we measure ourselves and others by that Word, never by our fluctuating feelings or our selective application of commandments. The log-and-speck passage is a warning against self-righteous moral measurement, not a prohibition against discernment rooted in the gospel. The image of Satan’s *spam mail* has stayed with me as strikingly apt. The enemy’s strategy is not always direct assault; often it is a relentless flood of accusation, hypotheticals, doubts, and *what-ifs* designed to keep the believer perpetually questioning what God has already declared settled. *What if I sin again? What if I fall short? What about Hebrews 10:26?* The saint who knows the finished work of Christ does not need to open that mail. They do not need to engage every piece of accusatory correspondence the enemy sends. They can return to the simple, unshakeable declaration: *He finished the work. I am justified by His resurrection. I am righteous because He did not fail.* This is not moral carelessness — it is theological clarity that actually *produces* godly living, because a person who knows they are beloved and righteous in Christ is freed to love others, not compelled to perform for God’s approval. ---
