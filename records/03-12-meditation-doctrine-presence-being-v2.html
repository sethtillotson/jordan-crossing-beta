# 03-12 Meditation: Doctrine, Presence, and Being Undone Before the Living God # Meditation: Undone at the Altar of His Presence — When Doctrine Collapses Before the Living God ## Opening Observation I find myself sitting with something I said out loud that I almost didn’t recognize as coming from me — and that arrested me more than anything I’ve read in weeks. A brother in Christ pressed me about doctrine, about division, about whether we truly need to think the same things, and somewhere in the middle of that exchange, something cracked open that I am still trying to hold. I cannot get this off my mind: what if the most priestly thing a person can say is not a confession of perfect theology, but an honest admission that they are still trying to live with God in mind — even in the middle of hating everyone? **Why This Matters:** We are living in a moment where the Body of Christ is fracturing along doctrinal lines with increasing ferocity — where the precision of one’s systematic theology has become the currency of spiritual credibility. But what the Spirit is pressing into me right now cuts directly against that grain. If the Kingdom of God is already here — present among the broken, the weeping, the lonely, the undone — then perhaps the most dangerous thing the Church can do is confuse the map for the territory, the doctrine about God for the living encounter with God Himself. This matters now because real people are weeping in real rooms, and what meets them there is not a confession of faith. It is presence. It is mercy. It is the breathtaking righteousness of a Father who dismantles us so that He can fill us. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely human perspective, the conversation I am sitting with reads like an intellectual negotiation — two reasonably thoughtful people trying to reconcile competing values: unity versus precision, love versus truth, community cohesion versus doctrinal integrity. The natural mind is drawn to this kind of binary because it is how human institutions survive. Organizations require shared beliefs to maintain coherence. Psychology tells us that shared doctrine functions as tribal bonding — it creates in-group identity and filters out dissonance. Philosophy, particularly from the Stoic tradition, might commend the discipline of holding correct belief as a prerequisite for virtuous action. And from a purely pragmatic standpoint, one could argue that without some doctrinal framework, communities dissolve into subjective spirituality with no accountability. These are not foolish observations. They are, in fact, the most reasonable conclusions a thoughtful person could reach without the Spirit’s illumination. **The Limitation:** The problem is that this framework, however reasonable, is ultimately self-referential — it circles back to the self as the arbiter of correct understanding. The natural man, as Paul writes in 1 Corinthians 2:14, “does not receive the things of the Spirit of God, for they are foolishness to him; nor can he know them, because they are spiritually discerned” (NKJV). A framework built on doctrinal precision without spiritual encounter will always mistake the structure for the substance. It will produce Pharisees — technically correct, spiritually hollow — who know the law of love but have never been undone by it. The natural mind cannot receive the truth that a man who says “I might hate everyone, but I still try to live with God in mind” is standing in closer proximity to the heart of the New Covenant than many who have memorized the Westminster Confession. ## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation I am seeing something I need to sit with carefully, because it shakes something loose in me. My brother said something in that conversation that I called “the most priestly New Covenant statement” I had heard — and I meant it. He said, in effect, that holiness is not about morality, but about consecration to God. And that even in his servitude — even in the grinding, inglorious reality of trying to serve while still wrestling with his own darkness — he recognized it was still service. I am struck by this because it sounds almost too honest to be spiritual. It sounds too raw. But then I hear Isaiah in the throne room, crying out, “Woe is me, for I am undone! Because I am a man of unclean lips” (Isaiah 6:5, NKJV) — and I realize that undone-ness is not the obstacle to encounter with God. It is the very doorway. The Hebrew word for “undone” in Isaiah 6:5 is דָּמָה (*damah*) — and it carries the semantic weight of being silenced, destroyed, brought to cessation. It is not a dramatic performance of grief. It is the genuine collapse of the self under the unbearable weight of divine holiness. And what arrests me about my brother’s confession — “I might hate everyone, but I still try to live with God in mind” — is that it is its own form of *damah*. It is an honest man standing before God without pretense, without theological costume, without doctrinal armor to hide behind. He is not claiming to have arrived. He is confessing that he is still in motion. And somehow, that posture is holier than a thousand well-constructed sermons on the nature of love. The Spirit is drawing my attention here to the Apostle John’s wild provocation in his first epistle: “If someone says, ‘I love God,’ and hates his brother, he is a liar; for he who does not love his brother whom he has seen, how can he love God whom he has not seen?” (1 John 4:20, NKJV). On the surface, this functions as a corrective — a check against empty piety. But I am sitting with something deeper in it right now. John does not say it is impossible to love God while struggling with love for people. He is dismantling the self-deception of claiming vertical intimacy while practicing horizontal contempt *without awareness*. There is a stunning difference between the man who hates his brother and knows it — who brings that darkness to God in anguished honesty — and the man who hates his brother and wraps it in religious language as if the hatred does not exist. My brother’s confession put him squarely in the first category. And I find myself wondering if that honest, anguished awareness is itself a form of the love John is describing. The Greek word John uses for “love” here is ἀγαπάω (*agapaō*) — the verb form of ἀγάπη (*agápē*) — that stunning, self-emptying, covenant-faithful love that is not rooted in affection or attraction but in will and sacrifice. And I notice something: ἀγάπη is not primarily a feeling. It is an orientation. A direction. A posture of the will that chooses the other even in the absence of emotional warmth. My brother, even in his raw confession of internal struggle, is still choosing — choosing to live with God in mind, choosing to remain in the place of service. That is ἀγάπη in its most primitive and honest form. Not the polished version preached from platforms. The version that crawls. I am also sitting with the distinction my brother drew between doctrine and holiness — and I want to be careful here, because the Spirit is not leading me to dismiss doctrine. He himself conceded: “I think doctrine is essential, but you have to move beyond that if that makes sense.” Yes. It makes breathtaking sense. The Greek word for doctrine is διδαχή (*didachē*) — teaching, instruction — and it is genuinely essential to the Body. But διδαχή was never meant to be the destination. It is, by its very function, a vehicle. It is the finger pointing at the moon. And somewhere in the history of Western Christianity, we began worshipping the finger. We began measuring one another by the precision of our pointing rather than by the radiance of our gaze. What the Spirit is revealing is that when the theology and doctrine “seem so minor in light of the Father’s divine providence” — that is not spiritual immaturity. That is the beginning of wisdom. That is the fear of the Lord breaking through the architecture of our certainty. The concept of holiness itself demands a reclamation here. The Hebrew root is קָדַשׁ (*qadash*) — to be set apart, consecrated, made sacred. It is a relational term before it is a moral one. The call to holiness in Leviticus 11:44, “For I am the LORD your God. You shall therefore consecrate yourselves, and you shall be holy; for I am holy” (NKJV), is not first a call to moral performance. It is a call to relational alignment — to be *qadash* because He is *qadash*. To be set apart because He is set apart. Consecration precedes conduct. Identity precedes obedience. The New Covenant does not change this — it deepens it. The writer of Hebrews draws this into full Christological resolution: we are sanctified — ἡγιασμένοι (*hēgiasmenoi*), made holy, consecrated — through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all (Hebrews 10:10). Our holiness is not generated by our moral performance. It is received through our union with the Holy One. Something else is pressing on me from these past few days of sitting in the presence of broken people. I occupied spaces where others wept. Others occupied spaces where I wept. And in those spaces, I did not bring doctrine. I brought presence. And I am realizing that this is the most ancient form of ministry — the ministry of presence — that predates every systematic theology ever written. It is the ministry of Emmanuel, God-with-us, who did not send a theological treatise to address the human condition but entered into it. The Word became flesh and dwelt — σκηνόω (*skēnoō*), meaning “to tabernacle,” to pitch His tent — among us (John 1:14, NKJV). The divine presence did not keep a safe doctrinal distance from human brokenness. It moved in. It became a neighbor. And in doing so, it revealed the deepest mystery of the Kingdom: that God is not found first in the cathedral of correct belief, but in the valley of contrite hearts. The Kingdom of God — already here, not yet fully visible — this tension is the very engine of what the New Testament calls the “now and not yet” of eschatological reality. Jesus declared in Luke 17:21, “the kingdom of God is within you” (NKJV). The Greek ἐντὸς ὑμῶν (*entos hymōn*) — within you, among you — locates the Kingdom not in a future geopolitical event but in the present relational reality of those who are surrendered to the King. The broken hearts and contrite souls I sat with this week were not on the fringes of the Kingdom. They were its interior. And that realization is what finally unraveled me — because I had been, without fully realizing it, placing theological correctness between myself and the Kingdom I was supposedly studying. I come back again and again to what I said: “Woe is me, for I am undone, dismantled, breaking apart at the seams.” I borrowed Isaiah’s language because it was the only language adequate. And I find it stunning that Isaiah’s undone-ness was not the end of the story but the beginning of his commission. The seraph touched his lips with a coal from the altar — purifying what had been the very source of his shame — and then the voice of the Lord said, “Whom shall I send, and who will go for Us?” (Isaiah 6:8, NKJV). The dismantling was not punishment. It was preparation. The breaking apart at the seams was not disqualification. It was the necessary precondition for genuine sending. I am sitting here wondering if the most Spirit-filled people in any room are not the ones with the most polished theology, but the ones who have been most thoroughly undone by the intimacy of God — and who have allowed that undone-ness to become their commissioning. ## Implications and Challenges Grace and peace to you, beloved — to every sincere seeker who has poured themselves into the study of Scripture, who has given years to theological formation, who loves God’s Word and desires to honor it faithfully. What I am about to offer is not a dismissal of your labor. It is an invitation, drawn from the same Scripture you love, to consider whether some of what we have inherited may have quietly displaced what it was meant to serve. First, the modern Western Church has, in many streams, made doctrinal uniformity the primary measure of spiritual fellowship. We divide over soteriological nuance, over eschatological timelines, over modes of baptism — and in doing so, we have made the secondary things primary and the primary thing — love, presence, and union with Christ — secondary. Scripture does not support this inversion. Jesus declared in John 13:35, “By this all will know that you are My disciples, if you have love for one another” (NKJV). He did not say, “By this all will know” — by your correct articulation of the atonement. The identifying mark of the disciple, before any doctrinal statement, is love. This is not a call to doctrinal indifference. It is a call to doctrinal humility. Second, the institutional conflation of holiness with moral performance has produced a Christianity that is, in many places, more concerned with behavioral conformity than with consecrated union with God. The scriptural witness is unambiguous: holiness is first a gift received through Christ, and then a life lived in responsive consecration. When we reverse this — when we place moral performance at the foundation rather than grace and union — we construct a system of spiritual meritocracy that the New Covenant explicitly dismantles. Galatians 3:3 asks the arresting question: “Having begun in the Spirit, are you now being made perfect by the flesh?” (NKJV). This is a loving warning from Paul that the flesh cannot complete what the Spirit began — and yet so much of modern discipleship culture is built on flesh-driven self-improvement dressed in sanctification language. Third, there is a striking and largely unaddressed tension between Paul’s injunction against believers suing one another in worldly courts — “Dare any of you, having a matter against another, go to law before the unrighteous?” (1 Corinthians 6:1, NKJV) — and the ease with which contemporary believers engage in litigation culture without theological examination. The conversation I sat with this week, about lawsuits and legal strategy, reminded me that the early Church had a radically counter-cultural posture toward personal injury and conflict resolution. Paul’s answer was not “get a better lawyer.” It was “why do you not rather accept wrong? Why do you not rather let yourselves be cheated?” (1 Corinthians 6:7, NKJV). This is not naivety. It is the scandalous economics of the Kingdom — where losing in the world’s court can be winning in God’s. Fourth, the tendency in contemporary Christianity to approach the Kingdom of God as a future event to be anticipated rather than a present reality to be inhabited has left the Church passive in the face of immediate human need. The weeping rooms I occupied this week were not waiting for the Kingdom to come. The Kingdom was already present in every act of faithful presence, every tear witnessed, every loneliness interrupted by love. When theology becomes primarily propositional and future-oriented, it can quietly anesthetize us to the radical demands of present Kingdom life — the demands of showing up, of staying, of being with. “The kingdom of God is within you” (Luke 17:21, NKJV). Not eventually. Now. May grace abound to every heart that receives this — and may every gentle correction be received as the loving surgery it is intended to be, from one broken vessel to another, all of us being made whole by the same Physician. ## What This Means For Us **For Your Spiritual Maturity:** The invitation here is to stop measuring spiritual growth primarily by doctrinal precision and begin measuring it by the quality of our undone-ness before God. True maturity looks like Isaiah in the throne room — not confidently lecturing about the holiness of God, but shattered by the encounter with it. It looks like a man admitting he might hate everyone while still choosing to live God-ward. Maturity is not the absence of struggle. It is the honest bringing of struggle into the presence of God, again and again, without pretense. Let the theology become the ladder, not the destination. Climb it, and then let it go when you reach the rooftop of encounter. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The specific posture this revelation calls us toward is the ministry of presence — deliberate, costly, unhurried presence with those who are broken. This means resisting the cultural urgency to fix, advise, or theologically instruct in moments of grief, and instead learning to simply remain. It means entering the weeping rooms. It means allowing ourselves to be wept with, to be known in our own breaking, to refuse the spiritual isolation that masquerades as strength. Kingdom obedience here looks like showing up — and staying. **For Prophetic Posture:** We are called to be a people who embody the “already and not yet” — who carry the future Kingdom into present brokenness through consecrated presence, sacrificial love, and radical honesty before God. This is not striving. It is yielding to the One who dwells within. Our prophetic posture is not proclamation from a safe distance. It is incarnational nearness — pitching our tent among those who suffer, as Emmanuel pitched His among us. We are sent not to have all the answers but to be the answer that a lonely, broken world is actually asking for: *Is anyone there? Does anyone see me? Am I worth staying for?* The answer the Kingdom gives — through us — is yes. ## A Closing Prayer Lord, I surrender this understanding back to You — every word of it, every flash of insight, every moment of being undone that led me here. You are not impressed with the architecture of my theology. You are not made greater by the precision of my confession. You are simply, breathtakingly, recklessly present — and that presence has destroyed my need to be correct. Thank You for the broken people who let me sit with them. Thank You for the moments I was allowed to weep and was not left alone in it. Thank You for brothers who speak priestly truth without realizing they are priests. Forgive us, the whole of Your Body, for mistaking the doctrine of the Kingdom for the King Himself. Teach us to be undone before You — regularly, honestly, without performance. May we carry this holy *damah* — this silencing before Your majesty — into every room we enter, that those who are already broken might recognize in us the same fracture lines, and find in that recognition the first thread of hope. You are worthy of every dismantled certainty. You are worthy of every surrendered argument. You are worthy of the whole of us — not polished, not perfected, but present and yielded. To the Father, through the Son, by the Spirit — all glory, now and into the ages.
