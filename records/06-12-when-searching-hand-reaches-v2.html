# 06-12 Conversation Reflection: Fallow Ground, Divine Pursuit, and Two Men Who Cannot Walk Away **Title:** 06-12 at 22:31 — When the Searching Hand Reaches Into Darkness, the Fallow Ground Breaks Open, and Two Men Discover They Cannot Walk Away **Recorded:** 2026-06-12 22:31:51 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Conversation Reflection ## Opening Observation As I sat with this long, winding, deeply holy conversation — running from early evening through the pre-dawn hours, winding through divorce papers and ghost hunting and prophetic words and suicidal histories and the inexplicable inability of two broken men to become atheists — something in me kept returning to a single thread beneath all the other threads: the Father was present the entire time, and neither my brother Samuel nor I could escape Him even when the conversation was at its most raw, its most profane, or its most uncertain. What arrests me most is not any single moment but the cumulative weight of an evening that moved from candlelight in a dead man’s room to a pre-dawn prayer vigil, from legal strategy to fallow ground theology, from laughter about a sad-eyed cat to the trembling threshold of genuine encounter with the living God. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Church in our generation has largely lost the grammar for what actually happened in that room and on those calls — the grammar of the wilderness, of broken ground, of divine pursuit through the most unlikely conversational vessels. We have built theological systems that produce either triumphant declarations of unwavering confidence or quiet, ashamed retreat, with almost nothing in between. But the Psalms are full of this in-between place. The prophets lived there. Job camped there for chapters. And Jesus Himself cried from the cross, “My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?” (Matthew 27:46). If the Body of Christ cannot make room for a man on his knees begging to encounter the God he already loves, for a young man carrying a prophetic word he never asked to carry, for two friends who should by every natural reckoning have become atheists and yet cannot — then we have mistaken performance for discipleship, and comfort for covenant. This evening matters because it is a portrait of what genuine Kingdom formation looks like when it happens not in a sanctuary but in a grandfather’s house, over beer and bagels and apple cider and trembling hands and a candle that did something neither of us could explain. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the vantage point of natural reasoning, this long evening of conversation reads as two emotionally scarred young men — one thirty-two, one eighteen — processing shared trauma through the therapeutic medium of conversation, reaching for religious language to give structure to their pain. The legal frustration is legitimate: divorce proceedings are genuinely slow, expensive, and often unjust, and the anger directed at a system that seems to favor whoever spends more is not irrational. The ghost hunting interlude reads as a harmless, slightly drunk detour into nostalgia and spookiness, the kind of thing young men do in old houses, especially houses where someone they loved has died. The prophetic word Brother Samuel produced would be analyzed as unconscious projection — a well-meaning friend generating language that mirrored what he already sensed his companion needed to hear. The candle is physics, not prophecy. The inability of either man to become an atheist is simply the psychological gravity of a shared identity, a coping mechanism dressed in theological clothing. And Brother Samuel’s suicidal ideation — alongside the speaker’s own — would be framed as the predictable consequence of religious guilt, early marriage, failed expectations, and unresolved grief. From this vantage point, the prescription is therapy, financial planning, emotional regulation, and perhaps some distance from belief systems that seem to compound the weight they carry. **The Limitation:** The problem with that framework is that it cannot account for what it cannot see. The natural mind possesses genuine capacity to analyze behavior, trace causality, and identify patterns, but it operates entirely within the dimension of the visible and the explainable. It has no instruments for measuring what actually moved through that room when my brother called upon the spirits of the dead and was met not with his grandfather but with something that communicated an unmistakable *get out* — something that did not feel like comfort but like a presence that did not want to be examined too closely. It cannot explain why the word Brother Samuel wrote — with spelling fractured and letters tumbling out in real time — landed on the exact interior territory I had been bringing before the Lord every morning in private lament, without Brother Samuel having been present for those prayers. It cannot explain why every sincere attempt one of these men made to disprove the reality of Christ resulted instead in new discovery of the Spirit. And it has no category for the fact that two men who by every natural ledger should be atheists — who have walked through fraud, substance abuse, failed marriage, financial ruin, and the gravitational pull of despair — were instead sitting together at sunrise, invoking the name of Jesus over one another with authority and expectation rather than shame. The Kingdom of God operates precisely through the things the natural mind would dismiss, and the most honest theology either of these men spoke all evening was this: *I cannot explain why I am still here, still in this, still unable to walk away.* ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with the full arc of this evening — from the earliest conversation about divorce papers and legal positioning to the final exchange about the fear of the Lord just before sleep — the first thing the Spirit presses on me is the moment my brother took a candle into the room where his grandfather died and said: *“Any spirits which possess this home, I make contact now.”* I felt something tighten in me even as I was present in that moment, and what I said was what I believed to be true: when you go searching for the spirits of the sleeping, the counterfeits come in. The Scripture is not ambiguous about the state of the dead. Ecclesiastes 9:5 says plainly: *“The living know that they will die; but the dead know nothing.”* This is not a cruel statement — it is a protective one. The dead are not wandering hallways waiting to be contacted. They are held in the hands of the One who made them. And the Law was so severe about mediums and spiritists precisely because divine intelligence understood what actually happens when the door is opened. Leviticus 19:31 says, *“Give no regard to mediums and familiar spirits; do not seek after them, to be defiled by them.”* The point is not that the spirits of the dead are demons — the point is that the space opened by seeking the dead is filled by something other than the dead. The שֵׁדִים (shedim, sheh-DEEM) of Psalm 106:37 — entities that receive what is offered to the dead — are not the spirits of the deceased. They are opportunistic powers who accept the invitation. And what my brother encountered in that room was not his grandfather. It was something that recognized an open door. And what it communicated was not comfort, not guidance, not the warmth of a man who loved his grandson — it was a forceful sense of *leave me alone.* Genuine love does not terrorize. The Holy Spirit, when He moves, produces conviction that draws near — not a fear that drives away. And so I said what I believed: *he’s in you, he’s not out there.* The grandfather my brother loves — the wisdom, the warmth, the presence that shaped him — is not trapped in that house. Whatever was good and true and loving about that man was put there by the image of God in which he was made. And the God who made him is not locked in a room. What I want to see clearly now is the connection between the ghost hunting and the divorce conversation — because they are not as separate as they first appear. Both emerge from the same source: grief, displacement, and the desperate human need to feel like something is in our control when everything has slipped out of it. The legal bravado — the posture of a man who has decided to run the courts — is the same energy as the candle in the haunted room. Both are attempts to impose order on chaos. Both reach for power that is not ours to hold. And both, if pressed too hard, will cost more than they return. What I said about lawyers is true from hard experience: the moment you make it about winning, you have already begun to lose in a different currency. Proverbs 17:14 says, *“The beginning of strife is like releasing water; therefore stop contention before a quarrel starts.”* The image is of a breach in a dam — פּוֹטֵר מַיִם (poter mayim, poh-TEHR MY-eem) — once the water breaks through, it cannot be re-contained at will. The legal machine, once engaged in earnest as a weapon rather than a last resort, does not stop where you want it to stop. It extracts from both parties indiscriminately. And the one most committed to winning is often the one who ends the process most depleted. But what the Spirit kept returning me to, even through the legal complexity, was the deeper architecture of what God was doing in Brother Samuel beneath the noise of the exchange. What I was watching was a young man learning — in real time, through a very worldly set of circumstances — what it actually means to be still and let the weight of another person’s choices fall where they must. There is a Hebrew word for this: חָכְמָה (chokmah, KHOK-mah) — wisdom in the Old Testament sense, not merely intellectual but the practiced skill of aligning one’s actions with God’s ordered governance of time and consequence. The wise son in Proverbs is not the one who wins arguments; he is the one who knows when to speak and when to be silent, when to press and when to wait. Samuel did not know he was practicing חָכְמָה (KHOK-mah) that night. He was just trying to get through a difficult situation. But that is often how the Spirit works — through the raw, unpolished, sometimes-profane texture of real life, training sons and daughters in things they would never learn in a classroom or a church service. Proverbs 20:22 says, *“Do not say, ‘I will recompense evil’; wait for the LORD, and He will save you.”* That is not passivity. It is a positioned stillness that requires more strength than most reactive people will ever know. And when Samuel realized, partway through our exchange, that the ball was back in her court and he did not have to chase it — something settled in him that went deeper than legal strategy. That settling was the Spirit’s work. The deeper conversation that followed moved somewhere I did not anticipate, and as I sat with it, I recognized the Spirit had been preparing both of us for this moment long before either of us knew we needed it. When Samuel described the repetitive, crushing thoughts he had been carrying — measuring himself against the man who replaced him, cycling through comparisons of worth — something in me recognized that particular darkness with a recognition that only comes from having lived inside it. What the Spirit pressed on me was not a therapeutic response but a prophetic one: this is what happens when a man attempts to define his value through horizontal comparison rather than vertical identity. The enemy’s oldest strategy against the sons of God is not brute force. It is the relentless invitation to measure yourself against the wrong standard entirely. The image that rose in me was from Hosea 10:12 — the prophetic call to break up the unplowed, fallow ground: נִיר (nir, NEER), the unbroken, untilled soil. This is not dead ground. It is ground that has never been opened to receive the seed. There is richness beneath it. But the surface has hardened through seasons of being walked over, and until the plow of God’s sovereign design cuts through that crust, no seed will take root. Samuel’s marriage, his divorce, his grief, his years of suicidal ideation — I am seeing now that none of this was the enemy winning. This was the plow. I must be honest about my own נִיר (NEER) as well, because it would be dishonest to write this as though I were simply the wiser man in that conversation. There was a season — not long ago — when I had run every dimension of my life into the ground. The businesses failed. There was no food in the house. My wife went to bed hungry. And in that starvation, at the lowest and most humiliating ledge I had ever stood on, I heard the Holy Spirit whisper to me to open the Scriptures. That whisper was not the reward for faithfulness. It was the mercy extended to a man who had nothing left to offer. The plow had done its work, and the seed finally found soil. What I said in that conversation about my wife — that she has been the grace of God embodied toward me in my lowest moments — is something I must say with the full vocabulary it deserves. The Hebrew concept of חֶסֶד (chesed, KHEH-sed) — the covenantal lovingkindness of God that persists beyond deserving — is what I see when I look at her faithfulness through those seasons. She did not leave when leaving would have been entirely reasonable. She stayed. Her prayer and mine reach the same Father. The חֶסֶד (KHEH-sed) she carried toward me in those years was not her achievement. It was God’s kindness mediated through a woman who knew how to kneel. As the conversation pressed into the earliest morning hours, Samuel asked a question I want to honor carefully: *“Why are we both not atheists right now?”* This is not the question of a man who has lost his faith. It is the question of a man who is stunned that his faith remains. And that distinction matters enormously, because the Psalms are full of men who prayed from precisely that posture. The psalmist in Psalm 73 nearly lost his footing entirely — *“My feet had almost stumbled; my steps had nearly slipped”* (Psalm 73:2) — until he entered the sanctuary of God and his perception was re-ordered. What my brother and I were doing in that conversation, whether we named it as such or not, was functioning as one another’s sanctuary. My brother said he had tried, out of love and not hatred, to prove me wrong — to disprove the reality of what God was doing — and that every attempt ended not in refutation but in new revelation. I recognized Gamaliel’s experiment being run from the inside: *“If this plan or this work is of men, it will come to nothing; but if it is of God, you cannot overthrow it”* (Acts 5:38–39). The thing that keeps disproving its own disproof is not a human construction. And this brought me to ἐκλογή (eklogē, ek-lo-GAY) — divine election, the choosing that Paul roots entirely in the character and initiative of the Caller rather than the worthiness of the called (Romans 9:11). *“Out of everybody I know, if anyone should be atheists, it’s us.”* My brother is not wrong by any human ledger. But the divine economy does not operate by human ledger. *“God has chosen the foolish things of the world to put to shame the wise, and God has chosen the weak things of the world to put to shame the things which are mighty”* (1 Corinthians 1:27). The very disqualification we carry is the precise qualification for the kind of glory He intends to display through vessels that have no other explanation for their transformation. The prophetic word my brother wrote — fractured in its spelling, tumbling onto the page in real time — carries something I need to honor carefully. He did not compose it. He was compelled to write it while I was still speaking. And in it, the line that most arrested me was this: *Let it be as in Egypt as it is for you.* I knew immediately what it meant, though it took a breath to find words for it. Egypt in the prophetic imagination of Scripture is not merely geography. מִצְרַיִם (Mitzrayim, mitz-RAH-yim) — the Hebrew name itself evokes constriction, being pressed on both sides. The Exodus was not only a physical departure. It was a rupture of identity, a dying to the self formed under Pharaoh’s economy. The exterior exodus has happened for me. But the interior exodus is ongoing. There are still parts of me that remain in Egypt spiritually — shaped by self-reliance, by the old craving for substances that once felt like control, by the subtle heresy that my own effort or ingenuity can secure what only He provides. The word came as confirmation, not condemnation — *you are heard; I am with you every step of the way* — but with a clear implication: the lament about poverty, the cry that this poverty is not my raiment, has been received. And the receiving comes with a call to deeper consecration, not just continued petition. The word my brother received also confirmed something the Spirit had been pressing about my calling — the word φίλος (philos, FEE-los): friend. Jesus said in John 15:15, *“No longer do I call you servants, for a servant does not know what his master is doing; but I have called you friends, for all things that I heard from My Father I have made known to you.”* The distinction from δοῦλος (servant/slave) is one of access to the master’s counsel. A friend is brought into the confidence of the one who leads. The prophetic identity confirmed through my brother — prophetic intercessor — is not the identity of one who performs an office. It is the identity of one drawn into the friendship of the Son, who hears because he has been granted access to the Father’s counsel. And I must name what also pressed through regarding my own Nicodemus tendency: I came to this knowing and this intimacy by night, in secret, in the prayer closet. But the arc of Nicodemus matters — from midnight secrecy in John 3 to cautious defense in John 7 to public witness at the crucifixion itself (John 19:39), when every other disciple had fled. The Lord told me coming down from a season in San Francisco: do not remain in the posture of Nicodemus’s old self. The intercession at my altar is holy. But it can become a refuge for flesh that does not want to pay the public price. There will come a moment when He says: step into the light. And the wilderness is the preparation for that step. The מִּדְבָּר (midbar, mid-BAR) — wilderness — shares a root with דָּבַר (davar, dah-VAR), meaning spoken word. The wilderness is, at its root, the place of speaking. It is where God speaks to the heart. Hosea 2:14: *“I will allure her, will bring her into the wilderness, and speak comfort to her.”* The מִּדְבָּר (mid-BAR) is not empty — it is saturated with voice. And what I have been discovering in my own wilderness is that He is speaking even when I cannot audibly hear Him. I know it by the fruit. I know it by convergence. I know it by the fact that a prophetic word written through my brother’s uncertain hands opened two books in me in a single hour. The silence Samuel was experiencing — the long quiet between the felt presence and the next moment of encounter — is not the silence of an absent God. It is the silence of a God who was asking His son: *Can you trust Me when you cannot feel Me?* The graduation of miracles moves from the spectacular to the sustainable, from the felt to the trusted, from the encounter to the Word that endures when the encounter has faded. And what Samuel was pressing toward in prayer — asking God to show Himself, not metaphorically but really, with the full weight of his longing — was precisely the cry the Father had been waiting to hear. The Hebrew root יָרֵא (yārēʾ, yah-RAY) — the fear of the Lord — does not primarily mean cowering dread. It means overwhelming awe in the presence of One who is categorically beyond you. It is the feeling Isaiah described when he said, *“Woe is me, for I am undone!”* (Isaiah 6:5). You cannot conjure יָרֵא (yah-RAY) through willpower. It comes when the Living God draws near enough that your smallness becomes undeniable. And I believe Samuel is closer to that moment than he knows. What Samuel said in his prayer — *“I honor You with my lips, but I do not honor You with my soul”* — is not a prayer of unbelief. It is a prayer of devastating self-awareness. And it is almost the exact indictment that Isaiah spoke over Israel, which Jesus later quoted directly (Matthew 15:8, quoting Isaiah 29:13). The stunning difference is that in Isaiah and in Matthew, that indictment comes from God about a people who do not know it is true of them. Samuel was saying it about himself, to God, voluntarily. That is not hypocrisy. That is the beginning of repentance. The Greek word behind conviction is ἐλέγχω (elegchō, el-ENG-kho) — to expose, to reprove, to bring into the light what was hidden. The work of the Holy Spirit described in John 16:8 is precisely this. What Samuel was asking for — without knowing the vocabulary — was the full weight of the Spirit’s convicting work. That is one of the most spiritually mature prayers a man can pray, even when it emerges from a place of desperation rather than theological sophistication. And what I said to him about τεηιλλαη — תְּהִלָּה (tehillah, teh-hil-LAH), the declaratory praise offered as proclamation in the darkness — was not a technique. It was a theology. To praise God from the floor, with honest desperation, is not weakness. It is warfare. It is the praise that Paul and Silas offered at midnight in prison (Acts 16:25), the praise that confused Jehoshaphat’s enemies (2 Chronicles 20:21–22). And God inhabits that kind of תְּהִלָּה (teh-hil-LAH) as a dwelling place (Psalm 22:3). The image of the borrowed vessel — which Samuel named and which rang deeply true — carries the full weight of the New Covenant theology of stewardship. We do not own our bodies, our stories, our houses, our marriages, our ministries, or our grief. Paul uses the image of clay jars — ὀστράκινα σκεύη (ostrakina skeuē, os-TRAK-ee-nah SKYOO-ay) — deliberately fragile, deliberately ordinary vessels, so that the surpassing power would be seen to belong to God and not to us (2 Corinthians 4:7). Samuel looked around his grandfather’s house and felt the vertigo of inheriting a story he did not write. But the Spirit was pressing something else: *this is now your chapter.* His grandfather’s story was written in that house. His great-grandfather’s story was written in that house. The house has never sheltered anyone who had it easy. That is the point. The house is a monument not to comfort but to faithfulness through suffering — and Samuel’s ugly chapter belongs in that lineage just as surely as any other. The image of the wick that my brother raised carries something I cannot set aside. A wick does not generate its own light. A wick is, by itself, a piece of cord — ordinary, unremarkable, combustible. Its entire function depends on the flame applied to it from outside itself. But once lit, the wick does something remarkable: it draws the oil upward through itself by capillary action, sustaining the flame through a kind of continuous self-giving. Jesus said, *“You are the light of the world”* (Matthew 5:14) — but He did not say we are the source. The menorah in the tabernacle was never lit by the priests; it was lit by the Lord, and the priests’ task was to tend it, to trim the wicks, to ensure the oil was present. My intercession, my poverty, my lament, my daily carrying of weight to the altar — these are not evidence of defeat. They are the very wick-function I was designed for. The oil is His. The flame is His. My task is to remain in position. And the weight I described — being able to move entire nations and battles with just the weight given in my chest — is not self-elevation. It is the testimony of what suffering consecrated to prayer actually produces. The Church has largely outsourced intercession to a specialized few and treated it as supplemental ministry rather than the priestly core of the Body’s function. But every believer is called into the royal priesthood — βασίλειον ἱεράτευμα (basileion hierateuma, bah-SIL-ay-on hee-er-AH-tyoo-mah) — and the intercession forged in personal suffering, poverty, doubt, and perseverance is not a burden to minimize. It is the oil that feeds the wick. As I listened to Brother Samuel describe his terror about carrying the prophetic word that moved through his hands, I recognized the posture immediately. Jeremiah cried: *“His word was in my heart like a burning fire shut up in my bones; I was weary of holding it back, and I could not”* (Jeremiah 20:9). The prophetic call is not chosen because it feels good. It is carried because it cannot be put down. The weight my brother felt was not pathology but encounter. The trembling before speaking, the holy terror of discovering that something moved through his hands that did not originate in his mind — this is not the beginning of delusion but of consecration. The candle that did something neither of us could explain is something I will not over-explain, because the Lord Himself seemed deliberate about keeping it on the edge of doubt. And I said it in the moment: He keeps you in doubt for His purposes. Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 12:9 that the Lord’s strength is made perfect in weakness — and that is not merely a comfort, it is a structural principle. God’s power operates through vessels that are not self-certain, because the self-certain vessel inevitably begins to take credit for what moves through it. The flickering and stilling of that flame was God answering my brother’s terrified prayer with the same gentle ferocity He used when He answered Elijah — not in the wind, not in the earthquake, not in the fire, but in the קוֹל דְּמָמָה דַקָּה (qol demamah daqah, kol dem-ah-MAH dah-KAH) — the voice of thin silence, the sound of gentle stillness (1 Kings 19:12). Or perhaps, more precisely, in the still small flame. And then, at nearly five in the morning, somewhere between laughter about a sad-eyed reservation cat and the threshold of sleep, something holy broke through one final time. Samuel said he was scared. And what came out of my mouth was not a prepared teaching. The fear of the Lord is not terror — it is reverence in His sovereignty. The יִרְאָה (yir’ah, yeer-AH) that awaits genuine encounter with the living God is not the endpoint. It is the beginning, the threshold, the door swinging open. Isaiah’s encounter in the sixth chapter begins with ruin — *“Woe is me, for I am undone!”* — but the seraph comes not to confirm his destruction but to press a coal from the altar to his lips, and the guilt is taken away, and the sin is purged, and the sending begins. John on Patmos falls as dead at the feet of the glorified Christ (Revelation 1:17) and the right hand of Jesus is placed upon him: *“Do not be afraid; I am the First and the Last.”* The fear produced by genuine encounter is not the endpoint — it is the threshold. Moses, who spoke with God face to face as a man speaks with his friend (Exodus 33:11), still pressed for more — *“Please, show me Your glory”* (Exodus 33:18). The psalmist who penned the twenty-seventh psalm had clearly known the presence of the Lord, and yet the singular cry of his heart remained: *“that I may dwell in the house of the Lord all the days of my life, to behold the beauty of the Lord”* (Psalm 27:4). And this hunger, I am seeing, is actually a mercy — because the soul that has stood before the living God and been genuinely marked by that standing is no longer interested in the substitutes. What I said to Samuel is true: he will spend the rest of his life trying to get back to that face. That is not a burden. It is the most beautiful description of what the rest of life can be. The deepest thing I want to say about the whole arc of this evening is this: grief is not a spiritual weakness. The longing my brother felt in that room where his grandfather died — the longing to hear the voice that has gone quiet — is sacred. It is an echo of the longing every human heart carries for the world that was meant to be, the world where death does not steal the people we love. And Jesus did not dismiss that longing. He wept at a tomb (John 11:35). He wept knowing He was about to raise the man inside it. He wept because the grief of the people around Him was real, and He honored it before He moved against it. That is the posture I want to carry into every conversation like this one — not dismissiveness, not theological superiority, but the willingness to sit in the grief first, and then speak the truth that is stronger than the grief. The Holy Spirit did not leave that room. He did not leave the call. He showed up in the middle of the mess — imperfect, late-night, over beer and bagels and apple cider and a sad-eyed cat — and He pressed, sometimes gently, sometimes with a kind of urgency that makes your hair stand up, in the direction of truth. And the truth that was pressing through every corner of that long evening was this: *you are held. He is with you. The dead are in His hands. The living are not forgotten. And two men who should by every natural account have walked away discovered, one more time, that they cannot.* ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation presses is against the widespread abandonment of a coherent theology of the dead. The modern Western church has largely replaced genuine doctrine here with a vague, sentimental assurance that everyone who dies is now a guardian angel available for conversation, sending signs through license plates and flickering lights. This is not comfort — it is a counterfeit of comfort that leaves people perpetually reaching into a space where predatory spiritual entities are far more active than our grandparents. My brother’s longing in that room was sacred. The boundary in Scripture is there precisely because God knows what grief will reach for when left unguarded. We owe the Body of Christ a theology of death that is both honest about its weight and victorious about its defeat — not a sentimental workaround that leaves the saints without a map. The second challenge is to those who have learned to treat patience as a spiritual concept while practicing panic as a relational habit. Many of us can quote “wait upon the LORD” while simultaneously making five reactive moves out of fear. Samuel was tempted to preemptively file a no-response claim that would have handed the advantage back to the opposition. Real patience is not the absence of activity; it is the discipline of withheld reaction until wisdom says it is time. The Body of Christ needs to be discipled into this, not merely encouraged by it. The escalation impulse — legal, relational, spiritual — is the פּוֹטֵר מַיִם (poh-TEHR MY-eem) of Proverbs 17:14: once the water breaks through, it cannot be re-contained at will. We owe our brothers and sisters the tools to recognize that moment before it arrives. The third challenge is to the tendency to treat youthful suffering as a disqualifier for spiritual authority. Samuel is eighteen, divorced, grieving, and has contemplated ending his life more times than he could count. The Church’s impulse is to triage him into a recovery group rather than recognize him as a man being forged. But Scripture’s consistent pattern is to take the youngest, most unlikely, most socially disqualified candidates and give them the weight of God’s message. The ἐκλογή (ek-lo-GAY) does not consult demographics. If our churches only celebrate testimonies that are palatable, we will miss the most powerful demonstrations of the gospel walking through our doors — and we will miss the Samuels entirely. God will not. The fourth challenge is to the compartmentalization of the spiritual from the practical. There is a widespread assumption in Western Christianity that God is primarily concerned with our prayer life and our Sunday attendance, and that the messy legal and financial and relational details of daily life are ours alone to manage. But Scripture does not support that compartmentalization. The wisdom literature of Israel — Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Job — is entirely concerned with how the fear of the LORD shapes everyday decision-making. The Spirit of God is as present in a conversation about divorce papers as He is in a Sunday morning altar call. We do our brothers and sisters a disservice when we fail to help them see this. The fifth challenge is to the prosperity-adjacent version of prophetic ministry that promises gifting without cost. My brother’s 4:00 AM terror when the word moved through him was not a malfunction — it was the appropriate response to genuine encounter. We should be concerned when prophetic voices carry no trembling, when gifts are worn as credentials rather than crosses. The weight my brother felt is the same weight that kept Jeremiah from ever being comfortable in his calling. If we are training the saints toward a prophetic ministry that feels exciting rather than costly, we are training them for something other than the biblical pattern. Finally, a word I must press on myself as well: those given influence over others in moments of vulnerability carry a responsibility to steward that influence with increasing sobriety. In this conversation, I was functioning as spiritual friend and counselor to my brother, and there is genuine value in that. But there were also moments where the conversation ventured into raw, unfiltered territory that would not always honor the Lord. I say this not to condemn what happened — God is gracious, and He works through the imperfect — but to acknowledge that iron sharpens iron (Proverbs 27:17) only to the degree that the sharpening leaves both men more like Christ, not just more strategically aware. The call to maturity here is not the call to be perfect. It is the call to pursue increasing sobriety in the stewardship of sacred space. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity is revealed not in how we perform during worship, but in how we respond when we are wronged, when the system fails us, when grief pushes us toward doors we would not otherwise open, and when patience costs something real. What this long evening calls us toward is a maturity that can sit in the middle of betrayal, legal complexity, family fear, a dead man’s room, and the inexplicable inability to become an atheist — and say: *I know where the ball is, and I know that I do not have to chase it right now. I know the dead are in His hands. I know the living are not forgotten.* That kind of maturity is forged, not given. It requires real situations, real stakes, and the willingness to let the Spirit instruct us even through the raw and the ordinary. True maturity also recognizes that doubt driving us to prayer is not the opposite of faith — it is one of faith’s most productive forms. The antidote to doubt is not more certainty. It is more prayer, more surrender, more watching God show up. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Kingdom obedience in this season looks like gathering evidence without manufacturing it — in legal matters and in spiritual ones. It looks like being still without being disengaged. It looks like refusing to open doors into the unseen world out of grief, however sacred the longing is. It looks like beginning to write — or to speak, or to pray — without waiting for the economics to align first. It looks like telling our covenant partners, with the full vocabulary they deserve, that they have been the grace of God embodied toward us in our lowest moments. And it looks like showing up for a brother who is hurting, staying in the mess, speaking the true thing when it needs to be spoken, and trusting the Holy Spirit to land it where it needs to land — even through a mouthful of peanut butter bagel, even at five in the morning, even through a sad-eyed cat and a flickering candle and two men who cannot explain why they are still here except to say: the grip of divine love is not of our making and is not ours to break. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic community must learn to hold the tension between the hidden altar and the public door, between the midnight encounter and the noonday witness, between the trembling that precedes the commissioning and the boldness that the blood of Jesus makes available in the Holiest Place (Hebrews 10:19–22). We must guard the doctrine of the dead — not as theological gatekeeping but as loving guardianship, spoken with the gentleness of one who also knows what it is to miss someone so much that candlelight in an old room feels like the only remaining warmth. We must cultivate the long obedience that waits for God’s timing rather than forcing encounter or manufacturing confirmation. We must pray with authority, release the outcome to sovereignty, and trust that the One who hears has already begun to move before the prayer was finished. And we must stay ready to walk through any door He opens — because we have already decided, in the quiet and the dark and the doubt, that the answer is yes. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who has sat with a brother through a long and unexpected evening — through grief and legal frustration and ghost-hunting and divorce papers and suicidal histories and prophetic words and trembling candles and the final, unexpected grace of a word about the fear of the Lord at the threshold of sleep — and I receive what You were doing in all of it, even the parts that were imperfect, even the parts that wandered into territory You would not fully endorse. I thank You that You did not leave that room. I thank You that Your Spirit moves through the fog, through the laughter and the frustration and the poorly-aimed invocations, and that when the shiver ran and the hair stood up and the word came through trembling hands, it was not the enemy who had the final word — it was You, pressing through with the unmistakable signature of truth. I ask You now, Father, on behalf of my brother Samuel — that You would meet him in the full weight of his loss, his legal exhaustion, his anger, his hunger for Your face, his terror at the calling beginning to press through him — and be to him what no grandfather’s ghost could ever be: the living Presence that does not go silent, does not grow cold, and is not trapped in any room. Guard the doors, Lord — in that grandfather’s house, in his legal proceedings, in the midnight hours when grief reaches for the wrong kind of comfort. Let the interior exodus that is still underway in both of us continue its work, killing in us what belongs to Egypt, and carrying forward only what belongs to the promise. I stand as Your servant, a co-heir of the Kingdom under the blood of Jesus Christ, making agreement with Your will between the heavens and the earth: that the living turn to the living God, that the grief of the broken-covenanted find its healing in the One who bore every breach upon the cross, that the wicks You have prepared — cracked, ordinary, combustible — be lit again by Your flame and sustained by Your oil and give light to everyone who is still in the dark. To the Father, through the Son, by the Spirit — be all glory, now and into the age to come.
