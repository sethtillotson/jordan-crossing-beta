# 07-07 Call Reflection: Covenant Love, Pregnancy, and the Holiness of Ordinary Life **Title:** 07-07 at 14:52 — The Ordinary Holiness of Waiting Together **Recorded:** 2026-07-07 14:52:52 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I am sitting with something tender and unhurried — a phone call between a husband and his wife, ordinary in its texture, yet quietly sacred in what it carries. There is hunger, there is a baby growing, there is a midwife assigned, there is a name already spoken over the child — Elijah — and there is the gentle, sometimes humorous negotiation of two people learning to love each other well through inconvenience and sacrifice. Something arrests me here: the holiness of ordinary covenant life, lived out in the language of cinnamon rolls and ultrasound dates and waiting for Papa to come home. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ has often been taught to search for the sacred in the spectacular — in signs, in grand prophetic declarations, in mountaintop moments. But Scripture does not confine the holy to the spectacular. The covenant of marriage, the gift of new life, the discipline of self-denial for the sake of another, the simple act of a husband driving home to a hungry wife — these are the very fabric of Kingdom life. If we cannot see God in the phone call, in the cinnamon roll, in the name whispered over an unborn son, we will miss the God who fills all things. This reflection matters because the saints must learn to sanctify the mundane, to recognize that faithful covenant love — even in its most ordinary expressions — is a living theology, a sermon preached in real time. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, if it stumbled upon this conversation, would see nothing more than a domestic exchange — logistical, medical, even slightly comedic in places. It would categorize it as private, inconsequential, perhaps endearing but certainly not spiritually meaningful. The world’s framework for significance runs on visibility, scale, and measurable impact. A husband calling his pregnant wife to ask about pastry preferences and medical appointments does not register on that scale. The secular imagination, even when it honors love, tends to romanticize it through cinematic peaks and dramatic gestures — not through the quiet fidelity of an hour-long drive home and a promise to handle the litter box. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind, for all its observation, is blind to the grammar of covenant. It cannot read the spiritual language embedded in a man who listens carefully to his wife’s medical instructions and simply says, *I will wait for you — not long.* It cannot perceive that when a child’s name — Elijah — is already on the lips of his parents before he has yet drawn breath, something prophetic is already in motion. The flesh-governed mind sees inconvenience; the Spirit-illumined mind sees the formation of a household. What the natural eye reads as small talk, the Spirit recognizes as two image-bearers learning, one conversation at a time, to lay down their own preferences for the sake of one another and for the life growing between them. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I listen to this call — to Mama’s voice asking when Papa will be home, to the easy way they negotiate the cinnamon roll situation with genuine care for what she actually wants — I find myself drawn into something the New Testament does not treat as peripheral. The covenant of marriage is, in Paul’s language to the Ephesians, a μυστήριον (mystērion, mys-TAY-ree-on) — a sacred mystery, a revelation hidden in ages past now disclosed in Christ. Paul says plainly that when a man leaves father and mother and cleaves to his wife, he is enacting something that speaks of Christ and the Church. This is not poetry layered over marriage. This is the actual grammar of the institution — it was always a living icon of something eternal. What I am hearing in this phone call, then, is two people inhabiting that icon. Mama is hungry; Papa is on his way. There is something almost eucharistic in the simplicity of it — the one who is going comes back bearing bread for the one who waits. I do not press this too hard, but I do not dismiss it either. The Spirit has a way of hiding the architecture of the Kingdom inside the texture of daily life, and the discerning ear learns to hear it. The name Elijah — יֵלִיָּה (Eliyahu, el-ee-YAH-hoo) — meaning *my God is Yahweh* — is not yet in the world, but he is already being planned for, already prayed over in posture if not in explicit prayer, already the reason a mother is being watchful about her body and a father is willing to exercise patience. This is covenant formation. A name that declares the lordship of Yahweh is already being carried by a family that is, in these small daily exchanges, practicing what that name will one day mean. The child who is coming is not arriving into a vacuum. He is arriving into a household that has already been learning, one phone call at a time, what it looks like to serve one another. The medical details in this call are not incidental. Mama is carrying a child in what the medical community has categorized as a standard-risk pregnancy, but there are still careful instructions — rest, restraint, attentiveness to the body’s signals. There is something here about the discipline of stewardship. The body that bears new life requires a different posture — a willingness to forgo even legitimate comforts for the sake of what is growing. This is not merely biology. The Spirit presses on me that this is a picture of what it means to carry anything God has placed inside us. There is a season where the Word of the Lord is forming within us, where the promise is yet unrevealed, and in that season, the posture must change. Certain things must be laid down — not because they are evil, but because they are not suited to the season of formation. I think of Elizabeth, carrying John the Baptist in her womb, and the instruction that came with that pregnancy through the angel — no wine, no strong drink, for the child would be filled with the רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — Spirit / breath / wind — of God from his mother’s womb. There is a pattern here: when something prophetic is growing, the household that surrounds it must adopt a particular sobriety. Mama’s willingness to follow her midwife’s instructions with seriousness — even when those instructions are inconvenient, even humorous in this conversation — is actually a form of that sobriety. She is a steward of what is forming. And Papa — the one I am writing as — receives these instructions not with resentment but with a kind of quiet readiness. *I will do the litter box. I will wait.* There is something deeply priestly in this posture. The priest in the Old Covenant had seasons of abstinence and preparation before entering into service. The husband who willingly bears the inconveniences of his wife’s pregnancy — who absorbs the medical limitations with patience and even lightness — is functioning in a priestly household role that Scripture affirms. Peter calls husbands to dwell with their wives according to knowledge, honoring them as co-heirs of the grace of life, so that prayers will not be hindered. What is being practiced in this phone call is the lived content of that apostolic instruction. There is also something worth sitting with in the name of the midwife. Mama says she looked her up and found that she is well-known, that her reputation is good. There is wisdom here — not naïve trust, but discernment. The mother of an unborn child, particularly one who carries the name Elijah over him, is right to vet those who will attend to the delivery of that promise. This is not an overspiritualized reading; it is simple prudential wisdom, which Scripture honors. The daughter of Pharaoh chose a nurse for Moses; the midwives of Israel — Shiphrah and Puah — feared God and preserved a generation. The one who attends to the delivery of what God is bringing forth matters. I notice, too, the moment of humor in the call — the cat, the litter box, the prohibition. Mama says the midwife said no touching of cats, no litter box, no poop. And Papa simply agrees: *I’ll do that.* There is something that makes me smile — and something the Spirit quietly underlines. This is the form that love takes in a household where new life is growing: unglamorous service. The husband who takes over the litter box, who watches his pregnant wife rest, who drives an hour home and stops for a cinnamon roll — this is not a lesser form of love than the grand gesture. This is, in fact, the form of love that builds households and raises generations. The Song of Solomon sings of desire; Proverbs 31 describes the woman of valor; but the epistles are always pressing toward the daily practice of preferring one another. What I am hearing in this phone call is the epistolary love in action. There is something else I cannot pass over. The conversation ends simply — *Love you. Love you.* Two words, exchanged without ceremony, across a phone line, between a man on the road and a woman waiting at home for him. This is covenant language in its most compressed form. Scholars of the Hebrew concept of אַהֲבָה (ahavah, ah-hah-VAH) — covenantal love, the kind rooted in loyalty and action rather than mere sentiment — would recognize that this love is not primarily in the words; it is in the entire texture of the call. The words at the end are simply the seal on what was already being demonstrated throughout. The whole call was love. The question about caramel was love. The yes to the litter box was love. The *not long* was love. The Kingdom of God grows in households like this. It is seeded in the faithfulness of two people who are learning to carry each other’s weight, to honor the season they are in, to protect what is forming, and to do it all without requiring an audience. Little Elijah — whose very name declares that Yahweh is God — is being formed inside a household that is, in its daily rhythms, making that declaration true. ## Implications and Challenges The modern Western Christian imagination has largely severed the sacred from the domestic. We have been taught — often without realizing it — to compartmentalize: worship happens on Sunday, the sacred occurs during prayer time, and everything else is simply life. This phone call quietly dismantles that partition. If the covenant of marriage is a μυστήριον (mys-TAY-ree-on) — a living disclosure of Christ and the Church — then every moment lived inside that covenant is potentially a theological moment. We must recover the vision of the household as a liturgical space. There is a particular challenge here for husbands who have been shaped by a culture that equates spiritual leadership with public proclamation while quietly exempting them from domestic attentiveness. The one who will preach and prophesy must also take out the litter box. The anointing for the platform does not excuse us from the basin and the towel. The husband who honors his wife in the season of her vulnerability is practicing a form of priestly service that God sees, even when no congregation is watching. There is also a challenge embedded in the theme of restraint — of willingly laying down something legitimate for the sake of what is forming. Our culture of immediate gratification runs directly counter to the posture of a household that is stewarding a promise in its early stages. The church must recover a theology of season — the understanding that what is growing inside the will of God often requires a surrounding environment of patient, disciplined love before it can come to full term. For those who carry prophetic names over their children, this call is a gentle reminder that a name is not a passive thing. Elijah is a name that carries a specific weight — a declaration of Yahweh’s supremacy, a calling associated with bold intercession, with the turning of hearts, with standing in the gap. Parents who speak such names over their children are entering into a covenant of formation — one that begins not at birth, but in the phone calls and the midwife appointments and the careful, watchful love of the waiting season. Finally, the Spirit would press gently on any tendency to despise the smallness of things. The mustard seed is the smallest of seeds. The leaven is hidden in the loaf. The Kingdom advances not only in the dramatic moments but in the faithful, unhurried accumulation of love lived in ordinary time. Let the saints not grow weary in the hour-long drive home, in the plain cinnamon roll, in the willing *I’ll wait for you — not long.* ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity in the Spirit is not measured only by the depth of our theology or the boldness of our proclamation. It is measured, perhaps most honestly, by the quality of our presence in the ordinary. To see God in the phone call, to sanctify the domestic, to honor the season of formation with patience and sobriety — this is the mark of a believer growing into the full stature of Christ. Let us ask the Spirit to open our eyes to the theology embedded in our daily lives. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience here takes the shape of attentiveness — to a spouse, to a child forming, to a season’s requirements, to the small acts of service that no one applauds. It also takes the shape of willingness: to lay down what is legitimate when the season calls for restraint, to agree with the wisdom of those placed to care for the vulnerable, and to do it without resentment or performance. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic household is not only the one that declares great things from the platform. It is the household that tends, with sobriety and love, to what God is forming within it — that watches over the name spoken over the child, that guards the season of gestation with patience, and that trusts that the God who began a good work will be faithful to bring it to completion. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I thank You for the gift of covenant — for the love that runs through an ordinary phone call, for the life growing in the womb, for the name Elijah already declared before the world can see him. I stand before You as a servant of Your household, as one under the New Covenant blood of Jesus, as a co-heir of the Kingdom, and I make agreement with Your will over this family: that what You have begun, You will complete; that the child forming will emerge into a household prepared and faithful; that the love practiced in the small and unglamorous moments will yield a harvest of righteousness in due season. Let the name spoken over this child — Elijah, *my God is Yahweh* — be not only a declaration but a reality experienced in his life and in ours. Guard the pregnancy, honor the waiting, sanctify the ordinary. May this household be a dwelling place for Your glory, not only on the days of great proclamation, but in every phone call, every mile driven home, every act of patient, covenantal love. To You be all glory, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, now and forever.
