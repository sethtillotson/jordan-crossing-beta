# 07-01 Conversation Reflection: Theology of Desperation, Sobriety, and Church Judgment **Title:** 07-01 at 12:29 — When the Desperate Become the Motivated, and the Survivors Become the Judges **Recorded:** 2026-07-01 12:29:57 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Conversation Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to this conversation unfold between myself and my brother Samuel, I was struck by something quietly profound moving beneath the surface of what sounded, at first pass, like two old friends catching up over a hot dog in the middle of nowhere. There is something here about the theology of desperation — about how the flesh reaches for what can quench its thirst, as I said in that moment, and how the world judges a man differently depending on whether he is still in the pit or whether he has clawed his way out of it. There is something here about sobriety as suffering, about the church’s capacity for judgment from a place of safety, and about the kind of loyalty that only forms between people who have eaten out of the same dumpster, metaphorically or otherwise. What surprised me, sitting with this conversation, is how much kingdom insight is buried inside what looked like ordinary fraternal banter. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Body of Christ is full of people who have been kept — kept from the worst expressions of the flesh’s hunger, kept from addiction, kept from desperation — and who do not realize that their keeping was grace, not virtue. When that distinction collapses, the church becomes a place of quiet condemnation, where the rougher testimonies are tolerated politely but never truly honored, and where the survivors of hard living are expected to sanitize their stories into something more palatable for the pew. But the Scripture does not sanitize. The canon is full of desperate people — Jacob wrestling, David on the run, Paul confessing the war within his own members — and it is precisely their desperation that made them useful. If we lose the theology of desperation, we lose the church’s capacity to receive and honor the testimonies of those who have been through hell and come out the other side still holding onto God by something barely thicker than a thread. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, this conversation looks like two men processing the residue of hard lives — comparing scars, venting about betrayal, laughing at the absurdity of where they’ve been. The worldly mind would frame the sobriety conversation as a psychological struggle, a matter of dopamine regulation and behavioral habit-formation. It would frame the commentary about church people who’ve never struggled as a class grievance — the outsiders resenting the insiders — and it would frame the discussion about loyalty and small teams as straightforward entrepreneurial wisdom drawn from lived experience. The humor about Little Debbie’s and homeless people in Florida would simply register as the digressive small talk of two men who are comfortable enough with each other to wander freely. And the business tension at the end — the unpaid work, the conditional involvement, the mention of little Elijah as the reason I am no longer willing to go unpaid — would simply read as a minor professional dispute between friends navigating an uneven working relationship. On its own terms, the world would see this as real, relatable, and basically unremarkable. **The Limitation:** The trouble is that the natural mind processes this conversation only on the surface layer, where it is essentially a story about two flawed men managing two flawed lives. It cannot perceive the thread of the Spirit that runs through it — the moment where I named the flesh’s thirst in a single clean phrase and then kept moving without realizing I had just articulated something genuinely scriptural. It cannot see that the frustration with the church’s judgment is not merely a social complaint but a prophetic tension — the same tension Paul navigates in Romans 14 and 15, the same tension Jesus names when he says the sick need a physician and not the healthy. The natural mind hears “crackhead motivation” as colorful language and misses entirely that what Samuel and I were describing is exactly what the saints of God have always called the fruit of necessity — the kind of faith that moves not from abundance but from having nothing left to lose. The Spirit must open the ear to hear what the transcript is actually carrying beneath its own noise. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I am seeing, as I sit with this conversation, that the first and perhaps most significant thread running through it is the one I named almost accidentally when I said, *“the flesh reaches for what it can quench its thirst.”* I was not preaching in that moment — I was commiserating with a brother about how hard sobriety actually is. But the image I reached for was not random. The flesh’s thirst is a biblical category. The Samaritan woman came to the well in the heat of the day — outside the hours when the respectable women drew water — because she was thirsty in ways that had already driven her through five men and into a sixth arrangement that wasn’t even a marriage. Jesus did not rebuke her thirst. He named it, honored its reality, and then offered her something that would mean she never had to come back to the well again. John 4:13–14 is not primarily a verse about physical water. It is a verse about the nature of flesh-thirst — that everything the flesh reaches for to quench itself only deepens the need — and it is a verse about the only answer that actually satisfies. What Samuel and I were wrestling with in that conversation, even in the casual register of two friends eating a hot dog, is the same thing: the reality that the flesh reaches, and that sobriety — real sobriety, the kind with no nicotine, no soda, no nothing, as I described it — is essentially the experience of sitting with unquenched flesh-thirst and refusing to let it dictate. That is not a psychological achievement. That is something closer to what Paul describes in Galatians 5:24, where those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires. The Greek word there for crucified is ἐσταύρωσαν (estaúrōsan, es-TOW-ro-san) — a completed aorist action, a decisive past-tense event — but the daily experience of it is anything but a single completed moment. It is the ongoing reality of holding the flesh to that cross while it reaches and claws and protests. What arrests me further is Samuel’s phrase: *“I’m going to be a victim before I’m sober.”* He said it with humor and self-awareness, but there is something true and even confessional inside it. The flesh, left to its own logic, would always rather be consumed than crucified. It would rather become the victim of its own hunger than submit to the discipline of denial. And this is not a moral failure unique to people with Samuel’s background or mine. This is the human condition. Paul says in Romans 7:18, *“For I know that in me (that is, in my flesh) nothing good dwells; for to will is present with me, but how to perform what is good I do not find.”* The apostle who wrote half the New Testament said this. The man who planted churches across the known world said this. The flesh does not improve with apostolic credentials. It must be continuously yielded, continuously brought back to the cross, continuously denied the drink it is reaching for. This brings me to the thread about church people who have been kept — the ones Samuel and I were referencing when we talked about the pastor who said that those who grew up sheltered within church culture, never touched by addiction or desperation, are kept by grace rather than by virtue. There is a real theology here that the church often handles poorly. Jesus makes this exact point in Luke 7 when a Pharisee named Simon hosts him for dinner and a sinful woman — unnamed, which is itself significant — enters the room and begins washing his feet with her tears and her hair. Simon’s internal reaction is immediate and predictable: *If this man were a prophet, he would know what kind of woman this is.* His keeping had produced in him a confidence that she was categorically other than himself. Jesus responds not with rebuke of Simon’s external behavior but with a parable about two debtors — one forgiven much, one forgiven little — and he draws the conclusion that the one who has been forgiven more loves more. The point is not that sin is desirable. The point is that the awareness of one’s own debt before God produces a quality of gratitude and love that the kept person often cannot access from within the safety of their keeping. Samuel said it more plainly: *“When you’ve been through hell, you can judge someone who’s been through hell. When you’ve not been through hell, shut the fuck up.”* I do not endorse the idiom for public teaching, but I recognize the theological substance behind it, and I think it maps cleanly onto what James means in James 2 when he challenges the assembly that offers a cold brother warm words — *“be warmed and filled”* — without offering the actual warmth and filling he needs. The judgment that comes from safety is always in danger of being abstract. The solidarity that comes from shared suffering is always more costly and more real. This is why Hebrews 4:15 is such a staggering verse: *“For we do not have a High Priest who cannot sympathize with our weaknesses, but was in all points tempted as we are, yet without sin.”* The Son of God did not judge from safety. He entered into the precise conditions of human vulnerability so that his intercession would not be abstract. He went through it. That is the model for how the church is meant to relate to those who are still in the pit. Something else presses on me from within this conversation — the image of the homeless people in Florida eating from the garbage and being, in my description, some of the happiest people I have ever met. I said it almost in passing, but it stopped me when I returned to it. There is a beatitude shape to that observation. Matthew 5 opens with a cascade of declarations about who is actually blessed — and not one of them maps onto what the surrounding culture identifies as success, stability, or comfort. *“Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.”* The Greek word for poor there is πτωχοί (ptōchoí, ptoh-KHOY) — not merely financially constrained, but utterly without resource, crouching-poor, the kind of poverty that has no reserve and no pretense. The homeless man who pulls a half-eaten box of donuts out of the Dunkin’ bin and celebrates the fifty percent discount is not performing poverty. He has nothing left to perform. And in that having-nothing, something opens. Samuel called it crackhead motivation. I think Scripture might call it the freedom that comes when mammon has finally lost its grip — when there is nothing left to protect, nothing left to hoard, and the simple provision of today is genuinely enough. This connects to the thread about desperation being reframed as motivation once success arrives. What Samuel named is a real social dynamic: while you are desperate, you are crazy. The moment you make it, the same behavior becomes a come-up story. The world’s categories for evaluation are entirely retrospective and outcome-dependent. But the Spirit does not evaluate from outcomes. Joseph was not less anointed in the pit than he was in Pharaoh’s court — the anointing was identical; only the visible circumstances had changed. David was not less the king in the wilderness than he was on the throne — the covenant of Samuel’s anointing was already sealed; only the manifestation was deferred. The saints who are still in the trench are not lesser than the saints who have broken through. They may, in fact, be carrying something that the arrived saint has already begun to lose — a rawness of dependence on God that prosperity has a way of quietly eroding. The business tension at the end of the conversation — my refusal to do further work without payment for completed work, and my naming of little Elijah as the reason — is worth pausing on, because it is not merely a professional boundary. When I said *“I ain’t fucking about with that anymore, I got little Elijah to worry about,”* I was naming a shift that fatherhood produces in a man. Proverbs 13:22 says that a good man leaves an inheritance for his children’s children. The stewardship of what I earn and what I spend my labor on is no longer simply about me. It is now about the covenant seed after me. That is a sanctifying weight. It is the kind of weight that clarifies priorities rapidly. A man who was once willing to absorb ambiguity in a financial arrangement because he could afford to be casual with his own sustenance becomes a different kind of negotiator when there is a child depending on him. That is not selfishness. That is the beginning of the kind of covenantal faithfulness that the Scripture honors in the provider and the father. The conversation ends with *“God bless and keep you”* — a phrase so familiar it risks becoming liturgical noise. But I notice it. Samuel says *“God bless, brother”* and I respond with *“God bless and keep you.”* The echo of Numbers 6:24 is faint but real — the Aaronic blessing, the oldest benediction in the canon: *“The LORD bless you and keep you.”* Two men who have been through desperation together, who have been addicted together, who have navigated betrayal and uneven business dealings and heartbreak and sobriety together, parting with a blessing that is thousands of years old. There is something quietly beautiful about that. The liturgy of blessing does not require a sanctuary. It can happen at the end of a phone call between two scarred men who are still, somehow, holding on to the grace of God. I find myself reflecting on what it means to be a small team of loyal people — which Samuel and I touched on briefly in the context of business partnerships. Samuel said he would rather have four partners than fifty, and I affirmed the wisdom of a small, loyal team over a large and unreliable one. This is not merely entrepreneurial insight. The entire pattern of Scripture is organized around the principle of faithful remnant over impressive multitude. Gideon’s thirty-two thousand became three hundred. Jesus chose twelve from the crowds that followed him. Paul’s inner circle was small and costly. The kingdom advances not through the accumulation of bodies but through the fidelity of the few who will actually stay. The betrayal Samuel described — the person who blocked him two weeks after being asked for fifty dollars of ad spend, the same person who later claimed to have been there for him — is the oldest story in the world. Judas carried the money bag. The inner circle always has the most capacity to wound. But the answer is not to stop trusting. The answer is to become more discerning about the difference between the crowd and the covenant, between the follower and the companion. What I am left holding, after sitting with this entire conversation, is something like this: the Spirit moves through ordinary human exchange. He does not require theological language or consecrated settings. He moves through the honest confession of two men who are struggling to stay sober, through the raw frustration at the church’s comfortable judgment, through the laughter about Little Debbie’s and the homeless man with his box of donuts. He moves through the naming of a son as the reason a father holds his ground financially. He moves through a parting blessing that neither man pauses to examine but both men mean. This is the nature of the רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — the Spirit, the breath, the wind — who, as Jesus told Nicodemus in John 3:8, blows where he wishes, and you hear his sound, but you do not know where he comes from or where he is going. He was present in this conversation. He was speaking through the texture of it. He does not always announce himself loudly. Sometimes he moves quietly through a hot dog and a hard conversation and a parting word of blessing between brothers. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation surfaces is the church’s tendency to valorize kept testimonies over recovered ones. The person who grew up in the church, never fell into addiction, and maintained external moral consistency across their lifetime is often held up as the model of Christian faithfulness. But Jesus’s own words in Luke 15 suggest that heaven’s loudest celebration is not for the sheep that stayed in the fold but for the one that was lost and is found. This is not an argument for prodigality. It is an argument against the subtle pride that keeping can produce — the pride that quietly positions itself as the standard by which others are measured, without ever acknowledging that the keeping itself was grace. The second challenge is the church’s discomfort with raw testimony. Samuel’s language throughout this conversation is unfiltered, and the temptation in a public-facing meditation is to sanitize it into something more palatably Christian. But sanitization is a form of dishonoring the struggle. The psalms of lament are not sanitized. Jeremiah’s complaints are not sanitized. Job’s accusations against God are not sanitized. The canon makes room for the unfiltered cry of the person in the pit, and the church must learn to make that same room — not as an endorsement of every word or posture, but as a recognition that God is large enough to receive the raw voice of the suffering and speak back into it. The third challenge concerns sobriety as spiritual discipline rather than merely behavioral modification. The modern therapeutic model frames sobriety primarily as a health achievement — a rewiring of neurological patterns, a management of triggers and cravings. And while that framework has genuine utility, it does not go far enough. What I described — *no nicotine, no soda, no nothing, just hardcore sober* — sounds from the outside like deprivation. From the inside, and from within the canon, it is closer to fasting. It is the deliberate refusal to let the flesh dictate. Isaiah 58 and Matthew 6 both treat the discipline of denial not as punishment but as realignment — a choosing of a different hunger, a training of the body to yield to what the spirit actually needs. The fourth challenge is around loyalty and covenant in community. Both Samuel and I have been burned by people who claimed friendship or partnership and then disappeared when things got costly. The temptation after repeated betrayal is to stop building with people altogether — to become self-sufficient, closed, survivalist. But that is not the Kingdom pattern. Jesus did not stop building with people after Judas. Paul did not stop planting churches after Demas forsook him. The right response to betrayal is not isolation but discernment — learning to distinguish between the crowd and the covenant, between the acquaintance and the companion, between the person who shows up in your success and the one who showed up when you were importing crystal meth via computer because you had run out of better options. The fifth and final challenge is generational. The mention of little Elijah is brief in this conversation, but it is weight-bearing. The decision to hold financial ground — to refuse to do further work without payment — is a decision shaped by fatherhood. This is a spiritual maturation point that the church needs to speak into more clearly: that the arrival of a child is not only a biological event but a covenantal one, and that it reshapes a man’s stewardship, his priorities, his risk tolerance, and his sense of what his labor is actually for. Elijah is a reason to be sober. Elijah is a reason to be paid. Elijah is a reason to stay — to not be a victim of the desperation that once drove both the recklessness and, paradoxically, the resilience. The seed after us is always a sanctifying weight. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This conversation calls me — and those who will read this — to stop treating the testimony of desperation as a lesser category of spiritual biography. The hard life is not a disqualification from the Kingdom. In many cases, it is the very curriculum through which the Kingdom forms its most useful servants. Spiritual maturity means learning to honor the scar as much as the safety, to recognize grace in the keeping and grace in the recovering, and to stop confusing moral comfort with spiritual advancement. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Kingdom obedience here looks like showing up for the brother who is still in the trench — not with warm words and no warmth, not with theological advice delivered from a distance, but with actual presence, actual solidarity, actual willingness to sit in the middle of nowhere and eat a hot dog and let the conversation go wherever it needs to go. It looks like holding financial integrity not from greed but from covenantal stewardship. It looks like saying *“God bless and keep you”* and meaning every syllable of it. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation calls for is one of watching for where the Spirit moves in the ordinary — in the banter, in the confession, in the parting blessing, in the small acts of a man holding his ground for the sake of his son. The prophet does not only speak into the extraordinary moment. The prophet learns to hear the רוּחַ (ROO-akh) in the ordinary conversation and to name what is moving beneath the surface. That is what this meditation attempts. That is the posture it invites us into. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I thank You that You are not a God who requires sanitized language and cleaned-up testimonies before You will show up in a conversation. You were present in that exchange — in the laughter, in the honesty, in the naming of the flesh’s thirst, in the frustration at easy judgment from comfortable places, in the quiet mention of little Elijah as the reason a man holds his ground. You are the God who met Hagar in the wilderness, who let David write his rage into the Psalter, who received the sinful woman’s tears without a word of rebuke. I ask that You would deepen in me the capacity to see Your hand in the ordinary, to honor the testimony of the desperate rather than domesticating it for easier ears, and to remain a brother who can be trusted when the cost is real. Keep Samuel close to You through the grief he is still carrying. Strengthen him in the sobriety that is costing him something. Remind us both that the anointing is not suspended in the trench — that You are present there as fully as You are present in any sanctuary. Let little Elijah grow up knowing a father who learned to hold the ground and bless the brother and receive the rוּחַ (ROO-akh) even in the middle of nowhere, even over a hot dog, even in the roughest and most ordinary moments of a life that is still being rewritten by Your grace. We agree with Your will, Father, between the heavens and the earth — that Your Kingdom comes, and that the desperate and the recovering and the scarred and the loyal are not afterthoughts in it but its very substance. To You be all glory, now and into the ages.
