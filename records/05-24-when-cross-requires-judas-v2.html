# 05-24 Conversation Reflection: Spiritual Formation Through Suffering, Servant-Authority, and Divine Curriculum **Title:** 05-24 at 03:55 — When the Cross Requires Judas and the Throne Demands the Wilderness **Recorded:** 2026-05-24 03:55:40 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Conversation Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to the voices gathered in this exchange — teachers, pastors, a sister carrying a word about rivers and candles and snakes — something in me recognized that the Spirit was weaving a single thread through what appeared on the surface to be several different conversations. The central tension pressing against my chest as I sat with this was not complex to name, but it was costly to receive: the instruments of our suffering are not our enemies, they are our curriculum. What arrests me is the staggering implication that the cross cannot be carried if Judas is removed, that the throne cannot be occupied if Saul is despised, and that the power of God matures precisely when miracles become unnecessary. **Why This Matters:** This matters for every believer who has ever prayed for God to remove a difficult person, exit a painful season, or fast-track a promotion they have not yet been formed to carry. The Body of Christ in this generation is saturated with a consumer spirituality that treats difficulty as spiritual failure, adversity as divine absence, and personal comfort as the metric of divine favor. What I heard in this gathering cuts directly against that current with the force of a prophetic plumb line. If we are to grow up into the full stature of Christ — into what Paul calls in Ephesians 4:13 “a perfect man, to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ” — we must come to terms with the terrifying and merciful truth that God is sovereign over the instruments of our formation, including the ones that wound us. The discipleship implications of this single truth are enough to reorder an entire life. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, when it encounters pain inflicted by another person, moves immediately and instinctively toward a binary: this person is wicked, I am the victim, and justice demands their removal or my escape. This is not moral failure — it is the logic of self-preservation, and it has a kind of social dignity attached to it. The world has even built entire therapeutic and activist vocabularies around this logic: trauma, toxicity, abuse cycles, self-protection, boundaries, liberation. None of those realities are entirely false, and compassion must never be abandoned in theological discourse. But when the worldly mind interprets Saul as simply a bully, Judas as simply a traitor, the wilderness as simply a failure, and the miracle as simply a sign of arrival, it remains in the shallows. It cannot see the scaffolding God is erecting. It cannot perceive that the affliction has a curriculum, that the betrayer has a function, that the smoothness of miraculous provision in the desert is itself a living sermon about the nature of covenant. The natural mind processes what is visible and immediate; it is structurally unable to perceive the teleological arc of divine formation without the illuminating work of the Spirit. **The Limitation:** The problem is not that human reasoning is evil — it is that it is finite, and finitude cannot perceive eternity’s purposes from the inside of a season. A man being trained by a master craftsman might, in the middle of a grueling apprenticeship, conclude that the master hates him. He feels only the repetition, the correction, the exposure of his weaknesses, the humiliation of being told to start over. Without the end in view, the process looks like cruelty. This is precisely why the Spirit must interpret what the mind alone cannot discern. The fear of being used, the resentment of the Saul-figure, the desperation to escape the wilderness — these are natural responses that, left uninterpreted by revelation, will cause a man to abort his assignment at the precise moment his formation is reaching its most decisive stage. It is not that the pain isn’t real. It is that pain, in the economy of God, is rarely what it appears to be to the one inside it. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I listened to the brother open this teaching with the image of Judas eating the flesh of Jesus, I found myself sitting very still inside. There is something almost unbearable in that image if you press it long enough: the Son of God, knowing all things, breaking bread with the very hand that would deliver Him to death. John 13:3 establishes the context with extraordinary precision — the Spirit directed the writer to record that Jesus moved toward the washing of feet *knowing* that the Father had given all things into His hands. Full authority. Complete power. And the first act that follows that revelation is not the expulsion of Judas but the bending of a knee before dirty feet. I am seeing something here that I cannot rush past: the posture of sovereignty is, in Christ, always downward-facing. The brother made a point that the wisdom of God in the cross was precisely its hiddenness from the principalities and powers. Paul states this in 1 Corinthians 2:8 — had the rulers of this age known, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory. This means Judas was not a disruption to the plan. Judas *was* the plan, in the sense that God’s wisdom is so deep it makes even treason a servant of redemption. I must sit with the weight of that. If I am calling something in my life a disruption, a betrayal, an obstacle — the question I now must ask is whether I am Judas-proof enough in my theology to receive that the hand of God reaches through even the betrayer’s hand. This does not excuse the betrayer. It glorifies the One who cannot be thwarted. The movement from Judas to David is not a leap — it is a deepening of the same current. What I heard about David struck me because I had always read the wilderness years as a kind of waiting room before the real assignment began. But the observation sharpened something for me: the David who killed Goliath was a soloist. A gifted, anointed, courageous, lone-wolf soloist. And a soloist cannot shepherd a kingdom of millions. So God, in His mercy, used Saul — dangerous, unpredictable, spiritually troubled Saul — to drive David out of isolation and into community. First Adullam, where four hundred men in distress and debt and discontentment gathered to him (1 Samuel 22:2). This was David’s first leadership classroom: broken people who needed a shepherd, not a performer. God sent Saul to force David into that room. I find myself genuinely confronted here. How many times have I interpreted the Saul-figure in my life as someone God needs to remove in order for my assignment to advance? But the brother’s word turns that entirely around: Saul is not the enemy of the assignment. Saul *is* part of the assignment. The pressure that comes from an adversarial relationship is sometimes the very force that pushes us into the formation we would never willingly enter. The David who never ran from Saul is a David who never discovers that he can lead the broken, the indebted, the bitter — and that capacity is precisely what the throne of a shepherd-king requires. Victim mentality, as was named so plainly, makes us die small. Not because we are weak, but because we refuse the curriculum that would make us large enough for the room God is preparing. Then comes the image that stopped me entirely: Jesus, who could walk on water, choosing a boat. Jesus, who could multiply bread, choosing to purchase what others have made. I am pressing into the theological gravity of this and I am finding something that cuts deeply against the charismatic culture I have sometimes swum in. The term that comes to me from the Greek is σωφροσύνη (sōphrosynē, so-fro-SOO-nay) — soundness of mind, sober self-restraint, the virtue of knowing the limits within which one ought to operate. Jesus did not use His supernatural capacity for personal convenience, and this was not weakness — it was σωφροσύνη (so-fro-SOO-nay) of the highest order. He did not subject Himself to natural processes because He lacked power. He did so because He understood that power is not for self-service. This directly confronts what I heard named plainly: we charismatics have been discipled, in many quarters, to use God — to deploy our knowledge of divine power for personal advancement, personal comfort, personal elevation. When that trajectory reaches its logical end, God ceases to be worshipped and begins to be wielded. And a God who is wielded is no longer God; He has become a spiritual utility. The brother’s word about Jesus washing feet after receiving the knowledge that all authority was His — that is the antidote. The revelation of authority, in Christ’s hands, produces *service*, not supremacy. If I have received revelation of authority and my first impulse is to leverage it for myself, I have missed the form of Christ entirely. The snake image offered by a sister in this gathering landed with a kind of quiet theological precision that I want to honor. She described watching a snake placed on a microfiber surface — smooth, frictionless — and observing that it could not move. Snakes, she said, require friction to travel. And immediately the pastoral application crystallized: the enemy moves through us when we carry internal friction — double-mindedness, bitterness, a defeated posture, unresolved offense. The Hebrew word for peace, שָׁלוֹם (shalom, shah-LOHM), does not merely mean the absence of conflict; it carries the sense of wholeness, completeness, a settled integration of every part of one’s being under the governance of God. A mind at שָׁלוֹם (shah-LOHM) is a frictionless surface. The enemy lands on it and has nowhere to travel. This is not passivity; it is a cultivated spiritual condition that must be chosen, returned to, and maintained with deliberate intentionality. The candle image from that gathering — the man who stood nightly before the seat of power not to change the institution but to protect himself from being changed by it — carries within it a pneumatological precision that I believe the Spirit intended. The great danger of prolonged engagement with darkness, injustice, or systemic wickedness is not only that you fail to change it but that it succeeds in changing you. Persistent presence with a single light is itself a form of witness and resistance. This resonates deeply with what Paul wrote in Romans 12:21: “Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good.” The overcoming is not always dramatic. Sometimes it is simply the maintenance of light in a posture of persistent, unhurried presence. The teaching on the preaching of the gospel as power — not as secondary to power, but as *constituting* power — opened something that I want to press carefully. The apostle who spoke drew a distinction I have not heard framed quite this way before: the miraculous works that God performs *before* a person comes to faith are persuasive in function. They are, in the apostle’s own language, God saying “It is Me.” They are introduction, not destination. The cessation of constant miraculous confirmation after the new birth is not abandonment — it is graduation. God is now calling the believer to *speak* to the mountain rather than wait for God to move it on their behalf. The locus of the miracle shifts. And this is what maturity looks like: not more visible wonders, but a people whose *word* carries the weight of heaven because they have become those through whom the Word operates. What I find myself understanding, as I hold all of these threads together, is that they are all describing the same formation. David in the wilderness is being made into a man whose authority is the fruit of suffering rather than the avoidance of it. Jesus on a boat is demonstrating that the fullest expression of divine authority is not self-promotion but self-giving. The snake on the smooth surface reveals that peace is not the absence of threat but the condition that renders threat inoperative. The candle in the dark teaches that faithfulness is not measured by visible transformation but by the preservation of one’s own light. And the apostle’s word on miracles and maturity reveals that the saints are not meant to remain perpetually dependent on the spectacular; they are meant to grow into the ones through whom the spectacular flows as a natural consequence of their union with Christ. The thread beneath all of it is this: God is forming a people who are *not* victims of their circumstances, not users of their gifts, not consumers of their inheritance, and not performers of their power. He is forming servants — κοινωνοί (koinōnoi, koy-no-NOY), partners and sharers — who have been so thoroughly shaped by the cross that when authority is placed in their hands, they bend down and wash feet with it. This is the image that will not leave me. All power in heaven and on earth — and the next recorded action is a basin of water and a towel. That is what I want the formation to produce in me. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation presses upon me — and upon us — is the thoroughgoing reformation of how we interpret adversarial relationships in our lives. The modern Western church has, in many quarters, been deeply shaped by therapeutic individualism, in which the goal of spiritual life is personal flourishing and the removal of painful relationships. But what I heard in this exchange suggests something more costly and more glorious: the Saul in your life may be precisely the instrument of your formation for the room God is calling you into. This is not a word against healthy boundaries or wisdom about toxic relationships. It is a word against the reflexive spiritual interpretation that assigns divine abandonment to divine discipline. Before we pray for God to remove the Saul, we ought to ask what the Saul is forming in us. The second challenge is the charismatic one, and it must be named with both honesty and love. There is a strain of teaching widely circulated in our networks that essentially instructs believers to leverage their knowledge of God’s power for personal advantage — healing, wealth, deliverance from difficulty, elevation, influence. When this reaches its full expression, God becomes a means rather than the end. The image of Jesus using boats when He could walk on water is a prophetic rebuke to that entire framework. The One who possessed all power chose not to use it for His own convenience. If that is the character of our Lord, then any theology that primarily orients divine power toward personal benefit has fundamentally misread both Christ and the cross. Third, the snake and friction image has direct pastoral application to our interior lives that I think we often resist because it requires genuine self-examination. It is easier to blame the enemy’s activity on his power than to acknowledge that we may be providing him a pathway through our own unresolved double-mindedness, bitterness, or defeat. The call to peace — to the cultivated, deliberate maintenance of שָׁלוֹם (shah-LOHM) — is not a passive call. It is an act of spiritual warfare. The believer who tends their inner life with the same seriousness that a gardener tends soil is the believer who will find that the enemy’s operations against them are increasingly without purchase. Fourth, the word about gratitude and the wilderness is a word this generation urgently needs. The children of Israel complained about meat in the presence of forty years of supernatural provision — shoes that did not wear out, clothing that grew with their children (Deuteronomy 8:4), manna every morning without one missed delivery. We have been taught to compare our present season to the promised land and declare lack, when in fact the wilderness itself is already a theater of divine faithfulness. Gratitude is not a spiritual nicety. It is a theological posture that determines whether we can perceive what God is doing in the season we are currently inhabiting. Those who cannot see His provision in the wilderness will not have the eyes to steward it in the promised land. Fifth, the word on miracles and maturity carries a liberating and sobering truth simultaneously. Liberating because it reframes the apparent silence of supernatural intervention not as divine distance but as divine confidence in the maturing believer. Sobering because it means that the call on the mature saint is not to wait for God to move the mountain but to *speak to it*. This requires a depth of settled faith that is not manufactured in comfort. It is forged in the seasons where God did not visibly intervene, and we learned to trust His word over our experience. Maturity costs something. The one who has paid that cost carries the mountain-moving word. Finally, the image of being used — and the remarkable reframe offered in this gathering — deserves its own place. If someone uses me, I was useful. If I am vulnerable and someone takes advantage, I showed them my authentic self and they revealed their hidden self. I came out with my integrity intact; they came out exposed. This is not naïveté — it is a Christological posture. Jesus came into a world full of users and was fine with it. That phrase should stop us cold. The Son of God did not enter human history with a self-protective strategy. He entered with an open hand, a servant’s towel, and a cross. And through all of that apparent vulnerability, He accomplished what no power-strategy ever could. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This meditation calls me — calls us — to a fundamental reorientation of how we read the painful chapters of our lives. Spiritual maturity is not marked by the absence of adversarial relationships, wilderness seasons, or apparent betrayal. It is marked by the capacity to receive those realities as part of a formation process that God is superintending with sovereign intention. The maturing believer asks not “Why is this happening to me?” but “What is being built in me through this?” That shift is not easy, and it cannot be faked. But it is the difference between dying small in a victim narrative and growing into the fullness of what God has called us to inhabit. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The Kingdom call here is toward servant-authority — the specific kind of power that expresses itself through washing feet rather than demanding recognition, through riding in boats rather than walking on water for public display, through standing with a candle night after night rather than waiting for a dramatic political reversal. Kingdom obedience in this season looks like tending the interior life with sober intentionality, honoring the instruments of our formation rather than despising them, and speaking with genuine faith to the mountains we have been assigned — because we have been formed in the seasons when God let us find our way to Him in the dark. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation cultivates is one of long faithfulness over short spectacle. The man with the candle did not come each night to change the administration. He came so that the administration would not change him. This is a prophetic word for the long-obedient: your faithfulness is itself a form of intercession. Your presence in the river of what God is doing — not as the captain but as the deckhand, taking your orders, keeping your lane, cheering on the other boats — is itself a witness that the river has not stopped, that the justice and love and Kingdom purposes of God are still moving, regardless of what appears on the shore. Abide. Remain. Keep the candle. Hold the oar. The river does not need you to control it. It needs you to be in it. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You in the authority of the Name above every name, as one who has been made a co-heir with Christ, a servant of the prophetic word, a steward of the mysteries You have given to Your servants — and I bring this meditation back to You as an offering. You have spoken through many mouths today, and I have heard a single word behind all of them: You are forming a people. Not performing a people. Forming. I thank You for every Saul You have ever placed before me. I thank You for every wilderness where the shoes did not wear out even when I was not paying attention. I thank You that You did not build me a boat I could walk on, because what I need is not the miracle for my convenience — what I need is the character that does not require one. Wash my feet again, Lord. Smooth every interior friction that gives the enemy passage through my thought life. Let שָׁלוֹם (shah-LOHM) so inhabit my inner world that the adversary finds no place to travel. Make me useful enough to be used. Give me the courage of the man with the candle who chose presence over performance. And train my tongue to speak to mountains with the settled confidence of one who has watched You be faithful in the wilderness long enough to know that You do not fail. We are the river, Lord. Keep us moving. Keep us loving. Keep us in the boats You have assigned us to, with our hands on the oars, our eyes on You, and our voices calling across the water to every other boat: *Keep going. We are with you. Go.* To the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit — be all glory, forever and ever.
