# 08-19 Reflection: Spiritual Wisdom in a Business Partnership Call **Title:** 08-19 at 15:02 — The Slow and Steady Building of a House **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I listened to a business conversation between myself and a brother, and what arrested me was not the strategy but the surrender buried inside it — the words *“I had to put my ego to the side.”* There is a spiritual architecture hiding in this exchange about SOPs, onboarding timelines, and dual approval, and the Spirit keeps drawing my attention to the pattern beneath the plan. Two men, having failed together, are trying to build something that will not collapse the next time pressure comes. That instinct — to counteract failure with clear process, patience, and shared accountability — is older and holier than either of us realized as we spoke. **Why This Matters:** Most of my life with the Lord is lived not in the mountaintop moment but in the ordinary conference call, the ledger, the training module, the decision made under fatigue at the end of a working day. If the Spirit cannot speak through a conversation about white-labeled services and LinkedIn accounts, then I have quarantined most of my waking hours from God. But Scripture refuses that quarantine. The same wisdom that governs how a man builds a house, keeps his word, disciplines his ambition, and cares for those under his authority is the wisdom that governs the Kingdom. This matters because discipleship is not what happens after work; it is the way I work, the way I repent mid-sentence, the way I refuse to rush a brother into ruin. What I do with process, patience, and power in the marketplace reveals what I actually believe about the God who built all things. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation Read naturally, this is simply two entrepreneurs recovering from a mistake and tightening their operation. A partnership nearly fractured — someone considered walking away — and the resolution came through negotiated terms: stricter standard operating procedures, scheduled check-ins, and a rule that no major operational decision proceeds without dual approval. The rest is competent business reasoning: protect new recruits by letting them keep their jobs during a three-month ramp, warm up the LinkedIn accounts slowly to avoid platform penalties, white-label a service for margin, and lean on an existing network to secure that crucial first client. The emotional core — *“I had to put my ego aside”* — reads as mature self-management, the sort of thing any good leadership podcast would applaud. It is pragmatic, sober, and largely admirable on its own terms. **The Limitation:** Yet the natural reading can name the surrender without perceiving its weight. It hears *“it was both our fuck-ups”* as conflict-resolution technique rather than as the rare and costly thing it actually is — two proud men mutually confessing fault instead of assigning blame. The worldly mind treats the three-month patient onboarding as strategy while missing that patience toward the vulnerable is a moral posture, not merely a tactic. It sees process as protection against loss but cannot see that process, at its root, is a form of covenant faithfulness — a promise about how people will be treated. What the flesh reads as clever risk-management, the Spirit reads as a quiet contest between the kingdom of self and the kingdom of God, being fought out one operational decision at a time. The deepest questions in this call are not *“will it profit?”* but *“whom will it serve, and how will it treat the least-secured person in the room?”* ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I keep returning to the moment my brother said, *“Okay, I’ve made my decision. I’ll stay.”* There is a whole night of wrestling compressed behind that sentence — a man who considered leaving, who sat with his own frustration long enough to name it, and who came back not with an ultimatum but with two conditions meant to protect us both. And then the line that undid me as I listened again: *“I had to put my ego to the side, which I did. I didn’t want to make a rash decision.”* I can hear the fatigue in the recording, the way the whole conversation is happening at the ragged edge of a working day, right before he says he needs to get ready for work. This is not a boardroom of polished men. This is two tired brothers trying to do the right thing when it would have been easier to fracture. What arrests me is that the healing did not come through winning the argument. It came through mutual confession. *“It was both our fuck-ups. I see where I went wrong.”* I have watched partnerships, friendships, and even churches die because no one would say that sentence — because each side needed the other to confess first. Something in the Spirit leans hard on this: the moment a man stops defending himself is the moment a thing can actually be rebuilt. The instinct that followed — *“the only way to counteract that is by having a very clear process”* — is the instinct of a man who has learned that good intentions are not enough, that love must take structural form or it will keep failing the people it claims to protect. The first Scripture that opened over this was the Lord’s own words about building: *“Whoever hears these sayings of Mine, and does them, I will liken him to a wise man who built his house on the rock… and it did not fall, for it was founded on the rock”* (Matthew 7:24–25). My brother and I were doing something small and unglamorous — digging deeper, laying foundation, refusing to build fast on sand again after the house had already cracked once. The whole conversation is, spiritually, a house being re-founded. The SOPs, the check-ins, the dual approval — these are not bureaucracy. They are the deep footing a wise builder lays precisely because he has felt the ground shift beneath a previous structure. The word *“decision”* recurs through the call, and beneath it sits the Hebrew concept of חָכְמָה (chokmah, khokh-MAH) — wisdom, not as abstract knowledge but as skilled, practical craftsmanship in living. In Exodus this same word describes the artisans who built the tabernacle; wisdom there is the ability to *build well* what God has designed. When my brother says he refused to make a rash decision, he is stumbling into chokmah (khokh-MAH) without naming it — the wisdom that measures twice, that submits ambition to process, that treats the building of a thing as a sacred craft rather than a gamble. Scripture says *“Through wisdom a house is built, and by understanding it is established”* (Proverbs 24:3). We were, without knowing it, quoting Solomon. And the mechanism we chose is deeply scriptural. *“No big decision is made toward operations without dual approval.”* This is the principle of *“in the mouth of two or three witnesses every word may be established”* (Matthew 18:16; Deuteronomy 19:15). God did not design His people to be governed by a single unchecked will, because He knows what a single unchecked will becomes. When we bind ourselves to require the agreement of another before acting, we are confessing something true about human nature — that we are not to be trusted alone with power — and something true about God’s design, that He accomplishes His purposes through the many-membered body and not the solitary ruler. Even the Godhead moves in agreement: *“Let Us make man in Our image”* (Genesis 1:26). But the passage that pressed hardest was the one about the new recruits. My brother insisted: *“We can’t have them leaving their job for the first three months… that way they keep their jobs, they keep stable, and we only have to start making time when we’re bringing the results.”* I sat with that longer than any other line. Here is the moral center of the whole call. In an industry that thrives on getting people to burn the boats, quit the job, and bet everything on the dream, my brother’s instinct was to *protect the security of the vulnerable person* until the results actually justified the risk. That is shepherd-language wearing a businessman’s clothes. *“The hireling… sees the wolf coming and leaves the sheep”* (John 10:12–13) — but here is a man who refuses to expose the sheep to the wolf of financial ruin for his own gain. This is where the Kingdom mystery begins to surface. The world’s model of scale is extraction — get many, use them fast, discard the ones who don’t produce. But my brother said the opposite: *“They do not need hundreds of clients. They need a few closes to get that recurring revenue.”* The Spirit lit up over that reversal. The Kingdom, too, does not operate by mass extraction but by faithful multiplication from a small, well-tended seed. *“The kingdom of heaven is like a mustard seed… which indeed is the least of all the seeds, but when it is grown it is greater”* (Matthew 13:31–32). A few faithful closes sustaining a whole enterprise is the same economy as a little leaven, a remnant, twelve men, a mustard seed. God has never been impressed by *hundreds*. He builds enduring things from *a few, tended well*. And the *“slow and steady training, weekly check-ins, three months”* — I cannot read that now without hearing παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) — the patient, fatherly training and formation of a person over time, never rushed. The world wants a quick training course; my brother said, *“We’re not going to treat it as a quick training course.”* This is the very posture God takes with us. He does not microwave saints. He forms us through *“the fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it”* (Hebrews 12:11), through seasons of weekly, patient, unglamorous formation. Every time we choose to disciple a new person slowly rather than exploit them quickly, we are imitating the παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) of the Father who bears long with His own children. Yet the Spirit will not let me leave the conversation unexamined, because there was a moment of tension I must sit with honestly. When the question of branding arose — *“the reason we don’t want to use Cleverly’s name is because then our clients are just going to go to Cleverly and get the service cheaper anyway”* — I feel the pull of a harder question. There is a difference between honest value-adding through management and structure, and the mere concealment of a source to prevent a client from finding a fairer price. The Spirit is not condemning the white-label model outright; providing genuine oversight, curation, and service is real labor worthy of real wage. But *“let all your things be done with charity”* (1 Corinthians 16:14) and *“provide things honest in the sight of all men”* (Romans 12:17) press me to examine whether the client is genuinely served, or merely kept in the dark. That is a question I want to carry, not bury. There was also my own quieter confession embedded in the call: *“What is this process? What are you fucking talking about?”* I did not fully understand the timeline my brother was proposing at first. And yet, rather than dismiss it, I listened until I could say *“I agree.”* The Spirit shows me that this too is a small grace — the willingness to sit inside another man’s plan long enough to see it, to have my ignorance corrected without wounding my pride. This is the mirror image of my brother laying down his ego. A partnership survives when *both* men are willing to be wrong, to be taught, to say *I see it now.* *“As iron sharpens iron, so a man sharpens the countenance of his friend”* (Proverbs 27:17). So I find myself repenting, gently, of a division I have carried — the false line between my “spiritual life” and my “work life.” Here in this ordinary, profanity-laced, exhausted end-of-day call, the Spirit was present, pressing wisdom, restraint, mutual confession, patience toward the weak, and honest dealing. If God was in this conversation, then there is no room in my life where He is not seeking to reign. *“Whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do all to the glory of God”* (1 Corinthians 10:31). The building of a business is not exempt from the Lordship of Christ; it is one more field in which His Kingdom either advances or is refused. And for the brethren, this is the witness I want to carry out of that call: that the ordinary decisions of provision — how we hire, how we train, how we price, how we protect the vulnerable, how we share power, how we confess fault to one another — are not spiritually neutral. They are the daily liturgy of whether we actually believe what we sing on Sunday. My brother ended the call with *“God bless you,”* and I answered *“God bless, brother.”* Even that was not decoration. It was two men handing each other back to God at the end of the labor, which is exactly what the Body is for. ## Implications and Challenges **On the sanctity of ordinary work.** It is easy for us in modern Western Christianity to relegate faith to designated spiritual zones — the service, the small group, the quiet time — while treating the marketplace as morally neutral territory governed only by profit. This call exposes that lie. Every operational decision my brother and I made carried moral and spiritual weight: how we treat recruits, how we handle power, how we price honestly. If we accept the flesh’s map, we will guard our tongues in the sanctuary and abandon Christ’s Lordship the moment we open the laptop. *“Whatever you do, do it heartily, as to the Lord and not to men”* (Colossians 3:23) does not exempt the spreadsheet. **On the rarity and power of mutual confession.** *“It was both our fuck-ups. I see where I went wrong.”* We must be honest that this sentence is nearly extinct in our culture, and often no less rare among believers who prize being right over being reconciled. Too many partnerships, marriages, and churches decay because each party is waiting for the other to confess first. The correction here is sharp but loving: pride defended has never rebuilt a single relationship. *“Confess your trespasses to one another… that you may be healed”* (James 5:16) is not sentiment; it is the actual mechanism of restoration, and we abandon it at our peril. **On protecting the vulnerable over accelerating our gain.** My brother’s insistence that recruits keep their jobs and stability for three months rebukes an entire industry — and, if we are honest, an entire strain of aggressive Christian entrepreneurial culture — that pressures the weak to bet everything on someone else’s vision. The shepherd protects the sheep from the wolf; the hireling exposes them to it for profit. We are challenged to ask, in every enterprise: who bears the risk, and can they afford to lose? *“Do nothing out of selfish ambition… but in lowliness of mind esteem others better than himself”* (Philippians 2:3) must reach into our hiring and onboarding, not just our devotions. **On honest dealing and the branding question.** Grace first: the labor of management, curation, and service is genuinely valuable and worthy of wage. But we must not use that truth to sanctify concealment. If our pricing structure depends on the client never discovering a fairer source, we have crossed from adding value into obscuring truth. The challenge is to build enterprises where, if the client saw the whole picture, they would still gladly pay us for the real service we provide. *“Provide things honest in the sight of all men”* (Romans 12:17) is a high bar, and the Spirit invites us to build businesses that could survive full daylight. **On the Kingdom economy of the few.** We are addicted to scale, to hundreds, to viral growth, and this addiction infects our churches as much as our commerce. My brother’s clarity — *“they need a few closes, not hundreds”* — echoes the God who builds enduring things from mustard seeds and remnants. The challenge is to repent of despising small, faithful beginnings, to stop measuring fruitfulness by volume, and to tend the few well. *“You have been faithful over a few things, I will make you ruler over many things”* (Matthew 25:21). God multiplies what is faithfully tended, not what is frantically accumulated. **On patient formation over quick production.** *“We’re not going to treat it as a quick training course.”* The wisdom to form a person slowly, with weekly check-ins over months, rebukes our microwave discipleship — the conveyor-belt conversions and rushed leadership pipelines that produce shallow, brittle saints. If we will train a sales rep for three patient months, how much more should the church refuse to rush the formation of Christ in a soul? The Father’s παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) is slow on purpose, and we are called to imitate His patience with those entrusted to us. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity is learning to see the Lordship of Christ in the ordinary rooms of life, especially the exhausted ones. This call trains me to stop compartmentalizing — to recognize that the moment I lay down my ego mid-argument, protect a vulnerable recruit, submit a decision to another’s approval, or refuse to rush a person’s formation, I am being conformed to the image of Christ as truly as in any prayer meeting. Growth is not measured by how spiritual my language sounds but by whether the wisdom, patience, and honesty of God are actually shaping the concrete decisions I make when no one religious is watching. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience here takes structural form. It calls me to build processes that protect the weak rather than exploit them, to share power through dual approval rather than hoard it, to deal honestly even when concealment would be more profitable, and to confess my own faults quickly rather than defend my pride. The response the Spirit asks is not merely a feeling of conviction but a rebuilt foundation — to lay the deep footing of righteousness into every enterprise, so that when pressure comes, the house does not fall because it was founded on the Rock. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture learned here is discernment in the mundane — the trained ability to hear God speaking through a business call, to watch for the moral center of an ordinary decision, and to testify that no room is exempt from His reign. It teaches me to abide patiently as God abides patiently, to steward the few faithfully as God stewards remnants, and to remain a watchful, honest presence in the marketplace so that my brethren might see that the Kingdom is not confined to the sanctuary but leavens the whole loaf of a working life. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come as Your servant and co-heir, standing in the authority of the New Covenant sealed in the blood of Your Son, and I thank You that You did not despise a tired, imperfect conversation but were present in it, pressing Your wisdom through it. I confess the false division I have kept between my worship and my work, and I lay it down before You now; be Lord of my laptop, my ledgers, my partnerships, and my ambitions as fully as You are Lord of my prayers. Thank You for the grace that let two proud men confess fault to one another, and I ask that You would establish that grace as a permanent foundation between us, that our house would be built on the Rock and not the sand. Teach me the patience of Your παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah), that I would form people slowly and protect the vulnerable rather than exploit them for gain. Give me the honesty to build only what could stand in full daylight, and search me where concealment has crept into my dealings. Multiply the few things faithfully tended, O God, as You have always done, and keep me from the idolatry of scale that despises small beginnings. I make agreement with Your will that every field of my labor would become a place where Your Kingdom advances, and I offer this enterprise back to You as an altar. Bless my brother, keep him stable through his working days, and bind us together in wisdom and truth. To You be all glory, now and forever.
