# 08-16 Call Reflection: Elijah’s Wilderness and a Brother’s Midnight Collapse **Title:** 08-15 at 18:30 — The Juniper Tree and the Snail in the Bag **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation A brother reached across an ocean and a midnight hour, exhausted, half-collapsed, telling me he needs to go into the wilderness. I kept hearing Elijah beneath the juniper tree in every word he spoke, and I kept watching the small comedy of two snails eating his grandfather’s bag while he confessed that the whole world felt like it was pressing in on him. Somewhere between the divination booths I had walked past that afternoon and the collapse I heard in his voice, the Spirit drew a single thread: the flesh cannot cover itself, and only One can hold a man together. This is my reflection on what I heard, what I discerned, and what the Spirit pressed through that long call. **Why This Matters:** This matters because there is a particular kind of tiredness that the modern Christian is taught to treat as failure rather than as the very threshold of provision. My brother kept circling the same valley, kept calling himself a sinner and a Judas, kept trying to earn a worthiness that was never his to earn. If we do not learn to distinguish the wilderness of judgment from the wilderness of preparation, we will abandon our brothers precisely at the moment God is baking bread on the coals for them. The Body of Christ is not served by strong performances; it is served by saints who know how to fall on the cornerstone rather than be crushed by it. Discernment, endurance, and love all converge in the question of who holds a man when he has nothing left. And the same call that carried divination booths and jousting and snails and homicide statistics carried, underneath all of it, the ancient pattern of God meeting the exhausted prophet with food and sleep before any further journey. That is why this matters: because most of us are being asked to journey before we have been fed. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation Read naturally, this was simply a late-night phone call between two friends, one of them lonely, jet-lagged in the soul, and probably running on fumes and a sleeping pill. A psychologist would note the classic markers of burnout: a young man carrying financial and familial responsibility far beyond his years, self-medicating with alcohol in the past, now overwhelmed by a business that demands the work of ten men. The “wilderness” he craves would be read as a natural longing for retreat, a nervous system begging for rest. The snails and the spilled Coke would be a comic interlude, the random chaos of a bad night, nothing more. Even the spiritual language — the intercession, the answered prayer, the talk of familiar spirits at the Renaissance fair — would be filed under coping mechanisms, meaning-making, the mind’s way of imposing narrative on a hard season. It is a compassionate reading, and it is not entirely wrong about the exhaustion. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind can only measure the fatigue; it cannot perceive the pattern beneath it. It sees a tired man and a strange night, but it cannot see that the exhaustion is shaped exactly like Elijah’s, that the craving for wilderness is a summons and not merely a symptom. It reads the past collapse into alcohol as a habit to be managed, when the Spirit reveals it as a man trying to replace the one true escape with a counterfeit that only sinks him deeper. The worldly frame has no category for a prayer prayed in one hemisphere becoming the words spoken in another, no category for a familiar spirit that has watched a family for generations, no category for a cornerstone that either lifts or crushes depending on where a man is standing. What looks like a random bad night is, to the eyes the Spirit opens, a night thick with contested territory — and the God who bakes bread on coals is nearer than the fatigue admits. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I keep returning to the sound of his voice on that call, thick with a sleeping pill and a day lost to sleep, telling me across the midnight distance that he needs to get away, that he needs to go into the wilderness. He said it more than once, almost helplessly — *I seriously need to go into the wilderness, I need to go away for a while* — and each time he said it something in me leaned in, because a man does not repeat a word that many times unless the Spirit is pressing it up through him. He was surrounded by an atmosphere of darkness in his own home, holding up a family at an age when no one should carry that weight, and the world itself seemed to be reaching for him. And into that, almost absurdly, came two snails eating his grandfather’s bag and a Coke spilling across the floor, and he laughed and cursed and said the devil was attacking him tonight. What arrests me is that he was right about the smallness of it and wrong about its meaning at the same time. The snails were not the attack. The snails were the mercy — a piece of comedy dropped into the middle of a genuinely dark night, the kind of thing a grandfather already home with the Lord might find funny. I even told him that: maybe your granddad sent a snail as an angel to eat your bag. And underneath the laughter I could feel the Spirit doing what He always does with my brother, which is refusing to let him stay buried under the weight. The man who moments before wanted to unleash his wrath on two snails caught himself and said, *no, that’s not the biblical thing to do.* Even collapsed, even half-drugged with sleep, the new creature in him kept surfacing. The first echo that opened for me, and I said it to him plainly, was Elijah under the juniper tree. He had run for his life, he had said *it is enough, now, O LORD, take away my life,* and he lay down and slept. And the angel touched him and said, *Arise and eat,* and there was a cake baked on coals and a jar of water at his head. He ate, and he slept again, and the angel came a second time: *Arise and eat, because the journey is too great for you* (1 Kings 19:5–7). That is the wilderness my brother is in. Not the wilderness of a man being punished, but the wilderness of a prophet so spent that God will not even discuss the journey until the man has been fed twice and slept twice. I told him he needed a cake baked on coals, some sleep, to rise and eat again, and then he could journey on. The word for that wilderness in the Hebrew is מִדְבָּר (midbar, meed-BAR) — wilderness, desert, the uninhabited place. What steadies me is that מִדְבָּר shares its root with דָּבָר (davar, dah-VAR) — word, thing, matter. The place of desolation and the place of the spoken word are bound at the root, and this is no accident in the mind of God. The wilderness is precisely where the LORD speaks. Israel met God’s voice in the מִדְבָּר; John cried out in the מִדְבָּר; the Spirit drove Jesus into the מִדְבָּר and there the ancient words of Deuteronomy became living swords in His mouth. When my brother says he needs the wilderness, his flesh hears *escape* but his spirit is asking for the place where the דָּבָר can finally reach him without the atmosphere of his home drowning it out. And this is where the canon deepened under my hand as I listened. Elijah’s wilderness was not the end of his ministry but the hinge of it — after the bread and the sleep came Horeb, and the wind and the earthquake and the fire, and then the still small voice, the קוֹל דְּמָמָה דַקָּה, the sound of a thin silence (1 Kings 19:12). God was not in the dramatic collapse. He was in the quiet that came after the man had been fed. My brother is trying to fix the wrong end of the story; he thinks he must generate the strength to journey, when God is simply asking him to eat, sleep, and wait for the thin silence. The forty days and forty nights he keeps invoking are real and biblical, but Elijah did not enter them until an angel had fed him twice. The fast follows the feeding. Then there was the Renaissance fair I had walked through earlier that same day, and I told him about it because the Spirit had made it a field of discernment for me. Tarot readings, oracle cards, divination, people costumed as witches — the whole marketplace of the old world reassembled for entertainment and tips. My wife wanted to know what tarot was about, and I did not forbid her; I told her the truth and let the truth do its work. What is spoken there may be partially true, but it is a counterfeit truth, spoken by the familiar spirit that has watched a family across generations — even, I said, the familiar spirits of her family back in the Philippines, who could tell her things she would later verify and mistake for God. Scripture calls this out without flinching: *there shall not be found among you anyone who practices witchcraft, or a soothsayer, or one who interprets omens* (Deuteronomy 18:10). The Hebrew for these mediums, אוֹב (ob, OHV), names the spirits consulted by those who traffic in the dead, and the whole prohibition exists because the counterfeit is real, not because it is empty. The theological tension the whole call kept circling was the tension between self-covering and being covered. My brother kept trying to cover himself — with worthiness, with sobriety achieved by willpower, with the fantasy of his old life in Tennessee, and in the worst moments with drink. And I told him what the Spirit pressed into me: there is only one thing that can hold him, and it is not his own righteousness. This is why the Lord cursed the fig tree. That fig tree was never merely about fruit; it was a judgment on the covering, on the fig leaves Adam and Eve sewed for themselves in the garden. Their self-covering could not stand, and so *the LORD God made tunics of skin, and clothed them* (Genesis 3:21) — blood was required, an animal died, God Himself did the clothing. Self-covering is cursed. The covering He provides is life. And here the Kingdom mystery opened that the natural mind cannot touch: the cornerstone is not neutral. I told my brother that the cornerstone either lifts or crushes. *Whoever falls on this stone will be broken; but on whomever it falls, it will grind him to powder* (Matthew 21:44). If you are standing on Him, you fall on Him when you stumble, and He holds you. If you are not standing on Him, He falls on you. The difference between being broken unto healing and being ground to powder is not the quantity of your failures — it is your position relative to the stone. This is the thing my brother could not see while he was calling himself Judas: he was standing on the stone the entire time. His failures were falls *onto* the cornerstone, not the cornerstone falling on him. What genuinely undid me, and what I could only attribute to the glory of God, was what had happened the night before. He had called me drunk, twice, and between the calls I prayed — specifically that any spiritual doors opened by his new creation would be closed, and that he would purge whatever was poisoning him. And when he picked the phone back up, before I said anything, his first words were the literal answer to the prayer I had just prayed in secret, and then he said he needed to go throw up. As he was purging I prayed it out loud, and by the second call his whole identity had shifted — from *I feel like Judas, how can I be worthy* to *I have two loaves of bread in my kitchen, I’m going to shower and go to bed and repent.* I have sat with this man for three hours before, arguing him out of the devil’s doctrine word by word. This time I simply prayed, and it shifted. That is not technique. That is intercession answered in real time across the earth. I notice, sitting with it now, that the two loaves of bread in his kitchen were the same bread as Elijah’s cake on the coals. He said it without knowing what he was saying. The man who wanted the wilderness already had, in his own kitchen, the provision the wilderness would give — bread, rest, and the decision to return to the Lord and repent. The Spirit had staged the whole parable in his apartment: exhaustion, collapse, comic mercy, purging, provision, sleep. He was living the Elijah pattern in miniature and did not recognize it, and part of my calling as his brother is simply to name the pattern so he can stop despising the very rest God is handing him. There was more in the call that belongs to this same thread — the grief we shared over a world that produces a hundred and seven male suicides a day, one every thirteen minutes, four times the female rate. My brother said the world is so ugly, so cursed, so corrupt, and then he said something I have not stopped turning over: *it is us who have betrayed the world; the Lord’s nature has always been purified.* Creation groans not because God made it corrupt but because man handed it over. And into that groaning, he said, we still draw nearer — not giving up, but living. He said he wished to be nothing before the Lord, because everything of the world had only ever disappointed him. That is not despair. That is the beginning of the still small voice. For the Body, what I carry away is how much of this brother’s rescue happened through ordinary faithfulness — a phone that stays on at midnight, a prayer prayed in secret, a willingness to name Elijah when a man can only name Judas. We have brothers and sisters scattered across the earth whom we can simply call, and my brother marveled at that gift on the call itself. This is what the communion of saints actually looks like: not a doctrine but a phone ringing at an impossible hour, a snail becoming an angel, a man being fed bread he already owned. The saints hold one another up precisely by refusing to accept the devil’s re-framing of a brother’s identity, and by praying instead of merely arguing. ## Implications and Challenges **The wilderness is provision, not punishment — and we have taught the opposite.** My brother kept treating his need for retreat as evidence of failure, as though a strong Christian would not need to collapse. But Elijah’s angel did not rebuke him for lying under the juniper tree; the angel baked him bread. Much of modern Western discipleship has no theology of holy exhaustion, so it either shames the tired man or hands him a productivity plan. The correction is gentle but firm: sometimes the most obedient thing a servant can do is eat, sleep, and let God speak in the thin silence afterward. If we cannot bless a brother’s need for the מִדְבָּר, we do not understand how God prepares His prophets. **Self-covering is the most respectable sin in the church.** The man calling himself Judas was, in that very moment, sewing fig leaves — trying to generate a worthiness that would make him acceptable. Our churches are full of this dressed as sanctification: the endless self-improvement that never lets a saint rest in the tunics of skin God already made. The fig tree was cursed for exactly this. Begin with grace here, because the impulse to cover oneself comes from real shame, but let the correction land: your covering is cursed, and His covering cost blood. Stop sewing leaves and receive the skins. **Divination is not harmless entertainment, even at a fair.** I did not lecture my wife on Deuteronomy, but I told her the truth — that the counterfeit is real, that familiar spirits watch families across generations, and that a partial truth deployed to knock you off the path is more dangerous than an obvious lie. The Western church has largely lost this discernment, treating tarot and oracle cards as quirky fun. Deuteronomy 18 does not treat them as fun. This is not fear; I walked through those booths unafraid because I stand on the cornerstone. But unafraid is not the same as unwise, and we owe our households the truth about what is actually being consulted. **The cornerstone either lifts or crushes, and position is everything.** The gravest error I heard in my brother was not his drinking but his misreading of his own falls — he thought each stumble proved the stone was crushing him, when in truth he was falling *on* the stone that holds. Modern Christianity often collapses this into a flat message of unconditional acceptance that never mentions the grinding to powder, or into a harsh message of judgment that never mentions the lifting. Both miss Matthew 21:44. The question is not whether you have fallen; it is whether you are standing on Him when you do. Teach the saints their position, and their failures stop condemning them. **Intercession accomplishes what argument cannot.** I have spent three hours arguing this brother out of the devil’s doctrine and lost; I spent one night praying in secret and watched his identity shift before I opened my mouth. The activist reflex of the modern church is to explain, debate, and persuade the struggling into wholeness. But some doors only close by prayer, and some brothers only come home when someone stands between the heavens and the earth on their behalf. This is not passivity; it is the harder labor. The saints must recover the conviction that a prayer prayed in one hemisphere can become the words a brother speaks in another. **Grieving the world’s corruption is not the same as despairing of it.** My brother said he wished to be nothing before the Lord, and that this was not giving up but living. There is a false peace that ignores forty-six homicides and a hundred-and-seven suicides a day, and there is a false despair that drowns in them. Between them is the narrow path where we grieve with the Holy Spirit over His lost children and still draw nearer, still let Him speak light into the darkness through us. Let the correction here be tender: do not confuse holy grief with defeat, and do not let the numbers steal the hope that the Lord’s nature has always been, and remains, purified. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity is learning to recognize the juniper tree when we are lying under it — to know that collapse is not condemnation but the very place God bakes bread. My brother could not see that the two loaves in his kitchen were the answer to the wilderness he was begging for; he had the provision and despised it as evidence of weakness. We grow up when we stop reading our exhaustion as failure and start reading it as the threshold of the still small voice. The mature saint eats, sleeps, waits, and lets God speak, rather than manufacturing strength to journey before he has been fed. And the mature saint learns to fall *on* the cornerstone rather than fear being crushed by it, knowing that his position, not his performance, determines whether the stone lifts or grinds. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience here has two faces at once. For my brother it means the discipline of rest — showering, sleeping, returning to the Lord to repent, and continuing to build the work that other families now depend upon, refusing to abandon responsibility even as he tends his own soul. For me it means keeping the phone on at midnight, praying in secret, and speaking the pattern of Elijah over a man who can only speak the name of Judas. The obedience God is calling forth is not dramatic; it is a cake on the coals, a prayer prayed while a brother purges, a refusal to let the enemy re-frame a saint’s identity. We obey by holding one another up in the ordinary, faithful, unglamorous ways the communion of saints has always required. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this trains is the posture of the wilderness itself — the place where מִדְבָּר and דָּבָר share a root, where desolation and the word are bound together. We are learning to wait in the uninhabited places for the thin silence, to yield rather than strive, to let God feed us before we prophesy. My brother’s longing to be nothing before the Lord is the true prophetic ground; the voice comes not to the man building his own covering but to the man emptied of it. To watch, to abide, to intercede across the earth, to grieve with the Spirit over His lost children while still drawing near — this is how the saints remain faithful. The prophet is fed twice, sleeps twice, and only then hears the voice. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come to You as Your servant and Your son, a co-heir with Christ, standing on the cornerstone that lifts rather than crushes, and I lift my brother to You by name in the secret place. Thank You that You do not rebuke the exhausted prophet under the juniper tree but bake bread on the coals for him; thank You that the two loaves were already in his kitchen before he knew to ask. I thank You for the mercy hidden in small things — even a snail on a bag, even a spilled cup, even a laugh in the middle of a dark night that reminded him he is not abandoned. Close every door opened by the enemy against him, and let the covering You made of blood and skin silence forever the fig leaves he keeps trying to sew. Feed him twice, let him sleep twice, and then bring him into the wilderness where Your דָּבָר can reach him without the atmosphere of that house drowning it out. Guard him as he carries a weight no young man should carry, and provide the increase in the work so that the families who depend on us are held. Let him hear the still small voice and know it is Yours, and let him rise from the ashes of self-condemnation as the saint You have already made him. And for me, Lord, prepare me for the day ahead, feed me though I have not slept, and keep my hand on the plow and my knee on the ground for my brothers scattered across the earth. All of this I pray between the heavens and the earth, in agreement with Your will, in the name of Jesus Christ, come in the flesh, who covers what we cannot cover ourselves. Amen. claude-opus-4.8 ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |----------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | 1 Kings 19:5–7 | מִדְבָּר (midbar, meed-BAR) — wilderness / desert | The prophet is fed and rested twice before God speaks of any journey; the wilderness is preparation, not punishment. | | Deuteronomy 4–8; Matthew 4 | דָּבָר (davar, dah-VAR) — word / thing / matter | Shares a root with midbar; the desolate place is precisely where God’s word reaches man unhindered. | | 1 Kings 19:12 | קוֹל דְּמָמָה דַקָּה (qol demamah daqqah) — a sound of thin silence | God is found not in the collapse or the drama but in the quiet after the servant has been fed. | | Deuteronomy 18:10 | אוֹב (ob, OHV) — a medium / familiar spirit | Divination is forbidden because the counterfeit is real; familiar spirits traffic in partial truth to divert the soul. | | Genesis 3:21 | (tunics of skin) | Self-covering with fig leaves is cursed; God’s covering requires blood and is provided by Him alone. | | Matthew 21:44 | (the stone that breaks or grinds) | The cornerstone lifts those standing on Him and crushes those it falls upon; position determines outcome. | | Luke 15:7 | (joy over one sinner who repents) | Heaven rejoices more over one restored than over ninety-nine who need no repentance. |
