# 05-22 Call Reflection: Kingdom Ethics, Gray-Market Intelligence, and Vocation at the Edge **Title:** 05-22 at 08:15 — Standing at the Edge of the Cliff, Looking Toward France **Recorded:** 2026-05-22 08:15:25 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture / Call Reflection) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I am sitting with the memory of this call, and what strikes me most is not the geopolitical theorizing or the investigative ambition — it is the image of my brother standing at the edge of a cliff on a mountaintop, warning signs all around him, peering over toward France while I urged him closer to the edge. There is something in that picture that arrests me. We were two men separated by an ocean, one standing on a literal precipice, both of us talking about entering gray markets, intelligence networks, and the spaces where risk and righteousness are not easily sorted. What I am pressing into now is the question underneath the conversation: what does faithfulness to the dignity of humanity actually require of men like us, and what does the Holy Spirit say when ambition and conscience stand at the same cliff’s edge? **Why This Matters:** This matters because the people of God are not exempt from the world’s complexity. We are sent into it. The disciples were not called out of the mess of Roman occupation, tax corruption, zealot insurgency, and religious hypocrisy — they were called through it, and their witness was forged in the friction of real human systems. The question this conversation raises for discipleship is not whether to engage the broken structures of the world, but how — and under what authority, with what motives, and held to what standard. If the salt of the earth withdraws from every uncomfortable or morally complicated space, it has lost its savor. But if it enters without discernment, it risks being absorbed rather than transforming. This call, with all its lightness and laughter and strategic musing, is actually a threshold moment about vocation, integrity, and what it means to do good in the world’s gray spaces while remaining anchored to the Kingdom of God. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural standpoint, this conversation reads as two capable, well-traveled men with investigative instincts and international awareness entertaining an entrepreneurial idea. The world would assess this through the lens of risk management and market opportunity: identify a niche, leverage connections, minimize exposure to powerful agencies, and find the price point where discretion meets demand. The logic is pragmatic — find the parties in conflict, obtain the information, sell to the highest or most protected buyer, stay small enough to avoid the attention of the FBI or MI6, and operate in that gray zone where cash moves quietly and no one asks too many questions. To the natural mind, the ethical ceiling in this framework is essentially “don’t get caught” and “don’t provoke the wrong agency.” The fact that my brother and I would attach a human dignity clause to our participation registers, in this framework, merely as a personal preference or professional branding — a way to feel better about what we are doing rather than an actual moral absolute rooted in something transcendent. **The Limitation:** The problem with that framework is that it locates the moral authority for our choices entirely within ourselves — in our own assessment of what constitutes “betterment of people” and what “holds up the dignity of humanity.” But the natural mind operating in a gray market is also operating in a fog of its own making. The very environment that makes the work possible — the hush-money budgets, the anonymous journalism, the legend-playing intelligence culture — is an environment deliberately designed to obscure accountability. A conscience trying to navigate that space without an anchor outside itself is not a moral compass; it is a preference. Scripture does not condemn complexity or risk, but it does insist that the human heart is “deceitful above all things” (Jeremiah 17:9, NKJV), and that what feels like righteous motivation can become self-justification at the speed of a compelling opportunity. The Spirit is not content to let me stay in the fog of “so long as it’s for the betterment of people” without pressing me to ask: who defines betterment, and by whose authority do I enter these gates? ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with this conversation, the first thing the Spirit surfaces is the image of those warning signs on the cliff. My brother told me there were a million of them, and I encouraged him to climb over the fence anyway, half joking, half genuinely wanting to see the view. He was wise enough to stay back — to get as close as he reasonably could without crossing the barrier that was placed there for protection. I notice now that this small physical moment may be carrying more weight than either of us realized in the moment. There is a difference between standing near the edge to behold the view and vaulting the fence because the signs feel like obstacles rather than wisdom. The Spirit is asking me: in the larger conversation that followed, were we standing near the cliff, or were we quietly negotiating our way over the fence? I do not say this to condemn the conversation. Much of what my brother and I discussed reflects genuine gifts — pattern recognition, investigative instinct, international awareness, a willingness to engage systems that most people do not want to touch. These are not small things. Throughout Scripture, God deploys exactly these kinds of men: Nehemiah reading the geopolitical landscape of Persia and knowing how to move within it; Daniel operating at the highest levels of a pagan imperial intelligence structure without surrendering his covenant identity; the sons of Issachar, described in 1 Chronicles 12:32 as men “who had understanding of the times, to know what Israel ought to do.” The capacity to read a world system from the inside and act with discernment within it is a legitimate Kingdom calling. The question is always whether that capacity is submitted to the One who gave it. What arrests me in this conversation is the phrase I used toward its close: *“So long as it holds up the dignity of humanity.”* I meant it when I said it. I still mean it. But I am hearing the Spirit press gently on it now, because dignity of humanity is not a phrase the world and the Kingdom define the same way. The world’s definition of human dignity is largely anthropocentric — it derives from human rights frameworks, from the liberal democratic tradition, from the UN Declaration. These are not worthless; they are shadow impressions of something true. But the scriptural definition of human dignity is rooted in something far more specific: the אָדָם (adam, AH-dahm) — the man, the human being — made in the image and likeness of אֱלֹהִים (Elohim, el-oh-HEEM), the Creator God. Genesis 1:26-27 does not merely assign value to humanity; it declares that every human being is a bearer of divine image, a צֶלֶם (tselem, TSAY-lem) — a likeness, a representation, a living icon of God in the earth. That is what makes the fraud coming out of South Sudan not merely a crime but a desecration. That is what makes the heroin trade not merely a market inefficiency but an assault on image-bearers. And that is what would make any work we did in this space genuinely righteous rather than merely strategic — if it were done in fidelity to that origin. The work of exposing fraud, of tracing the supply lines of human exploitation, of shining light into the corners where powerful people exploit vulnerable ones — this is not foreign to the prophetic tradition. The prophets of Israel were, in a meaningful sense, the investigative journalists of their age. They traced the financial crimes of the powerful: Amos naming those “who sell the righteous for silver, and the poor for a pair of sandals” (Amos 2:6, NKJV). They penetrated the cover-up culture of corrupt governments: Jeremiah standing before kings and priests who had constructed an entire institutional language to conceal what was happening. They operated, as my brother suggested good journalists do, at the intersection between what is publicly knowable and what is deliberately obscured. The נָבִיא (nabi, nah-VEE) — the prophet, literally the one who speaks forth — was also the one who *saw* what others were paid not to see. That gift of sight is not secular. It is sacred. And yet — and this is where the Spirit does not allow me to simply baptize the ambition and move on — the prophets did not operate for private contracts or personal revenue. Their intelligence work, if I can call it that, was done under a commissioning. Isaiah did not freelance. Jeremiah did not sell information to competing factions. They spoke because they were sent, and the authority that backed their speaking was not derived from their network of sources or the quality of their journalism — it was derived from the One who stood behind their word. When I sit with the question of whether my brother and I should be entering this kind of work, I am not opposed to the work itself. I am pressing on the question of commissioning. Under whose authority? Toward what end? With what accountability? This is where the New Testament sharpens the question considerably. Paul writes in Romans 13:1 that “there is no authority except from God, and the authorities that exist are appointed by God” — and he writes this under a government that would eventually kill him for his witness. He is not naive about the corruption of human power structures. But he is insisting that even within those structures, the governing of nations is not outside God’s sovereign management. This means that if my brother and I were to do this work — investigating the heroin trade’s Afghani connections, tracing the gray-market flows of terrorist financing, alerting border agencies to trafficking routes — we would not be operating in a space God cannot see or govern. We would be operating in a space He has been overseeing since long before we arrived. The question then becomes: are we entering it as His agents, submitted to His discernment and His timing? Or are we entering it as entrepreneurs who happen to have moral preferences? I want to be honest with myself here, and with my brother if he reads this. The conversation was fun. It was genuinely fun, in the way that two men with sharp minds and a history of doing this kind of thing together find themselves energized by the possibility of re-entry. I felt it. The laughter about the old KGB gambit, the riffing on selling clean information to competing parties, the memory of what we had done together with that Muslim charity investigation — there was real delight in it, and I do not think delight is automatically disqualifying. Joy in one’s calling is not sin. But I also notice that the conversation moved very quickly from *“so long as it holds up the dignity of humanity”* to very practical discussions about how to structure the financial arrangement, how to protect ourselves from FBI scrutiny, and how to exploit the gap between white budget allocations and what appears on paper. The ethical guardrail was mentioned at the end, almost as a closing qualifier, rather than functioning as the foundational architecture of the whole enterprise. That sequence matters. The Book of Proverbs speaks repeatedly about the danger of plans formed in enthusiasm before wisdom is consulted. “The heart of man plans his way, but the LORD establishes his steps” (Proverbs 16:9, NKJV). There is nothing wrong with my brother making calls or pursuing contacts. There is nothing wrong with the investigative instinct or the entrepreneurial frame. But the Spirit is inviting me — us — to make sure that the Lord is being consulted at the foundation, not appended at the close as a kind of moral decoration. The commission must precede the contract. The prayer must come before the call. The question “is this what God has for us now?” must be asked with genuine openness to the answer being “not yet,” or “not this way,” or “yes, but here are the boundaries I am setting.” What I find genuinely moving about my brother is the way he ended the call. After all of that — the cliffs, the France-spotting, the espionage theorizing, the journalism network strategy — he said, “God bless.” And I said it back: “God bless, brother.” It was reflexive, perhaps, the way covenant language sometimes becomes habitual. But I do not think it was empty. I think it was actually true. We are men who know that we operate under a blessing that did not originate with us. We know that the work we have done together in the past — exposing fraud, challenging organizations siphoning resources meant for the poor — was meaningful because it was aimed at something real. The cliff is still there. The view toward France is still extraordinary. The warning signs are still posted. And the question the Spirit is pressing into me is not whether to behold the view, but whether I will behold it as a man under covenant authority or as a man who has decided the fence is merely advisory. There is one more thing I cannot leave unaddressed. My brother mentioned the word *legend* — the intelligence tradecraft term for a constructed false identity, a cover persona used in covert operations. He immediately said he would not go into legend. He drew a line there, instinctively and firmly. I think that instinct is prophetically significant and worth naming clearly. A man of covenant does not take on a false identity to accomplish even a righteous goal. This is not a minor tradecraft preference. It is a theological statement. We are men who bear a Name — who have been identified, sealed, and sent under the authority of the One who said “I AM WHO I AM” (Exodus 3:14, NKJV). The Name of God is not a legend. The seal of the Holy Spirit is not a cover identity. If the work we are considering requires us to become someone other than who we are in Christ to accomplish it, then the work itself has exceeded the boundaries of Kingdom ethics regardless of how righteous the outcome might seem. My brother already knows this. His instinct said it before his theology could articulate it. I am simply naming it here so it does not get lost in the excitement of making calls. The call ended with a rabbit. My brother saw a rabbit. I do not know what to do with that, except to say that Scripture is full of small, unexpected living things that show up at the edge of significant moments — ravens feeding Elijah, a fish carrying a coin, a donkey stopping at the presence of an angel. I am not projecting prophecy onto a rabbit sighting. But I notice it with a kind of warm attention, because the Spirit sometimes uses small things to say *I am here too, in this ordinary moment, in this conversation between brothers on two sides of an ocean, watching over this.* I believe He was. ## Implications and Challenges The ease with which ambition and ethical language can coexist in the same sentence without genuine integration is something this conversation surfaces with unusual clarity, and it deserves honest reflection among those of us who consider ourselves servants of the Kingdom. When “so long as it holds up the dignity of humanity” functions as a closing qualifier rather than a founding covenant, we are not operating under Kingdom ethics — we are operating under personal ethics with Kingdom branding. This is a subtle but serious distinction, and it is worth naming without condemnation and without softening. The tradition of treating covert or gray-market intelligence work as inherently incompatible with Kingdom vocation has always been too simple. Scripture does not support that simplicity. Rahab hid the spies and was commended for it. Jonathan fed David information at personal risk inside a hostile court. Esther navigated a pagan imperial structure with extraordinary political sophistication. The issue is never the complexity of the environment. The issue is always the authority under which one operates and the accountability to which one submits. Men and women of God who work in complex, morally ambiguous systems need *more* intentional spiritual accountability, not less — precisely because the environment itself will not provide it. Modern Western Christianity has largely taught believers to withdraw from morally complex civic spaces and call the withdrawal holiness. The result is that the very spaces where prophetic witness is most needed — intelligence communities, investigative journalism, geopolitical aid work, financial systems — are left almost entirely to those who have no covenant anchor. This is not faithfulness; it is abandonment. The challenge for men like my brother and me is not to avoid these spaces but to enter them as *sent ones* rather than as *self-dispatched entrepreneurs*, and to maintain that distinction with rigorous prayer, genuine accountability, and the willingness to hear “not this” or “not now” as a legitimate answer. The structural pressure toward secrecy in this kind of work — the off-paper cash deals, the anonymous journalist contacts, the deliberately unattributable information flows — is not inherently sinful, but it is inherently corrosive to accountability. What cannot be spoken of cannot be prayed over. What cannot be prayed over cannot be submitted to the Spirit’s correction. Men who carry secrets they cannot share with any spiritual covering are men who are functionally outside the protective canopy of covenant community, regardless of how righteous their intentions. This is not a reason to avoid all confidential work. It is a reason to be extraordinarily intentional about maintaining some space — some relationship, some prayer partner, some accountability — where the full truth can be told and the full cost can be assessed. Finally, the image of the cliff and the fence matters for all of us who feel the pull of the extraordinary — the dramatic, the high-stakes, the view from the edge that only a few people will ever see. There is genuine vocation in proximity to the edge. Some of us are called there. But the fence is not always the enemy of the view. Sometimes it is the grace that keeps the person alive long enough to tell others what they saw. Discernment is not cowardice. The wisdom to know which warning signs are institutional timidity and which ones are genuine protection — that is itself a gift of the Spirit, and it is worth praying for with the same urgency we bring to praying for open doors. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This conversation calls me — and any of us who carry investigative, prophetic, or systemic gifts — to develop the practice of commissioning before engagement. Spiritual maturity in complex callings means learning to distinguish between the excitement of capability and the peace of confirmed calling. It means holding ambition loosely enough that it can be redirected, retimed, or restrained without loss of identity. It means asking “is this for now?” as seriously as asking “is this good?” **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this moment calls for is not grand. It is the obedience of pausing long enough to pray before making the calls. Of identifying at least one person in covenant community who can know the full scope of what is being considered. Of establishing the ethical framework at the beginning of the planning, not as a closing caveat. Of being genuinely willing to hear the Spirit say “not yet” — and trusting that a door closed by the Holy Spirit is not lost opportunity but preserved timing. **For Prophetic Posture:** Those of us called to operate at the intersection of the Kingdom and the world’s broken systems must cultivate a prophetic posture of watchfulness — not paranoia, but discerning attention. We watch what the Spirit is already doing in the systems we are considering entering. We wait for the sense of commission that exceeds our own enthusiasm. We yield the timeline to the One who sees both the open door and what is standing behind it. And we remain faithful to the testimony of who we are: not legends, not cover identities, but men and women sealed with the Spirit of the living God, sent as His own into the world He is still redeeming. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who has been sealed and sent, as a co-heir of the Kingdom You are establishing in the earth, as a servant who knows that the secrets of what You are doing are revealed to those who fear Your Name. I thank You for my brother — for his sharpness, his courage, his instinct to draw lines at legends, his willingness to climb mountains and peer toward France and say “God bless” at the end of it all. I ask that You would be the architect of every plan we are considering — that You would be the One who places the commission before the contract, the prayer before the call, the discernment before the door. Where ambition has run ahead of Your appointment, bring it back under Your authority gently and completely. Where genuine vocation is stirring — where You are positioning us to enter complex systems as image-bearers who carry Your light — give us the courage to go, the wisdom to know the difference between a warning sign and a divine boundary, and the humility to remain accountable to those who can speak truth into what we cannot see about ourselves. May every system we touch be touched by the Kingdom that cannot be shaken. May every human being whose story intersects with our work be honored as a צֶלֶם of the living God. And may the grace that blessed us at the end of this call — God bless, brother, God bless — be not reflexive language but living covenant, the seal of Your Name on everything we do. In the Name above every name, and under the authority of the One who was, and is, and is to come.
