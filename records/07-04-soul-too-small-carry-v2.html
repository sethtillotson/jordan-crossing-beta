# 07-04 Teaching Reflection: Leanness of Soul, Purpose, and the Outward Life **Title:** 07-04 at 19:54 — The Soul Too Small to Carry Itself **Recorded:** 2026-07-04 19:54:08 **Source Discernment:** E — Recorded Sermon, Video, or Online Reel + Reflection **Classification:** Teaching Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to this teaching, something in me recognized the particular ache being described — the hollow echo that fills a life constructed entirely around its own comfort and advancement. The speaker names it precisely: *leanness of soul*. This is not the language of pop psychology or motivational culture; this is the language of Scripture pressing itself through a human voice into a very modern dilemma. What arrests me most is this — that a woman with three paid-off houses before age thirty-five, by every visible Western metric a success, was quietly starving. And the cure was not more prosperity. The cure was *purpose beyond herself*. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Western church has, in many seasons, inadvertently discipled believers into treating the gospel as a personal prosperity arrangement — a covenant designed chiefly for the individual’s elevation, safety, and abundance. When that framework is the operating system, success becomes a trap. Comfort becomes a ceiling. Wealth, untethered from purpose and others, becomes a particular kind of prison that looks like a palace from the outside. What the Spirit is revealing through this teaching is that the *imago Dei* — the image of God stamped into our design — is fundamentally *outward* in its orientation. We were not made to be containers. We were made to be conduits. And when we live only as containers, something deep and God-given begins to atrophy. The saints need to hear this not as a guilt trip but as a *diagnostic* — a holy exposure of why so many believers who have received so much still feel so little. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind hears this teaching and nods along as self-help wisdom. It categorizes the message under “purpose-driven living,” “philanthropy as therapy,” or “meaning-making through social contribution” — the kind of insight that fills TED talks and bestselling memoirs about burnout recovery. From a purely psychological frame, what happened to the woman in this story is explainable through Maslow’s hierarchy: once survival and security needs are met, the human psyche naturally orients toward belonging, contribution, and self-actualization. The discomfort she felt — the leanness — is simply what behavioral scientists call *hedonic adaptation*: the diminishing return on external achievements. The solution, then, is to find a cause that re-engages the reward centers of the brain. From this vantage point, the trip to Lagos, the children’s scholarships, the renewed energy at work — these are positive behavioral interventions. Meaningful, even admirable. But fundamentally self-directed: she felt empty, she found a strategy, she felt better. **The Limitation:** The natural frame cannot see what the Spirit is actually doing in this story, because the natural frame has no category for *design* — for the truth that this ache is not a malfunction but a *signal from the Maker*. When we reduce the leanness of soul to a neurochemical problem, we rob the sufferer of the most important piece of information: that their dissatisfaction is *holy*. It is the cry of a derivative being trying to function independently of the selfless God from whom they are derived. The natural mind can prescribe philanthropy and arrive at similar external outcomes, but it cannot lead a person to the actual source — cannot tell them that the energy they feel when they give is not a psychological reward loop but the faint, blessed resonance of a soul moving in alignment with its *telos*, its created purpose. Only the Spirit can make that distinction, and only that distinction changes everything. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I want to begin where the speaker begins — with a phrase he uses almost in passing, as though it were obvious: *“You are a derivative of a selfless God.”* I cannot move past this too quickly. The word *derivative* is doing extraordinary theological work here. A derivative does not exist independently; its nature and properties flow from its source. And if the source is selfless — if the very essence of God is, as John declares, ἀγάπη (agapē, ah-GAH-pay) — covenantal, self-giving, other-oriented love — then a creature derived from that God carries ἀγάπη (ah-GAH-pay) as their deepest structural reality. Not as a virtue to be acquired. As a *nature* to be expressed or suppressed. This means that a life lived only for oneself is not merely an ethical failure. It is an ontological contradiction. It is a river trying to flow uphill against its own nature. The psalmist gives us the name for what the woman was experiencing. Psalm 106:15 records something sobering about Israel in the wilderness: “He gave them their request, but sent leanness into their soul.” The Hebrew word there is רָזוֹן (razon, rah-ZONE) — a wasting, an emaciation of the inner man. Israel demanded what they wanted, God gave it to them, and they grew lean on the inside even while their mouths were full. This is the precise condition the speaker is describing. It is possible to accumulate everything the appetite desires and simultaneously starve the soul — because the soul’s hunger is not for possessions. The soul was made to *participate in the life of God*, and the life of God is radically, beautifully, irreversibly for others. I am struck by the geography of the cure. The speaker sends this woman not to a counselor’s office or a meditation retreat — he sends her to Lagos. Specifically, to Makoko. There is a prophetic instinct in that redirection that I want to honor. Lagos does not allow you to remain theoretical about human suffering. Makoko — a floating slum on the Lagos lagoon, one of the largest in West Africa — is the kind of place that strips away abstraction and places you face-to-face with the weight of the world. And what happened when she arrived there? She did not just feel sad for others. She caught vision. She made decisions. She found energy. The encounter with need ignited something that three houses and a prestigious career had never touched. This is not accidental. This is how God designed the soul to function: in contact with the suffering of others, the latent ἀγάπη (ah-GAH-pay) locked in our nature finds its rightful expression and the soul becomes, at last, fully alive. Jesus addresses this directly in what I consider one of the most counterintuitive declarations in the entire canon: “For whoever desires to save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for My sake will find it” (Matthew 16:25). The Greek word translated “life” in both instances is ψυχή (psychē, psoo-KHAY) — soul, inner life, the seat of appetite and consciousness and identity. The paradox is architectural: the very thing you try to protect by keeping it for yourself is the thing you destroy in the keeping. And the very thing you release for the sake of Christ and His Kingdom is the thing that returns to you multiplied and whole. This is not a spiritual riddle. It is a description of how ψυχή (psoo-KHAY) actually functions. The soul was built for expenditure, not hoarding. And when we hoard it, it wastes. The speaker makes an observation that most motivational culture would not have the theological courage to make: he says, in essence, that the woman’s life had contracted to the size of one person — herself — and that *you are too small to be a vision*. I want to sit with that phrase. There is something in it that echoes the way Paul writes about the Body of Christ in 1 Corinthians 12 — that the eye cannot say to the hand “I have no need of you,” that the body does not consist of one member but of many. A single member trying to be the whole body is not a triumphant individual. It is a broken body. And a life organized exclusively around its own advancement is not a flourishing life. It is a life that has amputated itself from the Body it was meant to serve and be served by. What moves me further is what the speaker says about the shift in how she approached her work after the Makoko trip. She was no longer working *for* herself — she was working *for* something larger. And the fatigue that had been deadening became energy. This maps precisely onto what Paul describes in Colossians 3:23: “And whatever you do, do it heartily, as to the Lord and not to men.” The word translated “heartily” is ἐκ ψυχῆς (ek psychēs, ek psoo-KHEECE) — literally, *from the soul*. Work done from the soul, in alignment with God’s purposes beyond oneself, carries a different quality of energy than work done merely for personal gain. It is animated from a different source. And that source does not tire the way self-serving ambition does, because it is drawing from something that does not run dry. I notice the speaker makes a subtle but significant observation about wealth and the pursuit of money. He says that those who chase money find that money runs from them, but those who see a problem and ask *how can I make life better* — those people carry a confidence that draws investment and favor. This is not merely a business principle. It is a Kingdom dynamic. Solomon says in Proverbs 11:24–25: “There is one who scatters, yet increases more; and there is one who withholds more than is right, but it leads to poverty. The generous soul will be made rich, and he who waters will also be watered himself.” The economy of the Kingdom is not zero-sum. It is generative. What is given away multiplies. What is hoarded diminishes. And the one who carries a vision larger than their own survival walks with a different kind of authority than the one who is merely surviving — because they are not desperate for what they can get from you. They already belong to something greater. There is also a quiet prophetic diagnosis in the speaker’s aside about Nigeria — about how exposure to suffering can numb you, how a child knocking on a windshield for money at 2 p.m. can become invisible to someone who has lived inside that environment long enough. He names this carefully: *it is not normal, mentally*. He is not condemning those who have been numbed by proximity to suffering. He is naming a spiritual danger: that familiarity can destroy compassion. And this danger is not geographically limited to Lagos. It exists wherever the heart becomes callous through repeated exposure without intentional re-sensitization. The church must guard against this. We must not become so accustomed to the brokenness around us that we stop being moved by it. The prophet Amos warned a comfortable Israel that had grown numb to injustice while lying on beds of ivory and eating the finest food (Amos 6:4–6) — and the word of the Lord came not with affirmation but with grief: “they are not grieved for the affliction of Joseph.” Numbness in the face of suffering is a form of spiritual atrophy. We must pray for hearts that remain tender. The woman’s story ends with a particular kind of restoration that I want to name carefully: it is not that her circumstances changed. She still has the job, presumably still has the houses. What changed is the meaning framework inside of which those things now exist. She is no longer a person who has things. She is a person who has a purpose, and the things serve the purpose. This is the difference between what the book of Ecclesiastes calls הֶבֶל (hebel, HEH-vel) — vapor, vanity, breath — and what Proverbs 19:21 calls the counsel of the LORD that stands. When a life is organized around ψυχή (psoo-KHAY) alone — around self-preservation and self-advancement — it becomes hebel. It is real while you hold it, but it leaves nothing behind when you open your hand. But when a life is organized around a purpose rooted in the character of the selfless God, it becomes something that outlasts the individual. It becomes legacy. It becomes seed. I want to end this section of the meditation by returning to where the speaker ends: the posture of the person who does not sound like they are hustling, who does not carry the desperation of the self-seeking. There is a quality of rest, of settledness, of confidence that belongs to the person whose life is genuinely larger than themselves. And I believe this is, at least in part, what Jesus means when He speaks of bearing fruit that remains (John 15:16). The branches that abide in the vine do not strain. They do not hustle for sap. They receive it, and they bear from it, and what they bear is not for themselves but for the table — for others who need nourishment. The abiding life is the giving life. And the giving life is the only life that does not end up lean. ## Implications and Challenges There is a version of the prosperity gospel and even of ordinary evangelical Christianity that trains believers to receive rather than to carry — to accumulate blessing rather than to become a blessing. This teaching challenges that formation with gentleness but with clarity. If the structure of our discipleship produces people who ask primarily “what can God do for me,” we have produced people who are structurally vulnerable to the leanness of soul described in this message. True discipleship must train believers in the direction of their design: outward, generous, sacrificially engaged with the world around them. The speaker’s diagnosis — *you are too small to be a vision* — is a direct challenge to the cult of individualism that saturates both Western culture and, tragically, much of Western Christianity. A faith that fits inside the perimeter of one person’s comfort and advancement is not the faith of the apostles, the prophets, or Jesus Himself. We must honestly examine whether our life vision is large enough to require God — or whether it is small enough to fund ourselves. It would be easy to hear this message and apply it only to people of significant material wealth — to say, “well, this is for rich people who have lost meaning.” But that is too convenient an escape. The principle is universal. Any life organized exclusively around its own survival, security, or advancement — regardless of the size of the bank account — is a life moving against its own God-designed grain. The question is not *how much do you have* but *what is it for*. The speaker’s observation about numbness — about how proximity to suffering without intentional compassion can produce spiritual callousness — is a challenge for the church in every comfortable Western context. We must cultivate practices of re-sensitization: proximity to the poor, cross-cultural encounter, lament, intercession for the suffering of others. Without intentional disciplines of compassion, the heart learns to look through rather than at the human beings in front of it. Finally, this meditation surfaces a challenge to the way we measure success within the Body of Christ. When position, platform, title, and prosperity become the primary markers of God’s blessing, we inadvertently create a culture where the most self-giving, others-oriented, “scattering” members of the Body are invisible — while those who have accumulated the most are most loudly celebrated. The Kingdom inverts this. The one who waters is the one who is watered. The one who scatters is the one who increases. We must cultivate eyes to see and honor the kind of fruitfulness that looks like expenditure. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity is not primarily the accumulation of theological knowledge or spiritual experiences. It is the progressive expansion of the self — the growing capacity to carry something larger than personal comfort. The mature believer is not someone who has arrived at a place of sufficient blessing, but someone whose interior life has grown large enough to hold others within it without losing themselves. This teaching calls us to examine honestly: is my life getting larger or smaller? Is my vision expanding beyond myself, or contracting toward myself? Maturity moves outward. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this teaching demands is concrete and embodied. It is not enough to intellectually affirm that life should be about more than oneself. The woman in this story did not recover from leanness of soul through correct doctrine. She recovered through action — through getting on a plane, going to Makoko, putting her resources under children who had none. Kingdom obedience in response to this word looks like finding and sustaining concrete, costly, other-directed commitments: sponsorships, service, presence with the poor, investment in others’ flourishing. The vision must take flesh. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this word cultivates is one of holy discontent with smallness — a restlessness that refuses to let the life of God be confined within self-serving boundaries. The saints who will carry the prophetic witness of the Kingdom into the next generation will not be those who have made the most of their opportunities for personal advancement. They will be those whose lives became answerable to something — and Someone — greater than themselves. To remain in that posture requires sustained proximity to Jesus, sustained awareness of the world’s need, and sustained willingness to be spent. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I stand before You as one who has been made in the image of a selfless God, and I confess that the pull toward smallness — toward a life organized around my own comfort, security, and advancement — is real and daily. I thank You that You have not left us to figure this out alone: You have given us Your Word, Your Spirit, and the witness of those who have discovered that the scattered life is the fruitful life. I pray now in agreement with Your will as it stands written and revealed: let the leanness of soul that afflicts so many of Your children — the hollow echo of a life too small to carry itself — be met with the same cure You prescribed in this word. Send us to our Makokos. Expand our vision beyond ourselves. Let the ψυχή You have breathed into us find its rightful expression in the ἀγάπη that flows from You and returns to You through us. Make us those who scatter and increase, who water and are watered, who lose their lives and find them — truly find them — in You. Let no member of Your Body remain lean when You have made us to be conduits of the life of God. To You be the glory, in the Body and in Christ Jesus, throughout all generations.
