# 08-28 Call Reflection: The Third Door, the Leveled Table, and the Island of Only Faith **Title:** 08-28 at 22:11 — The Third Door, the Leveled Table, and the Island of Only Faith **Recorded:** 2026-08-28 22:11:51 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I sat with my brother Samuel across a long midnight call while something broke open in him that I had been interceding over for weeks. He told me, half-laughing, half-trembling, that he thinks he and Emily have fallen back in love — that the hatred between them had been exposed as heartbreak wearing a mask — and then his voice broke apart entirely, because for the first time in months he could cry. He kept saying it like a man testing whether a floor would hold his weight: *I finally see the Lord.* And before the night was through, the word turned back on me, and I found myself confessing that the thing I fear most is not God’s judgment but His mercy — that I am being called from servant to friend to son, and that being loved that completely terrifies me more than any hardship. **Why This Matters:** This matters because so much of what we call anger, contempt, and vengeance in the household of God is actually love that has been wounded and has nowhere to go, and because so much of our discipleship is spent striving to complete a work Christ has already finished. My brother said something I cannot shake — “I hate you because I love you” — and in that confession is a diagnosis of half the broken relationships among the saints. It matters because the modern Body of Christ has quietly exchanged the ancient, terrifying, liberating practice of confession for private management of sin, and my brother’s chains fell the second he spoke his secret aloud. It matters because he had reduced his future to two doors, prison or death, papers or ruin, and the God of Joseph specializes in the third door no one can see until it opens. It matters because the fear of the Lord and the fear of the flesh can wear the same clothing, and a disciple who cannot tell them apart will freeze at the threshold of encounter. And it matters because the Body of Christ needs sons who can bear the weight of being loved, not only servants who can bear the weight of being commanded. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind hears this call and constructs a tidy emotional narrative: two people who separated, hurled cruelty at one another to mask their pain, and then experienced a cathartic reconciliation fueled by loneliness and nostalgia. A therapist might rightly note the projection, the way Emily posted provoking things online precisely to wound because she could not admit she was hurting, and the way my brother’s “hate” was displaced grief. It would read his tears as simple catharsis, the nervous system releasing months of stored tension, and his confession to me as helpful only in the way any good conversation with a trusted friend is helpful — lowering stress, offering perspective and social support. The talk of “chains,” “deliverance,” “the third door,” and answered prayer would be filed under poetic metaphor, or under the mind’s tendency to assign meaning to coincidence and impose coherence on chance. It would read his fear before holiness as unresolved psychological tension dressed in spiritual language, perhaps the ache of a fatherless heart seeking a cosmic Father, and the two brothers naming the same battle across distance as mere codependence. There is genuine compassion available in this reading, for it does not deny the man’s suffering or his joy; it simply refuses to let either of them mean anything beyond the machinery of the human heart. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind can only measure the visible transaction between two damaged people; it cannot see the Advocate who has been praying through the whole storm. It can diagnose displaced grief but has no category for a covenant that binds two people as one flesh across thousands of miles even when one of them is shouting for hell to swallow the other. It reads coincidence where the Word reads providence, because it has no category for a prayer prayed in secret at six-fifteen that named the exact chain the Spirit would break before Samuel ever dialed. It reduces confession to venting and reconciliation to good fortune, when Scripture insists that spoken truth in the name of Christ is a hinge upon which unseen doors swing. And it reads the trembling before holiness as pathology, when Scripture reads it as the beginning of wisdom — unable to conceive that a plan which “seems unkindly” might be the very kindness of a Father refining His son. What looks like emotional release from below looks like deliverance from above, and the same set of facts yields two entirely different worlds, only one of which is true, and the truer one is invisible to anyone insisting on proof before belief. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I keep returning to the sound of my brother’s voice when he said he felt like he was going to cry and was embarrassed to admit it. There was a man who had spent months armored in fury, and in the space of one honest conversation the armor simply fell off and there was just a husband who missed his wife. He kept circling back to it, unable to leave it — “I’ve missed her so much, man. You don’t get it.” And the repetition was not weakness; it was a heart finally allowed to say the true thing after months of saying the false thing. He told me he admitted his love expecting rejection, and instead was met with equal love, and the shock of it broke him open on the spot. What arrests me is that the very words he used to describe the old state were the words of a man who had mistaken his own heartbreak for hatred. “I hate you because I love you,” he told her — and that single sentence unlocked her too. The contempt between them was never the opposite of love; it was love with no permission to speak. When two people each wait for the other to apologize first, the waiting itself curdles into a spirit of contempt, and each begins attacking the one they most long to embrace. The truth did not create the love. The truth simply removed the mask that had been strangling it. The first thing Scripture presses into this moment is the Lord’s own teaching about what truly defiles a man. Jesus said, “Those things which proceed out of the mouth come from the heart, and they defile a man” (Matthew 15:18). Emily screamed “go to hell” and “send the papers,” and yet her actual heart, as she later confessed, wanted neither. The mouth became the enemy’s instrument even while the heart stayed anchored. The word Jesus uses for defile is κοινόω (koinoō, koy-NOH-oh) — to make common, to render profane what was meant to be holy. It shares its root with κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-nohn-EE-ah) — fellowship, the holy sharing of life. What the enemy did through their mouths was take the holy communion of one-flesh marriage and drag it into the common gutter of insult — and yet the covenant held. That covenant is the ground of the promise I reminded him of, the word about a believing spouse covering an unbeliever. Paul writes, “For the unbelieving husband is sanctified by the wife, and the unbelieving wife is sanctified by the husband… For how do you know, O wife, whether you will save your husband?” (1 Corinthians 7:14, 16). The Greek behind sanctified is ἁγιάζω (hagiazō, hag-ee-AD-zoh) — to set apart as holy, to consecrate. Paul is not saying the unbeliever is automatically saved; he is saying the believing spouse’s steadfast faith becomes a canopy of consecration over the marriage, a holding-open of the door of grace. My brother’s yearning, kept faithful even through betrayal, was functioning as exactly this canopy over a woman who told him to burn. This traces backward through the whole canon, because the picture of a faithful covenant partner loving a wayward one is the oldest story God tells about Himself. Hosea was commanded to love a wife who wandered: “Go again, love a woman who is loved by a lover and is committing adultery, just like the love of the LORD for the children of Israel” (Hosea 3:1). The prophet’s aching, humiliating faithfulness to Gomer was a living parable of God’s covenant love toward a people who provoked Him to test whether He would let go. My brother, in his yearning through betrayal, has been walking an unwitting Hosea road, and did not know he was preaching a sermon with his heartbreak. But the deepest thing the Spirit unveiled was the intercession my brother never saw. I told him plainly what has been pressed on me for weeks: that the reason his faith did not shatter in the pit is that Jesus Christ Himself has been praying for him. Jesus said to Peter, “Simon, Simon! Indeed, Satan has asked for you, that he may sift you as wheat. But I have prayed for you, that your faith should not fail” (Luke 22:31–32). The verb for asked is ἐξαιτέομαι (exaiteomai, ex-eye-TEH-oh-my) — to demand out, to claim someone for oneself. Satan claimed Peter; Satan claimed my brother. And against that demand stood the prayer of the Son, whose intercession does not fail. The enemy does not launch a catapult at an empty field; the barrage of accusation my brother endured was the fingerprint of an enemy attacking something genuinely worth attacking. And here is the mystery natural reasoning would never catch: the answer God gave was not vindication at all. My brother himself said it — “I like hearing this a lot better than vindication.” The flesh wanted to be proven right. The Spirit gave him something better: his wife’s honesty and his own broken-open heart instead of the cold satisfaction of being justified. This is where the Joseph shadow he named becomes almost unbearable in its precision. He said, “It’s like you sent me through hell just to show me this. You were Joseph. It’s like everything was for this.” Joseph told his brothers, “You meant evil against me; but God meant it for good” (Genesis 50:20). The Hebrew for meant is חָשַׁב (chashav, khaw-SHAV) — to weave, to reckon, to devise a plan. The same weaving intended for destruction, God re-wove into deliverance on the same loom. After our last call, my exact prayer was that God would show up with a third door — because my brother had convinced himself there were only two, prison or death. And the God of Joseph specializes in the door no one can see until it opens. Then he told me something about the mechanism of it that I cannot move past. He did not say his healing came when his circumstances changed. He said, “The second I confessed to you, it came.” He had carried a secret he called his biggest secret, assuming that speaking it would cost him everything — that the man he told would recoil, would stand above him, would use it against him. Instead he found that the confession itself was the healing. The first Scripture that opened as he spoke was James: “Confess your trespasses to one another, and pray for one another, that you may be healed” (James 5:16). I have read that verse a hundred times and treated it as advice. That night I heard it as a law of the Kingdom, as fixed as gravity. The word for confession is ἐξομολογέω (exomologeō, ex-oh-moh-loh-GEH-oh) — to speak out fully, to acknowledge openly; the ἐξ carries the sense of *out* — the truth drawn from darkness into the open air. This is what David lived: “I acknowledged my sin to You, and my iniquity I have not hidden… And You forgave the iniquity of my sin” (Psalm 32:5). The hiding was the sickness; the acknowledgment was the cure. And Jesus promised, “there is nothing covered that will not be revealed” (Luke 12:2) — a warning with mercy hidden inside it, for we may drag the thing into the light ourselves before it is dragged out in judgment. Then came the tension the Spirit would not let me avoid, because it was my own. My brother kept saying he trusted me more than anything, and I had to correct him: “You should trust Christ more than anything.” I told him I was weaning him off the milk of using his brother as a crutch. And I could say it because I had just been through my own severe schooling — sixty minutes of what felt like a battering from a legion of high rank, the Lord standing there letting me take it until I finally understood. I had let a gift received quietly curdle into knowledge earned, a kind of Gnostic belief that made me think I had a part to play. And the Lord let me be beaten until I got it: “I’m just a signpost. There’s no playing my part. It’s done. I just point to Him.” Ministry that heals is ministry that refuses to stand above the sinner. This is the pattern of the Incarnation itself — the One who “made Himself of no reputation, taking the form of a bondservant” (Philippians 2:7), the verb ἐκένωσεν (ekenōsen, eh-KEH-noh-sen), emptied. A signpost does not save; a table cannot be set down upon if it is held up above the head of the one who needs to lay something on it. When my brother confessed, I did not receive it as a righteous man looking down; I confessed in the same direction, and leveled the table so he could set his burden on it. As the night deepened, Samuel got down onto his actual knees mid-prayer — *let me get on my knees for it, Lord* — thanking God not primarily for the restored marriage but for a *full circle moment of when I doubted you the most.* He said the marriage itself was not even the point: *it’s not even Emily. It’s the fact that he showed me.* That is the language of a man who has stopped bargaining, moved from petition to communion. When I read from Job, it was simply where the Spirit pressed: “I have heard of You by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees You. Therefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes” (Job 42:5–6). Job says this not after receiving explanations — God never explains the whirlwind — but after receiving God Himself. The Hebrew is רָאָה (ra’ah, rah-AH) — to see, to perceive, to be caused to see — the same root behind יְהוָה יִרְאֶה (Yahweh Yireh, yah-WEH yir-EH), the God who provides on the mountain. To be *shown* by God and to *see* God are, in Hebrew, nearly the same act. Samuel kept saying *He showed me,* and the Scripture answers: yes, and in being shown, you are seeing Him who provides. Then he told me Joseph had spoken to him in a dream the night before — of fatherly and brotherly love, of trusting your brothers — and the layers stacked, because I had prayed hours earlier over the third door, *the door Joseph found,* not knowing he had already dreamed of Joseph. The Spirit was writing the same word in two places at once. And this is why his testimony that *we are already saved, our only job is to be with the Lord* is not passivity but the deepest doctrine. Christ from the cross cried τετέλεσται (tetelestai, teh-TEL-es-tai) — *it is finished, paid in full* (John 19:30). The striving to complete a finished work is the very anxiety of the flesh laboring at a cross already carried. Samuel reached the hardest commandment too — not merely to forgive enemies but to love them — grasping why: *our sins, atom by atom, held Him against the cross, and for that very reason we are saved.* Paul writes that “while we were still sinners, Christ died for us” (Romans 5:8), and “while we were enemies we were reconciled” (Romans 5:10). I said it and meant it: *we’re Barabbas.* The guilty man walks free while the innocent takes his cross. He also insisted, and I want to hold it carefully, that *today’s church is wrong* — that Christ is found with *a man and his problems,* not in ceremony for ceremony’s sake. He is right that the idolatry of ceremony can starve a man standing in the sanctuary, and right that God is *natural, not supernatural* — the great I AM, אֶהְיֶה אֲשֶׁר אֶהְיֶה (’ehyeh ’asher ’ehyeh, eh-YEH ah-SHER eh-YEH), Being itself (Exodus 3:14) — found not in wind, earthquake, or fire but in a still small voice, in fifty pounds enough for food, in a returned wife, in a late-night call. Then the word turned back onto me, and this is where I had to stop being the strong one. My brother called with a word he said was not his own — that I still fear what I do not know in the Spirit, and that though the Father’s plan may seem unkindly, I am asked to give my whole heart. He handed it to me trembling, then stopped himself — “I know where my place ends to speak.” And I heard myself answer almost before I had thought it: “I believe I fear the mercy of Jesus Christ most.” Not His judgment. His mercy. I described closing my eyes in prayer and seeing flashes of light, feeling a presence beside me, and how the moment I look to verify, I break the beginning of the encounter. He saw it before I did: “Who but a paranoid person would believe that? I believe you’re paranoid in the face of the Lord.” He was not mocking me; he was loving me enough to name the thing I had spiritualized so carefully I had hidden it from my own sight. The Scripture that opens over this is John on Patmos: “And when I saw Him, I fell at His feet as dead… But He laid His right hand on me, saying to me, ‘Do not be afraid’” (Revelation 1:17). The same hand that could strike a man dead reaches down to lift him. The word my brother carries tattooed on his own body is the one the Spirit pressed into the center of it — νήφω (nēphō, NAY-fo) — to be sober, watchful, clear-headed, unintoxicated. Peter joins it to vigilance: “Be sober, be vigilant; because your adversary the devil walks about like a roaring lion” (1 Peter 5:8). But the Spirit was disentangling two watchfulness in me. There is the sobriety that guards against the lion, and the false vigilance that guards against the Lamb — and I had let the second masquerade as the first. “There is no fear in love; but perfect love casts out fear, because fear involves torment” (1 John 4:18); the Greek for that tormenting fear is φόβος (phobos, FOH-bos), the dread that expects punishment. Against it stands יִרְאַת יְהוָה (yir’at YHWH, yir-AHT ah-doh-NAI), the fear of the LORD, “the beginning of wisdom” (Proverbs 9:10), which David calls “clean, enduring forever” (Psalm 19:9). My brother made the very distinction the Apostle makes: “Your fear is built off honor. Your fear is not built off redemption.” The reverence is not the problem. The problem is when reverence stops trusting, when awe forgets that the One who is holy is also my Father. Here the doctrinal tension opened wide, because my brother named the exact transition I could barely name myself — I am moving from bond-servant to friend to son. “No longer do I call you servants… but I have called you friends” (John 15:15), and further still, “you have received the Spirit of adoption by whom we cry out, ‘Abba, Father’” (Romans 8:15). I confessed the strange truth: I could be a friend of Jesus all day, but sonship, and the inheritance that comes with it, is harder to accept. A servant fears the master’s power, a friend enjoys the master’s confidence, but a son must bear the master’s love — and being loved that completely is more exposing than any command. The Kingdom mystery hidden in this is that the very sin I keep noticing is evidence of the Spirit’s work, not its absence. The enemy whispers that because I still sin, God has done nothing — but “sin shall not have dominion over you, for you are not under law but under grace” (Romans 6:14). The fact that I now hate the sin is itself proof that the Spirit indwells me. The παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) — fatherly discipline — that “seems unkindly” is precisely how the Father treats a son and not a stranger: “whom the LORD loves He chastens, and scourges every son whom He receives” (Hebrews 12:6). The unkindness is the kindness; the chastening is the adoption papers being signed in my flesh. Then came the moment that broke both of us open, because my brother refused to let me stand above him. When I confessed my fear, he said, “Every inch of fear which you feel is shared with myself. I am him, and you are he. We are one another.” This is not sentimentality; this is the mystery of the Body: “For as the body is one and has many members… so also is Christ” (1 Corinthians 12:12). He made me sit down beside him in the same dust, two men still learning what it is to be loved, and said, “let us learn through grace, not through fear.” My subtle sense of being “further along” was itself a hiding place — “maybe this is some hidden pride in me,” I admitted. And the Father used my brother, the one who called himself “the biggest sinner,” “a brother condemned by Christ,” to level the ground between us. “The first will be last, and the last first” (Matthew 20:16); “the members of the body which seem to be weaker are necessary” (1 Corinthians 12:22). The one I might have counted behind me was the very vessel the Father chose to carry the word that would set me free. For the beloved brethren this carries a weight far beyond one marriage or one man’s fear, because we are surrounded by saints armored in contempt that is really unspoken heartbreak, and by disciples managing their shame alone behind a facade. My brother’s chains fell not in a private prayer closet but in the hearing of another member of the Body who refused to condemn him — this is what we are for. And when he reached at the end for the image of a small island in the middle of the world, palm trees and ragged cliffs, and said “the only thing I see on that island is our faith,” and that it did not matter whether we were physically there but “whether or not we trust we’re on that island,” I found the name the Spirit was pressing: Patmos. The island of exile that became the island of revelation. My brother had unknowingly prophesied our condition — that the place of pressure and isolation is exactly where the Lord unveils Himself, if only we will trust that we are standing there with Him and not alone. He told me Toby had shared this identical struggle, and I answered that the Spirit moves like a murmuration — thousands of birds turning as one without a single one commanding the rest. No man is refined in isolation. The word I could not speak to myself came through Samuel; the word Samuel needed he had already given to Toby; and all of it turns together in the hand of the Father. And behind every human intercessor stands the unfailing intercession of the Son, whose prayers cannot be denied (Hebrews 7:25). I could pray, I could read Job, I could refuse to hang up — but I could not put God in the room. The presence I could not manufacture arrived where I could not go. ## Implications and Challenges **On confession and our privatized faith.** Much of modern Western Christianity has quietly decided that confession is a matter strictly between the individual and God, treating James 5:16 as optional sentiment rather than a command with power attached. My brother’s testimony exposes the cost: he carried numbness and silence for months, unable even to weep, until he spoke his secret to a brother in the flesh — and “the second I confessed to you, it came.” The healing came in the *speaking to one another,* not in the private wince. The very thing the enemy uses to shame us into silence becomes, when spoken in Christ’s name, the hinge on which deliverance turns. Grace invites us out of solitude and into the risky, healing light of one another. **On the posture of the one who hears.** My deepest conviction from this call is that I did not minister to my brother by standing above him as a man who had conquered sin; I confessed in the same direction and leveled the table. So much of our shepherding fails precisely here — we offer help from a height, and the broken cannot set their burden on a table held above their heads. Philippians 2:7 shows us the Christ who emptied Himself and came down. The correction is sharp but the invitation is tender: come down, or you cannot help them up. And my own severe schooling that night was the proof — the Lord battered a Gnostic pride out of me until I understood I am only a signpost, that there is no part of mine to play, that the gift is a gift and never earned knowledge. **On vindication versus reconciliation.** My brother’s own words are perhaps the sharpest challenge: “I like hearing this a lot better than vindication.” Modern Western Christianity is saturated with a hunger to be proven right — to win the argument, to be justified in our grievance. But God’s better gift was almost never vindication; it was reconciliation, which requires the death of the demand to be proven right (Romans 5:10). We must ask honestly whether we are praying for restoration or for revenge dressed in spiritual language. The Joseph who wept over his brothers had let vindication die long before that day. **On reading the season as punishment.** For months Samuel believed God was punishing him — “I thought for so long You were punishing us, but now I see You have been blessing.” This is a merciful correction to a habit deep in many of us: we read hardship as divine displeasure and comfort as divine approval, when Scripture insists the opposite is often true. Joseph’s pit was the road to the palace, and “whom the Lord loves He chastens” (Hebrews 12:6) as παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) — fatherly training, not penal wrath. The same principle answered my own fear: the plan that “seems unkindly” is the adoption being finished in me. A church that cannot preach the kindness hidden inside hardship will produce disciples who abandon the Father the moment His plan stops feeling pleasant. **On the critique of the gathered church.** Samuel’s words — “today’s church is wrong” — must be received with both honesty and caution. He is right that Christ is found with *a man and his problems* and not in ceremony for ceremony’s sake; the prophets thundered against worship emptied of relationship (Isaiah 1:11–17). Yet the Body itself is not the problem — “not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together” (Hebrews 10:25) remains a command, and this very call was two brothers assembled. The corrective to a cold sixty is not zero; it is two or three gathered in His name. This whole revelation came not in my private prayer closet but through a phone call, the Spirit moving like a murmuration through the Body — koinonia is not a supplement to sanctification; it is often its very instrument. **On the fear of being loved, and sin as evidence.** There is a strain of Christianity that can labor endlessly as a bond-servant, even befriend as a companion, but cannot bear to be a beloved son. I confessed that friendship with Jesus felt natural but sonship felt “weird” — and much modern teaching hands out the language of “God’s child” cheaply, without reckoning that being loved to the depths is more exposing than being commanded (Romans 8:15). At the same time, the enemy’s oldest whisper is that ongoing sin proves God has done nothing, and a great deal of anxious, introspective Christianity plays into that lie. But the grief over sin is the Spirit’s own work in me (Romans 6:14). The measure of sonship is not that the old man is already dead but that he is dying daily under the hand of the Father. **On the empty-field question.** When the world collapses and your brother is begging in an empty field, will you feed him, or will you trust the politics on the news and the world strapped to your chest? The Lord’s reduction of everything to a single rule — treat your neighbor as you want to be treated — cuts against a Christianity that has learned to baptize its fears and its hoarding. “Inasmuch as you did it to one of the least of these My brethren, you did it to Me” (Matthew 25:40). Grace says the table Christ prepared is wide enough for every nation and even our enemies; the challenge is whether we will actually seat them there. Confession levels the table; love widens it. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity is learning to discern the heartbreak hiding inside hostility — in others and in ourselves — and learning to read our story backward from God’s faithfulness rather than forward from our fear. My brother spent months thinking he hated his wife when he was simply a man in unbearable grief with no permission to say so, and spent his season demanding explanations when what he needed was a *sight:* “now my eye sees You.” To grow up in Christ is to move past the servant who fears the master’s power, past even the friend who enjoys the master’s confidence, into the son who can bear the master’s love without breaking the encounter to verify it. It is also the willingness to be small — a signpost that points and does not perform, holding only a piece of the revelation with open hands. The measure of growth is not that I no longer sin but that I now hate the sin the Spirit is putting to death in me, and that I will stop letting the enemy read that grief as abandonment. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience here is concrete and immediate: confess the hidden thing to a trusted member of the Body, and when a brother confesses to you, refuse to stand above him — level the table so the broken can set their burdens down, and widen it until even enemies are seated at it. It is the willingness to lay down vindication and take up the ministry of reconciliation, to remain steadfast as a canopy over an unbelieving spouse without manipulating the outcome, to walk the Hosea road of faithful loving through provocation. It is also the surrender to give my whole heart even when the Father’s plan seems unkindly, trusting that the παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) is the proof of my adoption and not the withdrawal of His favor. And it is the concrete hospitality of the empty-field question — to feed the brother now, in the small daily tests, because what we do to the least of these we do to Christ Himself. Obedience is fidelity in the mundane, where God actually is. **For Prophetic Posture:** To watch and wait prophetically is to believe in the third door before it opens, refusing the enemy’s two-option cage when the God of Joseph weaves a third way on the same loom of our suffering. It is νήφω (NAY-fo) rightly aimed — sober and watchful against the roaring lion, never against the Lamb — and it is the posture of Patmos, believing I am standing on the island of revelation even when it feels like the island of exile. It trains us to pray before we see, laboring in secret intercession at six-fifteen over chains we cannot know will break, and to watch for God’s signatures — a Joseph dream, a shared prayer, a full circle — without forcing them. It teaches us that the highest form of intercession is worship from the pit, blessing His name and His will and His cup while we suffer, for demons have impeccable theology but cannot bless the Father from the pit. And it teaches us to watch how the Spirit moves like a murmuration through the whole Body, carrying the word I cannot speak to myself through a brother, and to remain low enough to receive it from the least among us. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as Your servant and co-heir under the blood of Jesus, still awake in the deep of the night, hoarse from a long call, my own eyes wet with the same tears my brother could not stop, and I bless Your name for what You did before either of us knew to ask. Thank You that Your Son is interceding around the clock for my brother Samuel, praying as He prayed for Peter that his faith would not fail even when Satan demanded to sift him like wheat, and I make agreement with that prayer between the heavens and the earth. Thank You for stepping into the room the moment truth was spoken, for closing the doors that inebriation opened, for breaking the chains the enemy had fastened, and for restoring to him his marriage, his tears, and his very ability to love — the full circle of the very place his doubt began, now flooded with sight. I confess that I am only a signpost, that there is nothing for me to do but point, that the work is finished and the gift is a gift I never earned, and I repent of every way I let it curdle into pride. I lay down the paranoia that has broken the beginning of my encounters, the flinch that looks to verify what should be received in the Spirit, and I ask You to cast out every fear that torments and leave standing only the clean fear that kneels within Your holiness. I thank You that You do not leave me a bond-servant or even only a friend, but by the Spirit of adoption You have made me cry Abba, patiently signing that inheritance into my flesh even through the discipline that seems unkindly. Teach us all to prefer reconciliation over vindication, to be first to confess love as You were first to reach for us while we were still Your enemies, to level every table so the broken can lay their burdens down and widen it until enemies and strangers and the unborn who will one day read this witness are seated at the feast. Set us on the island of only faith and let us find it is the place of revelation, and when the pit is deepest, teach us to worship — for the LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away, and the LORD has given again; blessed, blessed, blessed be Your name. Come, Lord Jesus, and tell us what to say. To You be all glory, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.
