# 07-10 Call Reflection: Sobriety, Providence, and Spiritual Formation **Title:** 07-10 at 18:32 — When Every Door Closes and the Taste Goes Bitter: Providence, Presence, and the Long Walk Toward Home **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to this conversation unfold between myself and my brother Garrison, I found something quietly extraordinary moving beneath the surface of what sounded, at first, like two friends simply catching up. There is a man fighting depression and staying afloat at Teen Challenge, and there is me — welding aluminum in a factory an hour from home, chasing sobriety, reading Jonah again for what feels like the hundredth time, and still finding things I had never seen before. What strikes me most is not any single theological point raised in the call, but the texture of the whole thing: two men being honest with God and with each other, iron sharpening iron in the most ordinary possible way. The Spirit does not always announce Himself with thunder. Sometimes He moves through a phone call on a quiet evening while your wife is out with friends. **Why This Matters:** The Church in this hour is saturated with polished content, produced discipleship pipelines, and curated spiritual aesthetics — yet genuinely honest conversation between brothers who are still bleeding, still stumbling, still searching, and still choosing to press into the Lord anyway is becoming increasingly rare. What happened in this call matters because it is precisely the kind of thing that forms people over time: not a perfectly constructed sermon, not a flawlessly worded prayer, but real men telling the truth about meth and depression and loneliness and Jonah and the four living creatures and whether it is disrespectful to bounce between churches. The Kingdom advances through this. The Body is built through this. And the Spirit is present in this — not in spite of its roughness, but through it. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, this conversation reads as two young men navigating the overlapping difficulties of early adulthood — economic instability, substance recovery, mental health struggles, social isolation, and the search for community and meaning. The natural mind would frame Garrison’s depression and Seth’s sobriety journey as psychological processes: recovery timelines, dopamine dysregulation, the social benefits of accountability relationships. It might commend the friendship warmly, note the therapeutic value of honest conversation, and perhaps observe that religious community functions as a useful support structure for people in vulnerable transitions. The natural mind might even find the theology charming — the Jonah insight about the Ninevites and the fish-god, the four living creatures, the both-and framework — filing them as interesting intellectual discoveries, the kind of pattern-recognition that curious minds enjoy. **The Limitation:** But natural reasoning, however sympathetic, cannot perceive the dimension in which this conversation is actually occurring. It cannot recognize that what happened when every door closed on my meth supply — the failed transactions, the dry season, the five-day airplane sobriety, the three weeks of illness, the month of failed attempts, and then the final moment when the taste itself had changed — was not a fortunate combination of logistics and biology. It cannot see that the Lord was doing something surgical. Natural reasoning can describe the mechanics of addiction recovery; it cannot perceive a God who shuts doors so precisely that a man arrives on the other side of his craving not through willpower, but through a quiet, sovereign exhaustion of every available avenue. Nor can it perceive that two men reading the same ancient book across years and still finding new things in it is not merely evidence of literary richness — it is the living Word doing what the living Word does: revealing. The natural mind categorizes. The Spirit reveals. And there is no optimization, no framework, no self-help scaffold that can substitute for the moment a man sits in silence, says *I am here, Father*, and feels something answer back. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It Listening back over this conversation, the first thing I notice is how sovereignty is threaded through the whole thing without either of us naming it directly. I shared what happened with the meth — and I said it plainly: I got to give it to God on that one. Because He closed every open door. What I was describing, even if I did not reach for the theological vocabulary in the moment, is the doctrine of divine providence operating at the street level. This is not the abstract God of scholastic theology who winds up the universe and watches it run. This is the God of Psalm 107 who breaks the gates of bronze and cuts the bars of iron — the God who can, when it pleases Him, simply make something taste wrong. Who can dry up a supply chain. Who can send a man home sick from San Francisco on an airplane with nothing in his pockets. The means were ordinary. The outcome was miraculous. And I know it, because willpower alone had not worked. This is what the Scriptures mean when they speak of God as the One who works all things according to the counsel of His will (Ephesians 1:11). The Greek word there — βουλή (boulē, boo-LAY), meaning counsel or deliberate purpose — carries the weight of intentional strategy. Not accident. Not fortunate circumstance. Counsel. The Lord had a plan for my sobriety that was operating beneath the surface of every failed transaction, every unanswered message, every dry week. And the beautiful, humbling thing is that I was barely even cooperating. I was trying to find it. I was sending money to people who never showed up. And God was saying: *this is not the path forward. Every door you try, I will close it.* That is not punishment. That is fatherly intervention. That is בּוּשָׁה (bûshāh, boo-SHAW) — shame in the productive sense, the exposure of a dead end — combined with the mercy that refuses to let a son destroy himself. What I hear in Garrison’s story is a different register of the same truth. He is at Teen Challenge, doing the program again, sleeping fourteen hours because depression is heavy, being honest that he has had a few slip-ups but that is between him and God. There is something I deeply respect in that honesty. He is not performing recovery for my approval. He is not catastrophizing. He is also not minimizing. He is a man who is still standing — which, in the economy of grace, is sometimes the whole testimony. Paul wrote in Ephesians 6:13 that having done all, to stand. There are seasons where the standing *is* the doing all. Garrison is standing. He is cooking in a kitchen. He has cheap rent and meals and a community around him. The Lord has him *held*, even in the depression. The Spirit does not always lift the weight immediately. Sometimes He holds you steady underneath it. The conversation about prayer — about silence, about sitting in the Father’s presence and saying simply *I am here* — this is where something in me comes alive. I shared this with Brother Samuel months ago, and I have been practicing it myself: entering a room, sitting down alone, turning off the music and the fan and the noise, and just being present to God without an agenda. What my brother Garrison added — mentioning the writings of Brother Lawrence and *The Spiritual Guide* by Michael de Molinos — confirms something the Spirit has been pressing into me for some time: the deepest prayer is not always the most articulate prayer. Sometimes it is wordless orientation. Sometimes it is simply remaining before the Father without demanding that the silence produce something measurable. David did this in the Psalms constantly — not just petitioning and praising, but *waiting*. Psalm 62:1 says: *Truly my soul silently waits for God.* The Hebrew word there is דּוּמִיָּה (dûmiyyāh, doo-mee-YAH) — a silence that is not empty, but attentive. Expectant stillness. The kind of silence that creates the conditions for the Spirit to speak, to press, to form. And it was exactly this posture — teaching Samuel to sit in dûmiyyāh (doo-mee-YAH) — that opened Samuel up to the gifts of the Spirit. I watched it happen. He called me after his first real attempt at it, and something had broken open in him. The discernment of spirits began to move. This is not coincidence; this is the pattern of Scripture. Elijah heard the still small voice not in the wind, not in the earthquake, not in the fire — but in the aftermath of all of them (1 Kings 19:12). The gifts do not always arrive in the spectacular. They arrive in the cultivated silence that follows the spectacular. Samuel was eighteen years old and God was already moving through him — which tells me that the Lord does not wait for maturity of age. He waits for maturity of posture. The Jonah insight that surfaced mid-conversation arrested me when I first said it, and it arrests me again now. I have read Jonah dozens of times. I went back to it today. And I am still finding new things. What I noticed — that the Ninevites, living roughly five hundred miles from Egypt, would have heard about Pharaoh, about the plagues, about the Red Sea — means that when Jonah arrived and delivered even his half-hearted proclamation, it landed on ears that had already received prior witness of this God’s power and wrath. They were not working from zero. The fear of the LORD had already traveled ahead of Jonah. And then Jonah himself — a man who had been swallowed by a great fish and vomited out alive on a shore — arrives in a city whose own mythology likely included a fish-deity, Dagon or a derivative form, and he is walking proof of something their own religious imagination had already been structured to receive. The timing is staggering. The preparation of the Ninevites for repentance was layered — historical, cultural, theological, and deeply personal for Jonah himself, who had just spent three days in the belly of Sheol praying his own repentance. This is how God works: He prepares the soil before He sends the seed. The conversation about the four living creatures from the book of Revelation — drawing on a sermon I had recently heard — opened up a thread I want to hold carefully. The four faces: lion (authority), ox or calf (service), man (humanity), eagle (divine transcendence). The insight that each one without the others becomes a distortion — authority without the others becomes pride, service without the others invites exploitation, humanity without the others collapses into carnality, divine transcendence without the others becomes disincarnated mysticism — this is a vision of spiritual balance that the book of Revelation embeds into the very fabric of heavenly worship. What I find striking is that these four living creatures in Revelation 4 are described as being full of eyes around and within — the Greek word is ὄμματα (ommata, OM-mah-tah), referring to an all-encompassing awareness or discernment. They see in every direction simultaneously. They are not blind to one dimension of reality because they are overcommitted to another. This is the fullness of the image of God in His people: authority held in service, service held in humanity, humanity held in divine identity. No single domination. Complete integration. What Brother Garrison said about his friend Nick — that being quickly and genuinely forgiven by his best friend was pivotal in his coming to Christ — held more theological weight than the conversational context perhaps communicated. Forgiveness, offered freely and without calculation, is one of the most evangelistically powerful acts a believer can perform. This is because it does something that nothing else can do: it makes the love of God *visible*. When I told Samuel — after he had confessed to lying to me, after he had laid out everything he had done — *you are already forgiven*, I was not performing grace. I was releasing what Christ had already accomplished. And what it did in Samuel was exactly what forgiveness always does when it is real: it collapsed the distance between him and the Father. He could not stay where he was after being forgiven like that. He had to come home. The conversation turning toward the need for a local church — and the Spirit’s consistent pressing on me this week toward finding one — is not disconnected from everything else that was said. The silence, the sobriety, the Jonah meditations, the gifts opening in Samuel, the desire for a mentor who is anointed — all of it is pointing in the same direction. The New Testament knows nothing of a solitary Christianity. The writer of Hebrews warns against forsaking the assembling of ourselves together (Hebrews 10:25), and the Greek word for assembling — ἐπισυναγωγή (episynagōgē, ep-ee-sin-ah-go-GAY) — is not a passive gathering but an active, deliberate coming together, a collecting into one place. I need more than sermons on YouTube. I need a pastor. I need elders. I need to be accountable to something that has my name on it. The Spirit has been pressing this, and I know better than to argue with that pressure. Tomorrow is Sunday. I am going. My own honesty about where I am — knowing Jesus, formed in the Word, carrying real theological insight, but also still figuring out denominations, still reading Psalms for the first time, still holding questions openly — is itself a kind of grace. Brother Garrison said it well: it is good that I don’t think I know it all. And I genuinely do not. What I have is not mastery; it is hunger. And I am learning that hunger, kept honest, is one of the most reliable forms of discernment available. The teachable mind is the mind that keeps finding things in Jonah it had never seen before. The man who is certain he has mined the book clean is the man who has stopped listening. I want to hold what Garrison said about the both-and nature of Christian truth — that the faith is not primarily either-or, but both-and. He was pointing toward something that serious theology has called paradox, and that the Eastern tradition has often held better than Western Christianity: that mystery is not the enemy of doctrine, but its natural atmosphere. I can hold the historical reading of Jonah and the theological reading simultaneously, without one negating the other, because God is both the God of history and the God of meaning — and both registers are true at once. This is not intellectual relativism. This is the wideness of the canon. It is what I called holding paradox in tension in my meditations. Both can be true. Both are true. The ones who are threatened by this are the ones who have mistaken certainty of understanding for faith. Faith is not certainty of understanding. Faith is confidence in the One who understands everything, including the things we don’t. The way I ended the call matters to me in retrospect. I told Garrison I would call, and I called. He noted that keeping your word is a hard way to live. It is. But it is the way. Let your yes be yes and your no be no (Matthew 5:37). There is a whole theology of integrity compressed into that exchange. In a world of perpetual rescheduling and soft commitments and *let’s connect soon*, simply following through — making the time, placing the call, being present for a brother who is sleeping fourteen hours and cooking meals and fighting depression in a halfway house — is an act of love that requires nothing more than doing what you said you would do. And sometimes that is the most Christlike thing available. ## Implications and Challenges There is a particular kind of testimony that modern Western Christianity tends to celebrate: the dramatic before-and-after, the clean timeline, the fully resolved story packaged for a Sunday service. What this conversation challenges is that framework. Neither Garrison nor I have a clean timeline. There are slip-ups. There are fourteen-hour sleep days. There is still-occasional weed and honest wrestling about what the Spirit is pressing on. The challenge here is not to shame that honesty, but to make room for it — to build communities where this kind of conversation is possible, where partial recovery and ongoing dependence on God are not embarrassments to be concealed but the ordinary terrain of discipleship. If the Church cannot hold people who are still in process, it cannot hold people at all. The reliance on YouTube sermons and social media pastors is something I raised myself, and it deserves direct attention. There is genuine value in accessing quality teaching through digital means — I found a sermon on the four living creatures that opened something real in me. But the digital stream cannot replace the embodied community. It cannot provide the pastor who knows my name, the elder who prays with me in person, the accountability of showing up when I said I would. The Spirit’s consistent pressing toward a local church is not legalism or institutional sentiment; it is the recognition that the Body of Christ is meant to be *local and physical* — not just broadcast and consumed. The episynagōgē (ep-ee-sin-ah-go-GAY) of Hebrews 10 requires bodies in a room together. The conversation about denominations — Garrison’s honest acknowledgment that Eastern Orthodoxy often communicates *you are ninety percent right but we have the other ten percent* — is a pattern that has fractured the Body across centuries. My own instinct — that if you confess Jesus Christ as Lord and live it, you are a brother or sister to me regardless of tradition — is not theological naivety. It is the application of the one baptism, one faith, one Lord of Ephesians 4:5. This does not mean doctrine is unimportant. It means that the communion of the saints precedes and supersedes the disputes of the councils. The challenge for me personally is to find a local church not out of brand loyalty or aesthetic preference, but out of discernment, prayer, and genuine submission to spiritual authority. The insight about Brother Lawrence and practicing the presence of God confronts a deeply rooted habit in modern evangelical culture: equating prayer with verbal output. The assumption is that effective prayer must be articulate, lengthy, structured, and demonstrably productive. What both Brother Lawrence and the pattern emerging in my own life quietly subvert is that assumption. The deepest communion with God sometimes requires nothing more than directed, intentional silence — the cultivation of dûmiyyāh (doo-mee-YAH) before the Father. The Church needs to recover this. Not as a replacement for petition or intercession, but as their foundation. You cannot hear the still small voice if you never stop speaking. Finally, the moment I described my radical decision when I came to Christ — cutting out all music, all film, all digital noise, sitting down with the Word alone and deciding to believe everything in it — deserves to be received not as an extreme personality trait but as a template of seriousness. I am not suggesting every believer must do exactly this. But I am observing that the depth of what has been formed in me over these months is directly connected to the depth of that initial consecration. The shallow discipleship epidemic in Western Christianity is partly an epidemic of half-measures at conversion — of people adding Jesus to an existing life rather than surrendering an existing life to Jesus. The willingness to go radical at the beginning is not fanaticism. It is wisdom. It produces exactly the kind of person who can, many months later, read Jonah for the hundredth time and still find something new. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Genuine spiritual maturity does not arrive through content consumption or theological accumulation alone. It arrives through the willingness to sit in silence before the Father, to let the Word speak without agenda, to remain teachable when the same book keeps expanding, and to stay honest about where we actually are rather than performing a version of ourselves that is further along than we have actually gotten. The conversations that form us most deeply are often the most ordinary ones — two brothers on a phone call, being real, holding each other accountable to their word. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this meditation calls forth is threefold: find the local church and submit to it with genuine humility, cultivate the discipline of silence and presence before God as a daily foundation rather than an occasional experiment, and keep your word. Let your yes be yes. These are not spectacular demands. They are the structural bones of a life surrendered to God’s order. The spectacular tends to grow from the structural. The gifts opened in Samuel after he learned to sit in silence. The sobriety came after months of closed doors. Obedience precedes breakthrough, and the obedience is often quieter than we expect. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture that emerges from this conversation is one of humble watchfulness: staying teachable, holding paradox without anxiety, discerning where the Spirit is pressing without demanding that the pressing conform to our preferred timeline or format. I am learning to notice when God is closing doors not as failure but as guidance. I am learning to receive the pressure of the Spirit toward the local church not as obligation but as invitation. The prophetic life is not lived in perpetual supernatural event; it is lived in the accumulated faithfulness of small obediences, sustained presence before God, and the willingness to tell the truth — about where you are, what you have done, and what you need. The testimony is still being written. The journey is still underway. That is not a problem. That is the point. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You with gratitude that has nowhere else to go but up. You closed every door I tried to pry open. You dried up the supply. You made the taste go bitter. You sent me home sick and You held me in the illness, and when I came out the other side, I was more free than I had engineered myself into being through any amount of willpower or strategy. That was You. Only You. I praise You for a brother like Garrison, who is standing in the depression, cooking in the kitchen, staying honest, and still calling it glory to God. I ask You to hold him with particular care — to lift the weight where You are ready to lift it, and to be his strength underneath it where You are not yet. I ask You to direct my feet to the church You have prepared for me, to the pastor whose voice I will recognize as anointed, to the community that can hold what I carry and add to what You have already formed. Teach me the silence. Teach me the dûmiyyāh. Teach me to say *I am here, Father* and mean it without an agenda. Let the Word keep expanding in me — let Jonah keep yielding what I have not yet found, let the Psalms open what has been waiting. I stand as one who belongs to You, hidden in Christ, a co-heir of the Kingdom, one of Your servants — and I make agreement with Your will: close the doors that need closing, open the ones that are Yours to open, and form us — me, Garrison, Samuel, and all the brethren You have knit us to — into the full-orbed image of Your Son: authority, service, humanity, and divine transcendence held together in the balance of Your glory. To You alone be all praise, now and forever.
