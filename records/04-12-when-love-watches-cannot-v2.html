# 04-12 Call Reflection: Intercession, Heavenly Courtroom, and False Identity Garments **Title:** 04-12 at 09:26 — When Love Watches and Cannot Fix: On Intercession, the Courtroom of Heaven, and the Garments We Were Never Meant to Wear **Recorded:** 2026-04-12 09:26:48 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother pour out his heart about his mother — her depression, her anger, her trauma-branded responses, her religion without root — something settled in me that was both grief and recognition. What began as a pastoral concern for one family gradually opened into some of the deepest theological territory I have found myself walking in recent months: the nature of intercession, the courtroom architecture of heaven, the lies we wear as identity, and what it truly means to know God well enough to pray with authority. The tension my brother named is ancient — what does a son do when love is not enough, when theology is not enough, when watching someone you love wear ruin as though it were righteousness becomes unbearable — and I found myself speaking from the same place I always arrive when human love reaches its limit: the borderline where intercession must begin, where pastoral clarity becomes the only honest mercy. **Why This Matters:** This exchange matters not merely because a son is burdened for his mother, but because what we touched in that conversation reaches into two of the deepest pastoral crises facing the Body of Christ today: the difference between a faith that *recognizes* God and a faith that *submits* to Him, and the difference between throwing words toward heaven in urgency and standing as a covenant witness before the Throne with knowledge and authority. The Body of Christ in this generation is saturated with spiritual activity that is largely powerless — not because God has withheld His power, but because we have not learned the mechanics of genuine intercession, have not learned to distinguish the voice of the accuser from the voice of the Father, and have not learned to stand before the court of heaven clothed in a righteousness that is not our own. If we cannot recover these distinctions with grace and without condemnation, we will continue producing generations of people who know the vocabulary of faith while living in the bondage of the past — wearing filthy garments God never gave them, singing hymns that are noise in the ears of Heaven, and dying slowly in depression while the Healer stands at the door with a new robe already in His hands. This is not a fringe case. It is the quiet epidemic of the Western church, and it demands honest, Spirit-led pastoral attention. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, encountering the situation my brother described, reaches immediately for familiar tools: therapy, medication, boundaries, communication strategies, time. It might also reach for social explanations — the psychological weight of unresolved grief, the way early survival instincts calcify into lifelong defensive postures, the nervous packing that mirrors exactly the motions made under a former abuser’s roof, triggered now by circumstances that have nothing to do with that original wound. A trained counselor would recognize these as classic trauma responses, possibly complex PTSD, possibly a nervous system that learned chaos as its native language. The worldly framework, even in its most compassionate and clinically literate form, would also file the second layer of our conversation under the category of two men processing family estrangement, psychological depression, and personal resilience — it would praise resilience as a personality strength, offer narrative therapy, encourage emotional intelligence, and conclude that everything in this exchange resolves into human psychology and interpersonal dynamics. Nothing supernatural, the natural mind would say, needs to enter the equation for healing to be possible. **The Limitation:** But the difficulty is that the natural mind — however sophisticated or well-intentioned — cannot perceive the layer of reality in which these conversations are actually taking place. It cannot see that behind the psychological pattern is a spiritual architecture, that trauma is not merely neurons and memory pathways but a territory that an adversary inhabits when it goes unconsecrated. It cannot see that the self-narrative of smallness my brother has carried is not merely a psychological construct but a garment — something placed on him by a spiritual adversary operating as a prosecuting attorney in a heavenly courtroom. It cannot see that the depression his mother carries is not merely a chemical imbalance to be managed but can be, and in this case appears to be, the fruit of a lie believed at the root: the lie that the past defines the present, that God’s forgiveness does not reach back far enough, that suffering is punishment rather than invitation. And it cannot see that the restraint I exercise before interceding — the long seasons of sitting empty before the Lord, the careful discernment of when and how to pray — is not emotional caution but spiritual precision, the kind that produces intercession that does not return void. Natural wisdom simply has no category for a man who prays knowing it will happen, because such confidence is not arrogance to the spiritual mind — it is the fruit of sustained covenant intimacy, and intimacy is not a category the flesh can generate on its own. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I find myself returning to a courtroom. Not as a cold or mechanical image — as a deeply covenantal one. The book of Job opens in the divine assembly where הַשָּׂטָן (hasatan, hah-shaw-TAHN) — the accuser, the one who stands against — presents himself before God and immediately pivots to legal challenge. The same figure appears in Zechariah 3, standing at the right hand of Joshua the high priest to resist him. And in Revelation 12:10, the great voice declares that the accuser of the brethren has been cast down — the one who accused them before God day and night. This is a legal architecture. הַשָּׂטָן (hah-shaw-TAHN) is not merely a tempter; he is a prosecuting attorney who knows spiritual law and uses it. When I sit with this image, what arrests me is not the accuser’s presence but the Father’s response — because the Father does not argue the charges. He does not explain the filth away. He simply says, *“The LORD rebuke you, Satan”* — and then, without a single moment of Joshua performing his own justification, the command goes forth: *“Take away the filthy garments from him”* (Zechariah 3:4, NKJV). The robe is not earned. It is given. The verdict is not based on the weight of the evidence; it is based on the will and mercy of the One who judges. This is the image burned on my heart when I try to understand what depression rooted in past guilt actually is: it is a person who has agreed with the accuser’s assessment rather than with the LORD’s decree. She is standing in her filthy garments — בְּגָדִים צוֹאִים (begadim tso’im, beh-gah-DEEM tsoh-EEM), literally excrement-soaked robes — refusing the מַחֲלָצוֹת (machalatsot, mah-khah-lah-TSOT), the garments of release and honor, because some part of her believes the accusation is more truthful than the pardon. The accuser’s argument — you are too dirty, too past-stained, too far gone — is answered not by defense but by divine replacement. What she needs is not to be convinced that her past is forgivable. What she needs is a genuine encounter with the God who has already removed the garment and is holding the new one out, waiting. And the tragedy is that no amount of therapy, however good, can dissolve a theological stronghold of this nature. That requires a different kind of knowing — one that comes not from trained observation but from the Spirit who searches all things, even the deep things of God. As I sat with my brother in that conversation, I was not primarily hearing a son’s distress — though that was real and I held it with him. I was hearing a case study in what happens when someone stands at the threshold of the Kingdom and refuses to cross over fully. She *recognizes* God, he said. That word landed heavily on me. Recognition is not submission. Even the demons recognize Jesus — they cry out His name, they acknowledge His authority — and yet that recognition does not save them. What my brother described in his mother is a faith that has stayed permanently in the vestibule — present enough to feel the warmth of the fire but unwilling to step fully into the room. She carries the cross, his words, but does not *recognize* it as the cross. There is no Golgotha without the awareness that this wood is going somewhere, that the weight has a destination, that the suffering is not random. The Apostle Paul wrote in Philippians 3 that he desired to know Christ — to know the power of His resurrection *and* the fellowship of His sufferings. The Greek word for fellowship there is κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-no-NEE-ah) — not passive companionship, but active participation, shared venture, mutual investment. Suffering that is merely endured produces bitterness. Suffering that is consciously offered produces transformation. The difference between the two is whether the person knows *to Whom* they are handing it. The image my brother gave me — his mother nervously packing in the States, the exact same packing motions she had made under his father’s abuse, triggered by a situation that had nothing to do with his father — this is what trauma does. It writes its own scripture over every circumstance. The body remembers what the mind tries to forget, and it reinterprets the present through the grammar of the past. But there is a spiritual dimension to this that the purely psychological account misses: the past, if it has not been brought before the throne of grace and surrendered, retains authority over the present. The stronghold is not merely neurological. It is a palace of imagination — λογισμοί (logismoi, loh-gis-MOY), the Greek word Paul uses in 2 Corinthians 10:5 for the arguments and reasonings that must be pulled down — erected over years of interpreted experience, and reinforced every time the accuser repeats his charges and the person agrees. My brother used a word that struck me: *programmed*. She’s been worldly programmed from her experiences, he said. And he is more theologically precise than he perhaps realized, because the Greek word for conformation in Romans 12:2 is σχηματίζω (schēmatizō, skhay-mah-TEE-zoh) — pressed into the mold of this age — which is exactly what he was describing: a mind so thoroughly shaped by the grammar of loss, abuse, regret, and disappointment that it cannot hold the shape of hope for more than a moment before snapping back. And this is where the courtroom image becomes not merely explanatory but practically transformative. The believer who understands the legal architecture of heaven is not merely petitioning God emotionally; he is stepping into the role of a covenant witness, a priestly representative, bringing evidence before the throne: *Lord, this is what I see. Your word says You are the One who heals. This infirmity is illegal. It does not conform to the statutes of the covenant. Come down and look at it.* This is not manipulation — it is the proper exercise of the royal priesthood, βασίλειον ἱεράτευμα (basileion hierateuma, bah-SIL-ee-on hee-eh-RAH-tyoo-mah), the function God always intended for His image-bearers (1 Peter 2:9). The difference between this kind of intercession and random prayer requests hoping one sticks is precisely the difference between a lawyer who knows the law and a man who walks into a courtroom and shouts his feelings. I am not dismissing emotional prayer — the Psalms are drenched with it. But there is a maturity of intercession that moves beyond petition into declaration, and that declaration is only credible when it rests on actual knowledge of who God is. Not theological knowledge in the academic sense — covenantal familiarity, the kind that comes from sitting empty before Him, again and again, with no audience, no agenda, nothing to perform. I keep returning to what I said about intercession — that I do not intercede simply because someone is hurting and I feel the impulse to pray. The pastoral instinct to immediately lay hands on a wound is not wrong in its love, but it can be deeply wrong in its mechanics. A one-time urgent prayer for a symptom will not locate the root. The Hebrew root פָּגַע (paga, pah-GAH) — to strike upon, to meet, to land on — is the deepest language the canon gives us for intercession. It means the intercessor makes contact with both the need and the Throne simultaneously. This is not casual petition or religious repetition. It is earnest, Spirit-led, sustained, priestly paga — someone making contact on behalf of another until Heaven responds. And that kind of prayer requires something from the one who prays: it requires knowing the One before whom they stand. The pattern in Scripture is consistent. Elijah on Carmel does not summon rain from his own desire; he prays because God has already spoken the word that rain is coming (1 Kings 18:1, 41–45). Daniel does not receive the vision and then pray; he prays first from fasting and mourning, and the angel tells him his words were heard from the first day he set his heart to understand (Daniel 10:12). The intercessor is not the originator; the intercessor is the one who brings the spoken agreement on earth into alignment with what is already written in heaven. There is something in the Jonah account that the Spirit will not let me move past quickly. Jonah went down to Joppa, down into the ship, down into the belly of the vessel, and finally down into a deep sleep — in the middle of a raging storm, surrounded by panicking sailors. That descent is not accidental geography. The Hebrew word יָרַד (yarad, yah-RAHD) — to go down, to descend — marks each stage, and it is the anatomy of spiritual apathy. Each level of downward movement represents a greater insulation from the voice of God, a deeper withdrawal into unconsciousness while the storm of someone’s life rages around them. The person I was reflecting on when I named this image — the mother my brother loves — may be caught in such a descent. Not because she is wicked, but because the cumulative weight of a fractured family and years of unresolved relational pain can press a soul downward through stages of withdrawal that feel, from the inside, like simply coping. Jonah was not malicious in his sleep; he was exhausted and spiritually dislocated. But the storm was real, and the calling had not been revoked. Someone had to come down into the ship and shake him awake. That is the posture of the intercessor who will not abandon the task. The Word from Amos 5 that surfaced in our conversation — *I hate, I despise your feast days…I will not hear the melody of your stringed instruments* — is not primarily a condemnation of religious observance. The Hebrew שָׂנֵא (sane, sah-NEH) — to hate, to set against — expresses divine grief as much as divine wrath. God hates empty worship not because He is harsh but because He is intimate. He did not create us for rituals; He created us for encounter. Every hollow religious motion is a missed meeting, a door left unopened, a table set and abandoned. And the tragedy my brother named — her theology is right, the routines are correct, but the heart is far — is precisely the tragedy Jesus confronted throughout His ministry. The Pharisees had the text memorized. They did not know the Author. The renovation Paul describes in Romans 12:2 — ἀνακαίνωσις (anakainōsis, ah-nah-KAY-noh-sis), a complete renovation, a making new again from the inside — is not incremental self-improvement. It is a supernatural restructuring that requires consistent exposure to the Word, sustained prayer, genuine community, and often what I named at the end of our conversation: deliverance — not as a dramatic event but as a sustained pastoral process. I keep returning to the hard word I quoted — the one from Luke 14:26, the saying about hating one’s family. The word translated *hate* — μισέω (miseō, mih-SEH-oh) — is operating in a Hebraic comparative idiom that is completely lost in flat English translation. In this idiom, *to hate* something means *to love it less by comparison*. The same root, שָׂנֵא (sah-NEH), appears in Genesis 29 where Jacob “hated” Leah — and yet Leah received blessing, covenant, and a father’s provision. The hatred is relational priority, not emotional hostility. Jesus is saying: if your love for family, self, comfort, even your own destiny is competing with your love for Me in the ordering of your life, you have misaligned your loves. And the sobering truth of that for my brother’s situation is this — he cannot rescue his mother. He cannot love her into the Kingdom. His love, as fierce and real as it is, is not the love she needs most. What he is being invited into is not detachment from his mother but a new kind of intercession — one that is not anxious management dressed as love but genuine priestly standing before God on her behalf, believing that God can do what the son cannot. Abraham laying Isaac on the altar did not love Isaac less by placing him there. He loved God more — and in loving God more, he found that Isaac was returned in a new covenant relationship that transcended what their natural father-son bond could have produced. The question of identity — specifically, the false identity narratives we accept from the accuser’s hand — moved through this entire conversation in ways I am still sitting with. My brother touched on his own lifelong narrative of smallness, and I could hear in it the very figure of Zacchaeus, who climbed a tree because he was small and wanted to see Jesus above the crowd (Luke 19:1–10). But what the narrative never finishes telling is what Jesus said when He looked up: *“Come down, for today I must stay at your house.”* The Lord does not reinforce the smallness narrative. He dismantles it by choosing to dwell there. The armor of God includes a breastplate of righteousness — θώραξ (thōrax, THOH-rax) — and that righteousness is not ours by achievement. It is the imputed righteousness of Christ, worn as a garment, protecting the vital center of our identity from the accusations of הַשָּׂטָן (hah-shaw-TAHN). When we strip that garment off and put on the one the enemy offers — stitched from past failures, from words spoken over us, from wounds unhealed — we have dressed ourselves for the court in the wrong clothing. And the singular lie underneath every false identity narrative is the one I named: that the finished work of Christ crucified is not enough. That His blood has covered most things but perhaps not this particular shame, this history, this pattern, this smallness. The Hebrew concept of כָּפַר (kaphar, kaw-FAR) — the covering, the atonement, the wiping clean — is total. The Levitical system of atonement was foreshadowing an event that would not merely temporarily cover sin annually but would, once for all, put it away (Hebrews 9:26). What the Levitical high priest enacted in shadow, Joshua stood receiving in concentrated form in Zechariah 3 — and what Joshua received in anticipation, every believer in Christ has received in full. The filthy garments are removed. They are not washed. They are not repaired. They are *removed* — and new ones are placed on Joshua by the hand of God Himself. This is grace in its most concentrated form. No one is asked to clean themselves before receiving the new robe. The new robe is given *precisely because* the old one is irredeemable. And the final layer I carry from that conversation is this: deliverance is not a one-time event. The language of Luke 11 — when the unclean spirit goes out and then returns with seven others — is a pastoral warning, not a horror story. It is the Spirit telling us that liberation, when it comes, must be filled with something. The swept and ordered house must be *inhabited*. Sustained pastoral care, consistent Word, genuine community, the ongoing practice of surrender — these are not optional afterthoughts. They are the architecture of the house that cannot be re-entered. My brother cannot provide all of this for his mother. But he can pray that God sends it. And he can refuse to carry a weight that belongs on the shoulders of the One who said, *Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest.* There is one more thing I must say about the question of suffering itself, because the theology of it is precisely where I believe the deepest pastoral care must be directed. The joy — and I use that word deliberately, not carelessly — the joy of experiencing depression and infirmity is that these become the precise coordinates where Yahweh Rapha, יְהוָה רָפָא (YHWH Rapha, yeh-ho-VAH rah-FAH), the LORD who heals, makes Himself undeniably known. The joy of walking through lack is that this becomes the territory where Yahweh Jireh, יְהוָה יִרְאֶה (YHWH Yireh, yeh-ho-VAH yir-EH), the LORD who sees and provides, becomes personal rather than theological. Suffering is not punishment for the redeemed. It is an *address* — a specific location where a specific aspect of God’s character can be encountered in a way that no other experience affords. When we refuse to bring our suffering to God, we are not being noble. We are refusing the invitation to a specific encounter He has prepared. The names of God that are revealed *specifically through need* — יְהוָה רָפָא (yeh-ho-VAH rah-FAH), יְהוָה יִרְאֶה (yeh-ho-VAH yir-EH), Yahweh Shalom — remain abstract doctrinal points rather than experiential realities when we relieve the need before bringing it to God, rather than bringing it to God *in* the need. This is not a theological footnote. It is one of the most urgently needed pastoral recoveries of this hour. ## Implications and Challenges There is a version of love in modern Christian culture that looks like spiritual devotion but is actually a form of control dressed in noble clothing. When we position ourselves as the ones responsible for another person’s spiritual outcome — when we stay awake at night crafting strategies for their transformation — we have subtly displaced God from the center of their story and installed ourselves in His place. My brother carries real love for his mother. But love must learn where it ends and intercession begins. The most loving thing he can do is not manage her, not monitor her depression through his own anxious concern, but stand before God on her behalf and then release the outcome. This is the hardest mercy — and it is the mercy that actually has power, because it refuses to substitute human effort for divine encounter. The church has largely lost the category of *sustained* pastoral deliverance. We know the word mostly from dramatic moments — public prayer lines, loud declarations, singular events. But the New Testament pattern is closer to a long obedience, a consistent accompaniment, a repeated exposure to truth until the stronghold weakens and falls. We have created a culture of spiritual microwave healing when what many people need is the slow fire of faithful pastoral presence over months and years. The language of Luke 11 is not a horror story — it is a pastoral blueprint. The swept and ordered house must be inhabited before the task is complete. If we will not recover this patience, we will continue to see people touched but not transformed, delivered but not sustained, freed but not filled. The distinction between recognizing God and submitting to God deserves far more pastoral attention than it typically receives. In many congregations, recognition is treated as sufficient — if someone prays a prayer, attends services, uses Christian vocabulary, they are counted among the flock. But Scripture draws a much sharper line. James 2:19 makes the uncomfortable observation that the demons also believe — and shudder. Belief that does not produce submission has not yet become saving faith in its fullness. This does not mean we reject or abandon those in this middle space. It means we become more honest about what they need — not more church programming, but an encounter with the living God that disrupts the comfortable arrangement they have made with religion. Naming this distinction is not condemnation. It is the truest pastoral kindness. The modern Western church has developed an allergy to stillness. In an age of constant media saturation, notification culture, and performance-driven spirituality, the discipline of sitting before God with nothing to offer and nothing to request is countercultural to the point of seeming unproductive. But it is precisely in those accumulated hours of consecrated emptiness that the power of genuine intercession is built — not because we earn authority through duration, but because intimacy with the Lord transforms the one who prays, aligns the will with His, and produces the kind of knowledge of His character from which bold, accurate declaration becomes possible. The problem is not that people pray too little; it is that people do not sit empty long enough to know what to pray. A prayer born from urgency and a declaration born from knowledge are not the same instrument, and the harvest field requires the latter. The trauma patterns my brother observed in his mother point to something the church must honestly reckon with: unhealed trauma does not respect theology. A person can hold correct doctrinal positions and still be governed by their nervous system’s encoded memories. This does not mean doctrine is irrelevant — it is the very medicine needed. But it must be administered with pastoral wisdom that understands how deeply the past can structure the present. Preaching louder at someone governed by trauma is not effective ministry. Walking with them patiently, praying consistently, creating the conditions for safe encounter with the Word in a trusted community — this is the slower and truer work. And it requires that the one doing the walking has first learned to sit in silence themselves, to hear the Father’s voice over the accuser’s, and to carry the weight of another’s story without becoming its owner. Finally, the confusion about what intercession actually is — what standing before the court of heaven on behalf of another actually requires — represents one of the most persistent gaps in contemporary prayer culture. The saints must learn the difference between petition and declaration, between emotional urgency and covenantal knowledge. Not because emotional prayer is worthless — it is not — but because there is a maturity of intercession that the Spirit is calling forth in this hour that operates from intimacy rather than from need, from knowledge rather than from anxiety, and from the posture of a priestly witness who knows the law of the Kingdom and brings it to bear on the conditions of the earth. This kind of intercession is not performed; it is inhabited. And inhabiting it requires exactly the disciplines of stillness, Word-saturation, and sustained surrender that the modern church is most reluctant to embrace. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity does not mean we feel nothing when those we love are suffering. It means we learn to feel it and then bring it rightly — to the Throne rather than to the strategy board. Mature intercession holds the burden without becoming the burden’s owner. It weeps and then yields. It loves fiercely and then releases. Growing into this posture requires us to honestly examine whether our concern for others is birthed from love or from an unacknowledged need to be needed — and to let the Spirit purify our motives without condemnation. It also requires that we know our covenant position: that we are not autonomous agents petitioning a distant God, but royal priests in a heavenly legal framework, called to bring the reality of God’s character into contact with the conditions of the earth through agreement, declaration, and priestly witness rooted in the finished work of Christ. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The call emerging from this conversation is specific and urgent: commit to sustained intercession for those caught in the intersection of depression and religious performance. Do not be satisfied with quick prayers and general hope. Learn to stand — to פָּגַע (pah-GAH) — on behalf of those who cannot yet stand for themselves. Return to the Word not as literature but as a living mirror, reading to find what God says about His people, His healing, His covenant — and letting that Word cut. Refuse to wear the narratives the enemy offers, naming them for what they are — illegal accusations before the court of heaven — and present the blood of the covenant as the answer to every charge. And for those in pastoral responsibility, begin to build the patient infrastructure of sustained accompaniment that deliverance ministry actually requires. One prayer service is a beginning. The long walk is the work. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation calls forth is the posture of one willing to sit in the cave, willing to wait for the קוֹל דְּמָמָה דַקָּה (qol demamah daqqah, kol deh-mah-MAH dah-KAH) — the still small voice — rather than chasing the earthquake. The prophetic capacity is not primarily about loudness or dramatic declaration; it is about intimacy, about having heard, about speaking only what the Father gives rather than what the ego or the moment demands. Those being trained as prophetic voices in this hour must know the architecture of heaven’s courtroom before they speak into the conditions of earth. They must learn the restraint to say nothing until they have something to say — and the boldness, when they do speak, to speak it without qualification. They must remember, always, that they are the messenger and not the message, the instrument and not the source, and that He who glories must glory in the Lord alone. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as a servant, a co-heir, and a priestly witness — not by my own righteousness but by the finished work of the One who became for me wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, and redemption. I stand in agreement with what You have already written in the scrolls of heaven concerning my brother who carries his mother’s depression, and concerning every soul represented in the anguish that surfaced in our conversation on this day. I thank You that You are יְהוָה רָפָא (yeh-ho-VAH rah-FAH), that You have not been surprised by any of this, that the filthy garments are already designated for removal and the rich robe is already prepared — that the accuser’s report, however accurate by the ledger of the law, has been answered definitively by the blood that speaks better things. I bring before Your court the garments the enemy has placed on Your children — the smallness, the shame, the guilt, the self-written sentences of unworthiness, the trauma-language that has reinterpreted every present moment through the grammar of the past — and I renounce them by the authority of that blood. I ask You to reach into the places where the past has built its palace of λογισμοί (loh-gis-MOY), and to pull down every argument that has made itself higher than the knowledge of You. Make the encounter with You so undeniably real that there is no longer any room for the accuser’s report. Send the sustained pastoral care, the consistent Word, the genuine community that those in the swept and ordered house require to remain inhabited and free. I intercede now for every person living in recognition without submission, in ritual without encounter, in suffering without an address for it — Lord, let this be a turning point. Let the intercession that has been offered in broken, honest words this day rise before You as incense, and let Your answer come not merely as comfort but as the kind of encounter that removes garments and replaces them entirely, that stops the יָרַד (yah-RAHD) and lifts a soul to the mountain where the still small voice is heard. We stand in the authority of Your Son, under the covering of His blood, as Your servants who know that You do nothing without first revealing Your secrets to those who watch and pray. Have Your way, Lord. We trust You with what we cannot fix.
