# 07-05 Call Reflection: Spiritual Transformation, Unlikely Vessels, and the Turning of the Soul **Title:** 07-05 at 17:51 — The Drunkard’s Word and the Turning of the Soul **Recorded:** 2026-07-05 17:51:33 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation What I am holding in my hands here is not a systematic teaching or a polished sermon outline — it is something far more alive than that. As I listened to my brother Samuel on this call, and then heard the meditation that came through his own spoken word afterward, something in me recognized the unmistakable fingerprint of the Spirit moving through the raw material of grief, honesty, and unlikely encounter. The image that will not release me is this: a drunkard in a bar becoming the instrument of divine liberation for a man sitting next to him — and that man, my brother, having the presence of mind to receive it. I am sitting with the weight of what it means that God did not wait for a more appropriate venue. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ has developed a dangerous allergy to unpolished testimony. We have trained ourselves to receive God’s voice through certain channels — the pulpit, the prayer room, the altar — and we have, perhaps without realizing it, quietly disqualified the bar stool, the parking lot, the grocery run, the late-night phone call between brothers still figuring out what they are carrying. What my brother’s spoken meditation confronts is not merely a personal story of heartbreak and healing; it is a prophetic challenge to the entire framework by which we decide where God is allowed to show up and through whom He is permitted to speak. If the saints cannot recognize the word of the Lord coming through a man who has not finished his Guinness, we are more Pharisee than we know. This matters for discipleship because the capacity to receive from unexpected vessels is itself a mark of spiritual maturity, and the inability to do so is a mark of a pride so subtle it rarely introduces itself by name. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, encountering my brother’s spoken meditation, would do what it always does with grief that has found poetic form: it would celebrate the emotional intelligence on display, affirm the speaker’s resilience, and frame the entire arc as a healthy psychological process of detachment and recovery. It would note the vivid imagery with approval — the bar, the Guinness, the roses on the feet, the walk toward light — and perhaps even commend the spiritual language as a productive meaning-making strategy, the kind that therapists sometimes encourage when helping clients reframe loss into growth. The secular reading is not malicious. It is simply operating from within its own ceiling, and that ceiling is the conviction that the deepest things happening in this meditation are ultimately personal rather than cosmic, therapeutic rather than theological, resolved by self-awareness rather than by grace. **The Limitation:** What the natural mind cannot perceive is that the grief Brother Samuel was processing had long since outgrown its romantic category. There is a species of heartbreak that does not merely injure a man — it unmasks him, exposes the architecture of misplaced devotion, reveals precisely where he had been asking a human being to carry the weight that only God was built to bear. The flesh has no grid for recognizing that the moment a man stops needing to finish his drink is a moment of genuine interior revolution — a reorientation of the soul’s entire gravitational field. And the flesh certainly cannot credit a stumbling, unfinished man at a bar with carrying a prophetic word sharp enough to sever a spiritual captivity. That kind of perception requires the very Spirit who searches all things, even the deep things of God, and who has never once been confused about which vessel He chose to pick up next. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I listened to my brother describe what he was writing — and then heard that writing played back through his own voice — I found myself drawn immediately into one of the most ancient patterns woven through the canon: the wilderness that precedes the inheritance. The ache he named, the wandering ashore, the begging and praying in the hour of longing — this is not merely the vocabulary of romantic loss. It is the vocabulary of a soul that had placed the full weight of its deepest longing upon something that was never architected to hold it. Augustine, sitting in the ruins of his own misdirected loves, arrived at the same discovery from a different direction: *our heart is restless until it rests in Thee.* Brother Samuel is learning this not from a theology lecture but from the particular and costly curriculum of heartbreak, which is one of the most effective classrooms God uses precisely because it cannot be argued with. The image that arrested me most completely was this: *I found God sitting at the bar drinking on a Guinness.* I want to resist the impulse to over-theologize this too quickly, because what my brother is naming is experiential and deserves to be honored as such before it is explained. But the Scripture will not be quiet here. Jesus was called φίλος (philos, FEE-los) — friend, companion, one who draws near by choice — to tax collectors and sinners. He did not announce office hours in the synagogue and wait for the broken to schedule an appointment. He went to where they were eating and drinking and grieving. The terrain of divine accessibility in the Gospels is consistently the place of disgrace, the low place, the place respectable religion had learned to avoid. The bar in my brother’s account is not atmospheric detail. It is theological geography. And then the instrument God chose: a drunkard. A man who had not himself finished what was in his hand. I am struck by this nearly without words, because the Scripture is saturated with this exact pattern — God using the very embodiment of what we think disqualifies a person to carry the word that sets another free. Balaam’s donkey saw the angel of the LORD before the prophet did. The widow of Zarephath was a Gentile with nothing left, and she became the vessel of supernatural provision. The Samaritan woman at the well carried five failed covenants into a conversation with the Messiah and walked away as the first evangelist in the Gospel of John. God has never once been limited by our categories of spiritual adequacy, and the drunkard’s unrequested counsel — *I don’t need to finish my drink* — is one of the most compressed pictures of genuine liberation I have encountered in spoken testimony in a long time. There is a Hebrew reality pressing against me here. The idea of חֵרֶם (cherem, KHEH-rem) — something devoted, set apart, whether by consecration or by destruction — carries within it the understanding that devotion is not neutral. It goes somewhere. When a soul has organized itself around a human attachment with the intensity my brother described, and that attachment is severed, the devotion does not simply evaporate. It spills. It becomes corrosive rather than consecrating because it was poured into a vessel designed for a different kind of love. The curse he named — *you left a curse* — is not poetic excess. It is an accurate description of what happens when devotion that belongs to God is spent on what cannot receive it rightly. The healing does not come from pretending the love was wrong to exist. It comes from the painful, grace-soaked work of redirecting that devotion back toward its designed object. What gripped me next in the meditation was the phrase: *I needed to live in truth to escape you, to find the light that shines in the skies.* This is not the language of avoidance or emotional self-protection. This is the language of exodus. The Israelites did not leave Egypt because Egypt had nothing to offer — Egypt had food and familiarity and the terrible comfort of a known suffering. They left because the life available to them there was fundamentally incompatible with the identity God had called them into. My brother’s departure from this relationship is being framed, whether he has fully named it in those terms yet or not, as a theological event. He is not simply moving on from someone. He is stepping into a self that can only be inhabited in the light, and the light requires him to leave the familiar darkness behind, however much that darkness once felt like home. The confession that follows is where the meditation becomes most bracingly honest, and therefore most beautiful: *I am a sinner, a cold-hearted killer, and I have made the mistake that every man like me can make.* I do not read this as self-condemnation without horizon. I read it as ἐξομολόγησις (exomologēsis, ex-oh-mo-lo-GAY-sis) — open, unhidden acknowledgment of one’s actual condition before God and others. Scripture does not call us to feel badly about sin as a spiritual exercise in self-punishment. It calls us to agree with God about it, to name it with the same unflinching clarity that God names it, and to do so as the first act of a man who has finally stopped negotiating with himself. There is a particular courage in saying *I have made the mistake that every man like me can make* — because it refuses both the pride of thinking oneself uniquely terrible and the denial of thinking oneself not terrible at all. It is the posture of a man who knows himself and has decided to stop hiding that knowledge. The image of walking away *with no roses on my feet* is the kind of phrase that carries more than it announces. My brother does not want to leave this chapter decorated, adorned, looking like a man who won something. He wants to leave clean. There is something deeply aligned with the Kingdom in that desire. The prodigal son, rehearsing his speech on the road home, was not imagining the reception party. He was imagining the servant’s quarters. He wanted nothing he had not been given freely. And the father, who saw him from a long distance and ran — κατεφίλησεν (katephilēsen, ka-teh-FEE-lay-sen), kissed him again and again — was not running because the son had earned the reception. He was running because restoration is simply what the father does when the lost son turns toward home. The farewell my brother offered — *to carry true, to know that I love you and I’ll see you later* — is not romantic capitulation. It is something more theologically complex and more mature than that: the acknowledgment that love, even love that has wounded and been wounded, is not simply cancelled by the end of a season. It is redirected. Transformed. The devotion that was being spent on what could not fulfill it is not destroyed by this grief — it is, through the very fire of this grief, being refined and returned toward its rightful source. Paul writes in First Corinthians thirteen that love οὐ πίπτει (ou piptei, oo PIP-tay) — does not fall, does not collapse, does not cease to have ontological weight even when its expression must change. My brother is not becoming loveless. He is learning to love in the order God designed love to flow, from God outward, through the human heart, and back again. What I see standing at the end of this whole meditation is not a man who has arrived. It is a man who has turned. And in the Scripture, the turn is everything. The Hebrew שׁוּב (shuv, SHOOV) — to turn, to return, to face a different direction — is the simplest and most radical act available to a human soul. Repentance is not primarily an emotional event. It is a directional one. My brother faced his grief honestly, sat still in a bar long enough to hear a word from an unlikely voice, put down what he did not need to finish, and turned toward the light. That is the whole shape of a gospel moment compressed into raw spoken testimony. I am honored to have been on the phone when this was still being born. And I notice something in the texture of this call that I do not want to pass over without naming: the ease between brothers, the laughter about wrong groceries and wrong plantains, the mention of a beautiful steak and a good piece of writing and a fireworks video shot in 8K because why not — all of it surrounding the moment when my brother said *this is going into a meditation, bro* and both of us felt something shift. The Kingdom does not only move in the solemn and the structured. It moves in the middle of ordinary Tuesday evenings when brothers are unloading groceries and talking about nothing in particular, and then suddenly the Spirit is right there in the conversation, and everything that was scattered begins to gather into something. That is precisely the kind of kairos moment I am trained to notice, and I am grateful I did not miss it. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation presses against me is the tendency within modern Christianity to require suffering to present itself with acceptable packaging before we take it seriously. My brother’s testimony came through the medium of a bar, a drunkard, unfinished grief, and a late-night phone call while someone was still bringing groceries in from the car. If we cannot receive the word of the Lord through that delivery system, we should ask ourselves honestly when it was that we decided God needed a more polished messenger — and where in the Scripture we found that requirement. The second challenge is subtler: the expectation that spiritual transformation should be immediately complete and emotionally resolved before it is shared. Brother Samuel’s spoken meditation is honest about ongoing grief, ongoing wrestling, ongoing confession. It does not pretend to be a closed chapter. And yet it is already rich with revelation and worth. We have trained the saints to wait until they are fully healed before they testify, and in doing so we have silenced the most powerful testimonies of all — the ones still bleeding, the ones still turning, the ones still learning how to put the drink down. Third, the confession embedded in my brother’s meditation — *I am a sinner, a cold-hearted killer* — deserves to be honored rather than quickly papered over with reassurance. There is a pastoral habit in Western Christianity of rushing toward comfort the moment someone names their darkness honestly, as though the naming itself is the problem to be solved. But genuine ἐξομολόγησις (ex-oh-mo-lo-GAY-sis) is not a crisis to be managed. It is a sacred act of agreement with God, and it requires room to breathe. When we rush past honest confession toward formulaic comfort, we short-circuit the very process by which the soul is being made new. Fourth, the misplacement of devotion my brother named — giving the full weight of one’s longing to a human relationship — is far more common in the Body than we publicly acknowledge, and it is rarely addressed with the theological precision it requires. We speak about codependency in psychological terms and we speak about idolatry in abstract terms, but we rarely connect them as the same underlying phenomenon: the חֵרֶם (KHEH-rem) of devotion directed toward a vessel not designed to hold it. This meditation calls the saints to honest examination of where their deepest love is actually aimed, not where they believe it to be aimed, but where the evidence of their grief and their desperation reveals it has been living. Fifth, the pattern of God speaking through disqualified vessels — the drunkard, the donkey, the despised Samaritan, the leper, the child — is not a minor biblical theme. It is a major one. And yet we continue to build ecclesiastical systems that tightly control who is authorized to carry a word. The call to discernment is real and necessary. But discernment is different from gatekeeping, and a community that has never received an unexpected word from an unexpected source is probably not a community practicing genuine discernment — it is a community practicing comfort disguised as caution. Finally, and I say this with deep affection: the willingness to feed live, unpolished spoken material into a Spirit-led process of theological unfolding — as my brother and I were both doing on this call — is itself a posture of discipleship. It is the decision to believe that what is raw and scattered in the natural can be Spirit-ordered and multiplied for those who will come after. That is not a small thing. That is faith in the redemptive capacity of the Spirit to work with what we actually offer rather than waiting for us to offer something more impressive. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This meditation calls us toward a deeper honesty about where we have been placing the weight of our deepest longing, and toward the courage to name it without euphemism. Spiritual maturity is not the absence of grief or the absence of misplaced devotion. It is the willingness to let both be seen — by God, by trusted brothers, and by ourselves — and to believe that the seeing itself is the beginning of the turning. The saints grow when they stop protecting their own reputation for being spiritually together, and start practicing the kind of transparency that makes room for the Spirit to actually move. **For Kingdom Obedience:** What this calls forth practically is a renewed willingness to receive the word of the Lord from unlikely sources — to stay present in the unpolished conversation, the unexpected encounter, the brother who calls while you are still unloading groceries — because the Kingdom moves in exactly those moments as readily as it moves in any sanctuary. It also calls for a renewed honesty in our confessional life: not performative self-deprecation, but genuine agreement with God about what is actually true, and a trust that such agreement is not the end of the story but the beginning of restoration. **For Prophetic Posture:** The saint who abides faithfully is the saint who learns to watch for God in the low places — not because God is exclusively there, but because our tendency is to look for Him exclusively in the high places, and the training of prophetic perception requires us to expand our field of vision. To remain faithful in the wilderness season, to put the drink down when the word comes, to turn even while the grief is still present — this is the prophetic posture this meditation calls us into. Not triumphalism. Not premature resolution. But honest, incremental, courageous turning toward the light. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come to You as one who has been given ears to hear, and who is learning — slowly, imperfectly, gratefully — that You have never once been limited by the vessels You choose to use or the settings in which You choose to speak. I thank You for my brother, for his honesty, for the spoken word he offered into the night, and for the Spirit who was already working in it before either of us had named what we were hearing. I ask that what was born in that conversation would reach every soul that needs to know that the bar stool is not outside Your jurisdiction, that the unfinished drink can be set down, that the turn is always available, and that You run toward the one who is still rehearsing the servant’s speech. Let this word do what You designed it to do — not to impress, not to accumulate an audience, but to set something free in the one who receives it and finds their own grief echoed and their own soul called back toward home. I agree with Your will, which is that none be lost who belong to You. I stand between the heavens and the earth as a servant of the Word, a keeper of what has been spoken in the open air of an ordinary evening, and I declare that nothing spoken in Your Spirit returns void. To the Father, through the Son, by the Spirit — all glory.
