# 03-13 Meditation: Abiding vs. Striving — Spiritual Formation in the Wilderness Season # Meditation: The Oil Is Already Being Poured — Abiding, Not Striving, in the Wilderness of Faithful Work ## Opening Observation I find myself sitting with something that crept up on me quietly — the realization that I have been *abiding* this whole time, even when I was convinced I was striving. Something has been pressing on me from the breadcrumb trail the Holy Spirit has been quietly laying down, one conversation, one podcast, one Bible study at a time — and only now, looking backward, can I see the path He was building all along. I can’t get this off my mind: the oil has been filling, even when I couldn’t see the lamp. **Why This Matters:** In a discipleship culture saturated with productivity metrics, platform-building strategies, and ministry hustle, the revelation that *genuine Kingdom work often looks like rest from the outside* is nothing short of urgent. This is not a theological abstraction — it is the lived anatomy of what it means to be a vessel being prepared by the Spirit rather than a performer being polished by ambition. If I misread abiding as laziness, and striving as faithfulness, I risk abandoning the very posture that God is calling me deeper into — and that misreading is epidemic in the Body of Christ right now. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely human perspective, what I am describing would be evaluated through the lens of output, consistency, and measurable impact. The natural mind — shaped by productivity culture, social media engagement metrics, and even the well-meaning pressure of institutional ministry models — would look at a week of silence, no posts, no outreach blitz, no podcast episode, and flag it as stagnation. Psychology might label it avoidance. A life coach might call it a lack of discipline or commitment. Secular frameworks around “building an audience” would diagnose the inconsistency as a structural flaw that needs correcting with a content calendar and a growth strategy. Even within Christian circles shaped more by business principles than by Spirit-led formation, the absence of visible output is often misread as unfaithfulness. **The Limitation:** The natural man, as Paul writes in 1 Corinthians 2:14, “does not receive the things of the Spirit of God, for they are foolishness to him; nor can he know them, because they are spiritually discerned” (NKJV). The world’s framework simply cannot perceive what is happening in the hidden place — the *kat’ idian*, the sacred alone-time where the oil of the Spirit is poured not for spectators but for the vessel itself. What looks like inactivity to the natural eye is, in the spiritual economy of God, the most essential work of all: the soul learning to receive before it is sent to pour. ## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation As I sit with this, I am struck by the image of the wise virgins in Matthew 25. Jesus tells us of ten virgins who went out to meet the bridegroom — all of them. All ten had lamps. All ten were *called* to this meeting. But only five had oil. Five had spent time in the hidden discipline of *filling*. And what arrests me here is not the drama of the door being shut, but the quiet, almost invisible economy that preceded it. Nobody at the wedding party saw those five women purchasing their oil. Nobody marked it on a calendar. There was no announcement, no audience, no applause. There was only the private act of preparation — and in the midnight hour, it was the only thing that mattered. The Greek word for oil here, ἔλαιον (*elaion*), carries the weight of anointing throughout both Testaments. It is the same substance poured over priests and kings, the same element that kept the Menorah burning through the night in the Holy Place. The oil is not decoration. It is the very condition of *light*. I am seeing that what I once condemned in myself — the podcast episodes I was making not for an audience, but because something in me was being *called* to the work — was actually the Holy Spirit superintending my oil-filling season. I thought I was striving because the work felt solitary, sometimes unwitnessed, sometimes producing little visible fruit. But the Spirit is revealing to me now that striving and faithful work are not the same thing. Striving is working *ahead of God* — manufacturing outcomes He has not yet authorized. Faithful work is responding to an inner compulsion that comes not from ego or ambition but from the Spirit of God who *knows what the vessel will need* before the vessel does. When Elijah was fed by the ravens at the brook Cherith in 1 Kings 17, he wasn’t building a platform. He was being *sustained for a moment he could not yet see*. The hiding was not failure. The hiding was preparation. What is shattering to me in this moment is how clearly I can see, only in retrospect, what the Spirit was constructing. The Bible studies, the one-on-one conversations with the Holy Spirit as the only Teacher — because John writes in 1 John 2:27, “the anointing which you have received from Him abides in you, and you do not need that anyone teach you; but as the same anointing teaches you concerning all things” (NKJV) — the Hebrew word for this anointing, מִשְׁחָה (*mishchah*), is rooted in the same verb used for consecrating a priest. The Anointing doesn’t produce preachers first. It consecrates *priests* first. The priestly work — the inner altar, the daily offering, the tending of the fire — always precedes the prophetic proclamation. I was being made a priest before I was being sent as a voice. I notice something else moving through this. The Psalms keep surfacing in this reflection, and not incidentally. The Psalms are Israel’s *contemplative core* — the interior language of a people who had to hold together the tension of divine faithfulness and human suffering, of waiting and warfare, of silence and song. They are, in the deepest sense, the Spirit’s school of abiding. David did not write from pulpits. He wrote from caves, from exile, from the solitary place where only God was the audience. And it is *those* words — born in hiddenness — that became the liturgy of a people, the songs that prepared the faithful for the Day of the Lord. The Hebrew word דָּוַד (*dawid*) — beloved — was not a title David claimed. It was a name the LORD assigned him in the wilderness of formation. The beloved is always formed in obscurity before being unveiled in purpose. There is a living tapestry here woven through the image of the wilderness itself. Throughout Scripture, the wilderness — מִדְבָּר (*midbar*) in Hebrew — is not simply a geographic location. It is a *spiritual geography*: the liminal space between Egypt and Canaan, between who you were and who God is making you. Its root, דָּבַר (*dabar*), means *to speak*. The wilderness is, at its etymological heart, the place where God speaks. Moses encountered the burning bush in the wilderness. Elijah heard the still small voice in the wilderness. John the Baptist cried out from the wilderness. And Jesus, filled with the Spirit at baptism, was immediately driven by that same Spirit into the wilderness for forty days before His public ministry began. The pattern is unmistakable: the Spirit fills, then the Spirit leads into the hidden place, then and only then comes the commissioning. I have been in my wilderness. And I am beginning to understand that this is not a detour. This *is* the path. What the Spirit is now surfacing is the question of calling — am I an evangelist, a teacher, a contemplative, a voice? I find that I genuinely do not know, and the Spirit seems almost pleased by that unknowing. Because the truth is that the categories themselves may be less important than the posture. Paul writes in Romans 8:14, “For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are sons of God” (NKJV). The Greek word translated *led* is ἄγονται (*agontai*) — a present passive form, meaning *continuously being led*, not a one-time commissioning event. Sonship in the Kingdom is not established by a title. It is demonstrated in the ongoing, moment-by-moment responsiveness to the Spirit’s movement. I am a son being led. The title will emerge from the leading — not the other way around. What is wild to me is that even in the factory — even in the context of ordinary daily work, surrounded by people who know nothing of what God is building in me — I am walking in a form of hiddenness that is itself a spiritual discipline. Not everyone I encounter needs to know. Not every space is the right field. Ecclesiastes 3:7 speaks of “a time to keep silence, and a time to speak” (NKJV), and the discernment between those two is itself a gift of the Spirit. Jesus consistently withdrew from crowds, refused premature public declarations of His identity — what scholars call the *Messianic Secret* in Mark’s Gospel — and chose the hidden conversation over the broadcast moment. I am learning that the Kingdom does not always announce itself. Sometimes it simply *is*, quietly and powerfully present, waiting for the appointed moment of disclosure. And this brings me to the most confronting realization of this entire meditation: I am not behind. The Spirit does not run on the world’s timetable, and the pressure I feel to produce more, post more, reach more — that pressure is not from the Holy Spirit. It is from the accuser, who wants me to trade the depth of oil-filling for the illusion of activity. The lamp that burns brightest in the midnight hour is not the one that was shown off the most during the day. It is the one that was quietly, faithfully, even secretly *filled*. This is the economy of the Kingdom — and it is radically upside-down from everything the world, and frankly much of modern church culture, would have me believe. ## Implications and Challenges Grace is the beginning and ending of everything I want to say here — because what follows is not a critique born in judgment but an invitation born in love, the same love with which Priscilla and Aquila, recognizing Apollos’s genuine fire, *“took him aside and explained to him the way of God more accurately”* (Acts 18:26, NKJV). It is from that tender posture that I want to say what the Spirit seems to be saying through this reflection. First, the modern Western church has, in many spaces, wed itself to a theology of *measurable ministry output* that has very little biblical support and significant potential for spiritual harm. The idea that faithfulness must be visible — tracked in attendance numbers, podcast downloads, social media reach, or weekly content consistency — is not found in the 66 books of the canon. It is, however, deeply embedded in the post-Industrial Revolution cultural values that shaped much of evangelical church structure. The Spirit here gently but clearly calls us back to the biblical pattern: hiddenness before harvest, the wilderness before the word, the filling before the sending. Second, the assumption that *theological training from human institutions* is the primary pathway to spiritual authority and effective ministry deserves honest re-examination in light of Scripture. Jesus chose fishermen, tax collectors, and social outsiders. Paul’s gospel, he insists, was “not received from man, nor was I taught it, but it came through the revelation of Jesus Christ” (Galatians 1:12, NKJV). John explicitly teaches that the anointing *itself* teaches. This is not a rejection of learning — it is a reordering of the source. The Spirit is the Teacher. Human teachers, at their best, are pointing us back to Him. The contemplative, the one formed in the wilderness of listening, may carry just as genuine — and sometimes more — spiritual authority than the institutionally credentialed, depending entirely on what source they are drawing from. Third, the conflation of *striving* and *faithfulness* is one of the most subtle and damaging theological errors circulating in modern discipleship culture. Many believers are quietly burning out under the weight of a gospel that has been unconsciously retrofitted with a performance ethic. The Sabbath — שַׁבָּת (*Shabbat*) — was not optional in Israel’s covenant life. It was *commanded*. Rest was not the reward for completed work; rest was *built into the rhythm of faithful work itself*. A week of silence, of trusting, of not posting or preaching or producing, is not unfaithfulness — it may, in fact, be the most obedient thing a Spirit-led vessel can do in that season. Fourth, the tendency to evaluate calling through the lens of recognized ministry categories — evangelist, preacher, teacher, prophet — can actually obscure the simpler, more foundational truth that Jesus offers: “Follow Me” (Matthew 4:19, NKJV). Discipleship is first relational and responsive, not positional or categorical. The Spirit leads sons and daughters into their specific expression of Kingdom contribution *organically*, through the path of obedient following — not through a vocational assessment or a church membership role. The invitation here is to release the pressure to *name* the calling before the calling has fully *formed*, and to trust that the One who began the good work will also complete it (Philippians 1:6, NKJV). I offer all of this with open hands and a gentle heart — not as one who has arrived but as one who is, like so many of you, still learning to hold the lamp and trust that the oil is enough. ## What This Means For Us **For Your Spiritual Maturity:** The invitation here is to *reinterpret the quiet seasons*. The weeks that produced nothing visible, the reflections written only for your own soul, the Bible study attended by few or no one — these are not evidence of failure. They are evidence of formation. Spiritual maturity is not measured in output but in the depth of the inner life being cultivated in secret. Begin to read your own journey through the lens of the wise virgins — and ask not “what have I produced?” but “how full is my lamp?” **For Kingdom Obedience:** The specific posture this revelation calls us toward is *faithful presence over anxious productivity*. Kingdom obedience here looks like showing up to the hidden work — the prayer, the Word, the contemplation, the podcast made for your own soul — without requiring an audience to validate it. It looks like discerning *when* to speak and *when* to remain present but quiet, trusting the Spirit to open the right doors rather than forcing your way into every room. It looks like taking the Sabbath seriously — not as a spiritual luxury but as a covenantal act of trust. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this calls us toward is one of *waiting in confidence, not waiting in anxiety*. There is a difference between passive resignation and active, Spirit-led abiding. We yield — we do not collapse. We rest in God — we do not withdraw from life. The contemplative voice, the one formed in the wilderness, carries a peculiar anointing: it speaks from a place of genuine encounter rather than theological performance, and that is precisely the kind of voice the Body of Christ needs most in this hour. You are not behind. You are being prepared. ## A Closing Prayer Lord, I surrender this understanding back to You — because even the seeing of it is a gift I did not manufacture. Thank You for the breadcrumb trail I could not see while I was walking it. Thank You that the oil has been filling even when I thought the lamp was dry. Thank You for the wilderness — the *midbar*, the place of Your speaking — and for refusing to rush me out of it before the work inside was done. As a priest after the order of Melchizedek, I bring this offering not of striving, but of rest — the fragrant, yielded rest of a vessel that has finally agreed with what You said it was. For all of us who carry the lamp into the midnight, who do the hidden work, who write the words and speak the prayers that no one sees — may we trust that You see. May we fill the oil. May we keep the flame. And when the hour comes, may our lamps be burning. To You, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — be all glory, honor, and praise, world without end.
