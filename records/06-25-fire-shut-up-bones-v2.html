# 06-25 Call Reflection: Kingdom Business, Stewardship, and Divine Momentum **Title:** 06-25 at 17:00 — The Fire Shut Up in the Bones: Kingdom Business and the Stewardship of Divine Momentum **Recorded:** 2026-06-25 17:00:27 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture / Call Reflection) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I listened to my brother lay out a vision with the kind of clarity and velocity that only comes when something has genuinely broken open — when the fog lifts and the path forward is suddenly, startlingly visible. There was fire in that call, and I recognized it. What arrested me most, though, was not the pitch deck or the pricing structure or even the scalability model — it was the moment near the end when my brother said, almost in passing, *“the Lord has bestowed it on us — we don’t deserve it — we deserve it through Christ,”* and we both said together, *“we accept the gift.”* That was the hinge of the whole conversation. Everything else was the fruit of something rooted much deeper than strategy. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ is not exempt from the domain of work, enterprise, building, and commerce — we are not called out of the world but sent into it as salt and light, as stewards of whatever the Father entrusts. What this call revealed, under the surface of sales infrastructure and distribution models, was a real and present question: *How do men who belong to the Kingdom of God steward divine momentum without losing the source of it?* This matters because the fire of God is not the same as the adrenaline of ambition, even though they can feel identical in the body. Saints who cannot tell the difference will build quickly, burn out completely, and wonder what went wrong. The capacity to hold fire loosely — to bring it to the secret place before releasing it — is not spiritual timidity; it is the discipline of sons who know whose fire it actually is. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a natural vantage point, this conversation reads as exactly what it appears to be on the surface: two driven entrepreneurs in a productive flow state, mapping out a scalable business model, excited about a high-ticket client prospect, and rallying each other toward momentum. The natural mind would assess it through the lens of hustle culture — two young men grinding toward financial independence, leveraging modern sales infrastructure, motivated by income targets and investor readiness timelines. It would celebrate the energy, affirm the strategy, perhaps caution against overwork and Monster energy drinks, and measure success entirely in closed deals, monthly recurring revenue, and the speed of the path to the thirty-thousand-dollar runway. From that frame, the spiritual language sprinkled throughout — the references to the Lord, to grace, to the Kingdom — would read as either motivational religious metaphor or personal quirk. Nothing more. **The Limitation:** But there is a dimension of what happened on that call that natural reasoning simply does not have the instruments to measure. When my brother said he felt the Spirit had gotten hold of him, when I responded by pointing him toward the secret place before making major decisions, when we together confessed that this gift is received through Christ and not deserved through performance — we were not using spiritual language as a productivity hack. We were acknowledging that there is a Source beneath the source, a current running under the current of strategy and execution, and that current does not belong to us. The natural mind can optimize a business model. It cannot discern whether the fire in a man’s bones is the breath of God or the burn of his own desire. It cannot teach a man to hold a thirty-thousand-dollar target loosely because the Spirit might move before the number is reached. That kind of discernment is not available to the unrenewed mind — it is the inheritance of those who have learned to be still enough to hear. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with this call now, I find myself drawn back not to the pitch deck but to a phrase I used almost instinctively: *“I need a cake baked on coals.”* My brother laughed, but I meant it in some deep and tired place. The reference is to Elijah — the prophet who had just called fire from heaven, who had outrun a chariot, and who then collapsed under a broom tree and asked to die. And the angel of the LORD touched him and said, *“Arise and eat, because the journey is too great for you”* (1 Kings 19:7, NKJV). There was a cake baked on coals. There was a jar of water. There was no condemnation, no rebuking of weakness — only provision, and the tenderness of a God who knows the weight that genuine Kingdom labor places on a human body. I did not use that phrase accidentally. The Spirit was pressing something through my exhaustion. The fire my brother was carrying on that call — the dialed-in clarity, the cascading vision, the sense that everything was finally converging — I recognized it because I have felt it. It is a real thing. But Elijah’s story does not end at the broom tree, and it does not end at the cave on Horeb where he hid. It ends with a still small voice — קוֹל דְּמָמָה דַקָּה (*qol demamah daqah*, KOLE dem-ah-MAH dah-KAH) — a voice of gentle stillness, a thin silence — asking the same question twice: *“What are you doing here, Elijah?”* (1 Kings 19:9, 13). The question is not an accusation. It is an invitation to re-narrate. It is the Spirit drawing the prophet back to assignment from the grip of momentum and exhaustion alike. What I notice in this is that both fire and fatigue can become places where we lose orientation. My brother was in fire — magnificent, clarifying, Kingdom-useful fire. I was in fatigue — real, legitimate, body-asking-for-rest fatigue. And in that conversation, without either of us fully naming it, we were doing something ancient: we were bearing one another’s burdens, as Paul instructs in Galatians 6:2, and in doing so fulfilling the law of Christ. His fire was loaning me something. My groundedness was offering him something. That is what the Body does. It is not merely transactional partnership — it is the mutual supply of grace through covenant relationship. The moment that stopped me most deeply was not the nine-thousand-five-hundred-dollar pitch breakdown — though I want to honor the genuine excellence and diligence in that — but the moment my brother said, almost breathlessly, *“We don’t deserve it. We deserve it through Christ.”* There is a whole theology compressed into that correction. He started to say we don’t deserve it — which is the posture of a man who has not yet fully inhabited his sonship — and then he caught himself and reframed it through the righteousness of Christ. That is not small. That is the difference between receiving grace as a beggar and receiving grace as a son. Ephesians 1:3 declares that the Father has *“blessed us with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ”* — past tense, accomplished, already granted in the heavenly realm, to be received by faith in the earthly one. My brother was not just being humble. He was rehearsing the grammar of the covenant. The word we both used in response was *“we accept the gift.”* That phrase deserves to be unpacked slowly. The Greek word for gift in the New Testament is often δωρεά (*dōrea*, doh-REH-ah) — a free gift, given without condition, entirely from the will of the giver. It is not χάρισμα (*charisma*, KHAR-iz-mah), which emphasizes the grace-endowment for specific Kingdom function, nor is it merely δῶρον (*dōron*, DOH-ron), the general term for offering. δωρεά (doh-REH-ah) is the gift that cannot be earned, negotiated, or deserved — it can only be received. When John 4:10 records Jesus saying *“If you knew the gift of God,”* the word is δωρεά (doh-REH-ah). When Acts 2:38 speaks of the gift of the Holy Spirit, the same word appears. My brother and I were, perhaps without knowing it, speaking the language of Pentecost. The business is a δωρεά (doh-REH-ah). The vision is a δωρεά (doh-REH-ah). We accept it. We do not seize it. There is also something I want to name about the wisdom I offered when my brother talked about the thirty-thousand-dollar target and whether to quit his job. I told him to hold that number loosely. I said that the Spirit may move before the number is reached. I referenced fire shut up in bones — and that reference is not accidental either, because Jeremiah uses that exact language in Jeremiah 20:9 when he says, *“But His word was in my heart like a burning fire shut up in my bones; I was weary of holding it back, and I could not.”* Jeremiah did not quit because the budget was right. He could not stop because the fire would not let him. That is a different kind of readiness than a savings account reaching a threshold. Both thresholds matter — the financial wisdom of preparation and the spiritual responsiveness to divine timing — but they must be held in tension, not in opposition. The secret place is exactly where that tension gets resolved. What I find the Spirit pressing through this is the doctrine of stewardship as a form of worship. The Parable of the Talents in Matthew 25:14–30 does not describe men who received gifts and then waited passively for theological clarity before acting. The faithful servants put the money to work immediately. The wicked servant buried it — in fear, in excessive caution, in a misunderstanding of the master’s character. My brother’s dialed-in momentum, his cascading vision, his urgency to build — that is putting the talent to work. The spiritual question is not whether to build; it is whether the building is oriented toward the King’s return or toward the builder’s own glory. That orientation is everything. And then there is the scalability language — the affiliate networks, the content creators, the commission structures, the distribution infrastructure — and I do not want to spiritualize business mechanics unnecessarily, but I also cannot miss the Kingdom parallel. The model my brother was describing — train setters, they learn and earn, graduate to closers, add affiliate distribution, multiply reach through relationship networks — is structurally not unlike the model Jesus used. He poured into twelve, who poured into many more, who established ekklēsia communities that spread through relational networks across the known world. The Church was the original viral distribution model, and it worked because the product — the Gospel — was genuinely transformative and the commission structure was anointing and eternal life. I am not flattening business into ministry or ministry into business. I am saying that fruitful Kingdom people often find that the principles of multiplication, mentorship, and faithful stewardship operate across domains because they are woven into the fabric of how God designed increase to work. There is one more thing I need to sit with. Near the end of the call, I said: *“Just remember every meeting, every phone call, every room we enter with this business is an opportunity to bring the Kingdom’s light into it.”* I meant that with everything in me. And I want to expand it here, because it bears expansion. The Kingdom of God does not arrive only in church buildings or prayer meetings. It arrives wherever a son or daughter of the King carries it with them — into negotiation rooms, into pitch calls, into the architecture of a sales funnel, into the way we treat a client who can’t afford the premium tier. Matthew 5:14 says we are the light of the world, and a city set on a hill cannot be hidden. The city is not the Sunday service — the city is our lives, our businesses, our conversations, our way of operating in the world. When my brother closes a deal with integrity and excellence and then says, *“the Lord bestowed this,”* the Kingdom just arrived in the room of that transaction. I also want to name something quietly sobering: momentum can become its own theology if we are not careful. The feeling of flow, of things clicking, of vision cascading into vision — it is intoxicating, and it is genuinely a gift when it is God-sourced. But the enemy is not above counterfeiting the feeling of anointing. Proverbs 14:12 warns that *“there is a way that seems right to a man, but its end is the way of death.”* This is not a word of discouragement — my brother’s vision seemed sound and Spirit-adjacent in every way I could discern. It is a word of posture: keep the secret place open, keep the ear tuned, keep the thirty thousand loose enough that if the Spirit says go at twenty-eight, you can go at twenty-eight. That kind of responsiveness is not irresponsibility — it is faith. Finally, I want to return to the beginning of this call, to the exhaustion I named, to the cake baked on coals. Rest is not the absence of faith. Rest is the act of trusting that the Kingdom does not depend on my output for its advance. The very structure of Sabbath — שַׁבָּת (*shabbat*, sha-BAHT) — built into creation before the fall, before the curse, before the need for recovery ever existed, tells us that cessation was God’s design before it was humanity’s necessity. He rested on the seventh day not because He was tired, but because rest is the declaration that the work is complete, that it is good, that the one who worked is not enslaved to the work. When I told my brother I needed twenty-four to forty hours off, I was not confessing weakness — I was practicing shabbat (sha-BAHT). And that too is an act of Kingdom faithfulness. ## Implications and Challenges The grace of God is the ground beneath every insight in this meditation, and it is from that ground I offer these observations — not as condemnations but as invitations to greater clarity. First, there is a subtle but dangerous conflation that can settle into Spirit-filled entrepreneurs: the tendency to interpret breakthrough momentum as perpetual divine endorsement. The feeling of being *dialed in* is real and can genuinely be the Spirit’s breath. But feelings of momentum are not the same as confirmed direction. The practice of bringing even exciting convergences to the secret place — before acting, before committing, before announcing — is not unbelief; it is the spiritual due diligence that separates sons from spiritual opportunists. Second, the modern Western Christian workspace often treats rest as a spiritual liability rather than a covenant provision. We celebrate the grind, we quote Proverbs 31 in the direction of productivity, and we quietly shame the need for stillness. But the God who built shabbat (sha-BAHT) into creation before sin entered the world is telling us something structural about human design: ceasing is not falling behind. It is an act of worship that announces the Lord as the sustainer of the work rather than our own uninterrupted effort. Third, there is wisdom in holding financial and logistical thresholds — like a thirty-thousand-dollar runway — as wisdom structures rather than as the voice of God. Both Scripture and Spirit can coexist: make a wise plan, hold it loosely before the Lord, and be willing to be moved earlier or later than the plan accounts for. The plan is a scaffold; the Spirit is the architect. Fourth, the business-as-Kingdom-vehicle framing is genuinely beautiful and biblically coherent — but it requires ongoing vigilance about whose glory is actually being served. The line between building for the Lord’s increase and building for one’s own legacy can blur imperceptibly under the warmth of godly language and genuine fruit. The question is not whether God can bless a business — He clearly can and does. The question is whether the business remains under His lordship when the success becomes consistent, when the numbers grow large, and when the applause of the marketplace begins to feel like sufficient affirmation. Fifth, the discipleship model embedded in this call — train setters, graduate them to closers, multiply through relationship and mentorship — mirrors genuine Kingdom multiplication. The challenge for saints who build this way is to ensure that the people being trained are not simply resources being leveraged but brothers and sisters being genuinely developed. The difference shows up in how the model treats people who do not perform, how it responds to failure, and whether the community being built has room for the weak and the slow as well as the sharp and the hungry. Sixth and finally, the closing acknowledgment of this call — that every room entered is an opportunity for Kingdom light — is perhaps the most important and most frequently forgotten principle of sacred commerce. Let it not remain a motivational closing line. Let it become an operational posture that governs how every deal is pitched, how every client is treated, and how every moment of failure is processed before God and before people. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This meditation calls us to develop a practiced discernment between fire that originates in God and fire that originates in the flesh — not because one is holy and the other is sinful, but because only fire that originates in God sustains without consuming. It calls us to become men and women who can be fully in flow and fully in surrender simultaneously, who can build with velocity and hold direction loosely, who can receive momentum as a gift and release it back to the Giver without grasping. **For Kingdom Obedience:** We are called to bring the whole of our lives — including our businesses, our pitches, our partnerships, our distribution models, and our financial milestones — under the lordship of Jesus Christ. This is not pietistic language. It means that every threshold decision — when to quit, when to launch, when to commit — gets brought to the secret place before it gets committed to the calendar. It means that rest is practiced, not merely survived. It means that the people we build with are treated as image-bearers before they are treated as contributors. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation calls us into is one of attentiveness — learning to hear the still small voice beneath the noise of momentum, strategy, and excitement. It is the posture of men who know that fire shut up in the bones is real, that divine timing is real, and that the Lord who initiates is also the Lord who sustains. We watch for His movement. We wait in active readiness. We yield our timelines to His. We testify in every room we enter that the source of the gift is not our cleverness but His grace. And we remain faithful to both the thirty-thousand-dollar discipline and the Spirit’s liberty to say *go* before the number is reached. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as a son who holds a gift he did not earn and a vision he could not have assembled alone. What broke open on that call — the clarity, the pricing, the cascading infrastructure, the fire in my brother’s voice — I receive it as δωρεά (doh-REH-ah), freely given through Christ, freely received in His name. I thank You for brothers who speak Kingdom language in the middle of a pitch breakdown, who correct themselves mid-sentence to say *we deserve it through Christ*, who call each other to the secret place before making the leap. I thank You for the cake baked on coals. I thank You that You know the journey is too great and You provide for the prophet before the next assignment begins. Lord, I ask that You guard the fire — keep it from becoming mere adrenaline, keep it from becoming idolatry of momentum, keep it tethered to Your purposes and Your glory. I ask that every room we enter — every pitch call, every client meeting, every booked call that converts and every one that does not — would carry the light of Your Kingdom into it. I agree with Your Word that the laborer is worthy of his hire, that diligent hands bring profit, and that unless You build the house the laborers labor in vain. Build this house, Lord. We are the servants You have given gifts to. We are putting them to work. We are coming back with the increase. And we are keeping the secret place open, so that when You speak in the still small voice, we will hear it above the noise of everything we are building. Be glorified in all of it. In the name of Jesus Christ, the Lord of every room and every negotiation and every thirty-thousand-dollar threshold — Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | 1 Kings 19:7 — “Arise and eat, because the journey is too great for you” | מַלְאָךְ (mal’ak, mal-AHK) — messenger / angel | God’s provision for prophetic exhaustion precedes the next commission;
