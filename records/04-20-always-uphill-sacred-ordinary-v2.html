# 04-20 Personal Meditation: Sacred Ordinary — Shared Pilgrimage Through San Francisco **Title:** 04-20 at 08:01 — Always Uphill: The Sacred Ordinary of Shared Pilgrimage Through San Francisco **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Personal Meditation ## Opening Observation As I sit with these transcripts — these living, unhurried records of days in San Francisco — I find myself arrested not by doctrine debated or Scripture quoted, but by something quieter and far more difficult to name: the sacred texture of ordinary togetherness. There is no pulpit here, no formal exegesis, no structured teaching moment across any of these hours, and yet the Spirit does not require any of those things to speak. What I am hearing, across the cable cars and coffee shops and rain-slicked uphill streets and driverless cars, is the sound of pilgrims in transit — and I am beginning to understand that the Kingdom of God is not absent from such moments. It is, in fact, most itself in them. **Why This Matters:** The Western church has developed a troubling reflex — the habit of quarantining the sacred from the ordinary, reserving holiness for designated hours and spaces while treating the rest of life as spiritually neutral filler between “real” moments of faith. But Scripture knows nothing of such a partition. The same God who spoke from Sinai also bent low into the dust of daily bread, into borrowed boats, into wedding wine, into road-dust on sandaled feet between Galilee and Jerusalem. Discipleship, rightly understood, is not a retreat from ordinary life into spiritual isolation — it is the transformation of ordinary life into the arena where the Kingdom moves, where love is practiced without an audience, where patience is tested not in persecution but in uphill streets and Waymo rides and mismatched airplane seats and Starbucks bathroom codes. If I cannot find God in the crooked streets and the cold rain and the shared breakfast burrito and the cable car swipe, I am not prepared to find Him anywhere that truly matters. And if I cannot carry the same receptivity through a Tuesday in San Francisco that I practice in a Sunday sanctuary, then I am spiritually present for only a fraction of my actual life. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, encountering these transcripts, would classify them as noise — conversational clutter, logistical chatter, vacation banter between travel companions navigating airports, coffee shops, cable cars, tourist itineraries, and a driverless car whose novelty wears off before the first intersection. It would look for significance in the data: the destinations, the ticket prices, the weather complaints, the phone battery percentages, the bathroom codes, and perhaps note with mild sociological interest the linguistic mixing of English, Tagalog, and Indonesian that surfaces in moments of warmth and laughter. The secular framework is not wrong that these are ordinary things. What it misses entirely is that ordinary things, when inhabited by people who are learning to love one another — who reorganize around an unglamorous need, who share food before long journeys, who repeat a five-digit code until they are certain everyone has it — are never merely ordinary. The psychologist might note bonding behaviors. The sociologist might note code-switching as a marker of relational intimacy. The travel writer might sketch the scene pleasantly. But none of these lenses possess eyes for what is actually happening at the level of covenant — at the level of persons formed by God, walking through His world together. **The Limitation:** There is a register of perception that belongs entirely to the Spirit, and it cannot be accessed through intelligence alone — not because the truth is concealed from sharp minds, but because what is being revealed here belongs to a dimension that requires spiritual sight to perceive. The natural eye reads the bathroom-code detour as an inconvenience. It reads the cable car swipe as a transit transaction. It reads the Waymo’s precise, unsupervised navigation as a technology demonstration. It sees the church on the corner — Saint Dominic’s, named and nearly passed — as architectural scenery. But when the interior eye that Paul calls “the eyes of your heart” (Ephesians 1:18) is opened by the Spirit, something else becomes visible entirely: the community that finds the Starbucks code so everyone gets through the door is practicing covenant. The car that governs perfectly without a visible driver is a shadow of a Kingdom governed by a King whose hand never leaves the wheel. The church glimpsed on the hill and briefly named is an act of reorientation — a small, unrehearsed turning toward what is set apart in the middle of all that is not. What the natural mind dismisses as unremarkable is precisely where most of the actual life of faith is formed. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I want to begin with the geography, because the Spirit seems determined to begin there. San Francisco is a city of hills — vertiginously, uncomfortably, insistently steep. The streets tilt at grades that require cable cars to ascend. Lombard Street, the famous crooked road, winds downward in curves because a direct descent would be impossible. And threaded through all of these conversations is the recurring reality of climbing: uphill to Twin Peaks in the rain, uphill through Chinatown, uphill to the Painted Ladies, uphill with a failing umbrella and luggage and tired legs. “It’s all uphill,” someone says. And the others laugh, and press on. The Spirit will not let me pass this too quickly. What I am hearing in that laughter and that pressing-on is not merely a travel observation. It is κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-no-NEE-ah) — the New Testament word for fellowship, drawn from Acts 2:42, carrying a freight of meaning far richer than pleasant association. Koinōnia is participation in a shared reality. It is co-participation in both spiritual inheritance and daily bread, in the uphill and the downhill alike. The early church devoted themselves to it alongside the apostles’ teaching, the breaking of bread, and prayer — and what I hear in these transcripts is κοινωνία (koy-no-NEE-ah) happening without ceremony: shared food, shared laughter, shared navigation, shared inconvenience, shared wonder. The Waymo lodges something in me I cannot quite release. Here we were — a group navigating a vehicle with no visible driver, and yet the car made every correct turn, stopped at every appropriate intersection, and governed each challenge with precision. When someone noted that someone was watching — that cameras and AI were monitoring everything — I heard beneath the observation an echo of something much older: אֵל רֳאִי (El Ro’i, el roh-EE), the God who sees me. Hagar spoke that name in the wilderness, astonished that she had not been invisible to the One who governs all things (Genesis 16:13). The driverless car that maintains perfect watch without a visible operator is a shadow, however imperfect, of a Kingdom governed by a King whose hand is always on the wheel even when His form is not seen. This is not a mystical overreach — it is the Spirit borrowing from the visible to speak about the invisible, which is precisely His habit across all of Scripture. He spoke through burning bushes and donkeys and dreams and sea crossings, and He is not less eloquent now because we have moved on to autonomous vehicles. Then there is the Starbucks ending — and I want to slow all the way down here because I almost missed it. The day concludes not with a summit view or a formally spiritual moment but with a bathroom code. One three nine seven zero. I repeated it five times. I wanted to make certain everyone had it. That is a small thing. It is also not a small thing at all. What I was doing, in the middle of a city morning full of logistical motion, was making sure every person in my care had what they needed to get through the door. I did not deliver it as a speech. I just kept saying the numbers until I was certain they had landed. This is what shepherding looks like below the threshold of self-consciousness: quiet, repeated, sufficient. And I am sitting now with the image of the Good Shepherd who goes before — who does not push the flock through a door He has not first opened — and I am seeing something in that repeated bathroom code that carries the shape of priestly care. Make sure everyone has what they need. Say it again if you have to. I keep returning to the church. Saint Dominic’s, there on the corner, identified by someone in the group and folded back into the conversation about rain and cable cars and which direction to walk. A place set apart for the worship of God stood in the middle of a busy city street, and we noticed it. Even that noticing matters. The Hebrew concept of זָכַר (zakar, ZAH-kar) — to remember, to call to mind, to reorient — is not merely a cognitive act. In the Old Testament, to zakar is to turn one’s whole posture toward covenant reality. When Israel was told to remember the LORD their God, it was not a mental exercise; it was a reorientation of the whole person. That brief naming of the church — *is that where the priest lives? Yeah, it’s a church* — was a small act of זָכַר (ZAH-kar). A noticing. A momentary turning toward what is set apart, in the middle of a city that had no shortage of other things competing for attention. The Spirit does not require the noticing to be eloquent. He only requires it to be real. The multilingual texture of these conversations carries its own theological weight, and I do not want to pass over it lightly. English, Tagalog, Indonesian, and the universal language of laughter moved through the exchanges like water through an open hand — natural, unforced, belonging to no one more than another. “My English is finished,” someone said, laughing — and then the conversation simply continued by other means. Terima kasih. Apa kabar. The reaching-across was not merely polite. It was philoxenia — φιλοξενία (philoxenia, fil-ox-en-EE-ah) — the love of the stranger, the one outside the familiar circle, which the letter to the Hebrews instructs us not to neglect, for by it some have entertained angels without knowing it (Hebrews 13:2). And I am also seeing in this multilingual fellowship something that touches the reversal of Babel. At Babel, the confusion of languages was the sign of division — the scattering of those who had attempted to build a kingdom without God (Genesis 11:1-9). At Pentecost, the Spirit reversed that sign — not by imposing a single tongue, but by enabling each to hear in ἰδίᾳ διαλέκτῳ (idiai dialektoi, ih-DEE-ah dee-ah-LEK-toh), their own native language, the mighty works of God (Acts 2:6-11). What I witnessed in these conversations does not require a single language to sustain itself. It moves between tongues with grace and humor, and the connection does not break. This is a sign of what Spirit-wrought unity looks like: not uniformity, but love that transcends the barriers where language runs out. The Sagrada Família entered the conversation almost in passing — that great unfinished basilica in Barcelona, designed by Gaudí, under construction for over a hundred years and only now approaching completion. I cannot let it pass so easily. There is something deeply theological in a building that takes a century to complete. The Western church has largely lost its capacity for long obedience. We want quick revival, quick breakthrough, quick growth, quick answers. But no single generation completed the Sagrada Família, and no single generation could have. It is more glorious for that fact. This is what Paul was reaching toward in Ephesians 2:19-22, where he describes the household of God as συναρμολογέω (synarmologeō, soon-ar-mo-lo-GEH-oh) — being fitted together joint by joint, stone by stone, each piece shaped to meet the next precisely. We are not mass-produced spiritual material. We are being fitted. And fitting takes time, and it takes multiple generations of architects, and the blueprint belongs entirely to the One who commissioned it. The Sagrada Família is, whether its designers intended it or not, an accidental sermon about the patience of God’s building project — and I walked past its verbal mention in a San Francisco conversation without realizing I was receiving the sermon until I sat still enough to hear it. The phone call with my brother — breaking into the middle of a tourism-filled morning to speak of something serious, something involving danger and captivity and the need for prayer — deserves its own moment of careful attention. There was no transition warning. A voice broke in from another dimension of reality entirely, carrying weight that had nothing to do with cable cars or Chinatown or cable cars. And my response was not homiletically crafted. It was simple, immediate, and covenant-shaped: I’ll definitely pray. Not I’ll think about it. Not that sounds complicated. Just: I hear you. I take this seriously. I will carry this before God. This is intercessory posture living inside a casual morning — and it is exactly the kind of kingdom instinct that gets formed not in conferences but in the accumulated practice of choosing presence over performance, day after ordinary day. The challenge embedded here is this: am I the kind of person who can hold both registers at once? Can I laugh about smoke-smell in a Starbucks and then pivot, in the same hour, to interceding for people held in dangerous places? Ecclesiastes 3 names the seasons without ranking them. The saint who can hold both without losing either is further along in formation than the one who can only inhabit one at a time. Something in the construction site moment holds me. I mentioned watching a boom lift being used to clear debris — work I had done myself, work that involves taking what is heavy and elevating it so it can be properly disposed of. There is a pastoral metaphor here that sits quietly and persistently. Much of the Spirit’s work in our lives follows this exact pattern: He lifts what is heavy, positions it correctly, and releases it into a space where it can no longer cause damage. The burden does not disappear through our own straining up the stairs. It is lifted — borne — by something with more capacity than our arms possess. Isaiah 46:4 says, “Even to your old age, I am He, and even to gray hairs I will carry you! I have made, and I will bear; even I will carry, and will deliver you.” The Hebrew behind “I will carry” is אֶשָּׂא (essa, es-SAH) — I will bear, I will take up, I will sustain in motion. The boom lift does what human effort alone cannot. And then you throw it in the chute. This is what the yoke of Christ is: not the elimination of weight, but its redistribution onto shoulders that can actually bear it — so that our arms are freed for the work that is actually ours. The Painted Ladies — those famous Victorian row houses standing against the San Francisco skyline — carried something I did not name in the moment but cannot leave unnamed now. They stood through the 1906 earthquake. They stood through decades of urban change. The buildings around them changed and fell and were replaced. The Painted Ladies remained. There is something the Spirit keeps pressing through this image: the household built on the Word does not simply appear beautiful — it endures. Matthew 7:25 says of the wise builder’s house that “the rain descended, the floods came, and the winds blew and beat on that house; and it did not fall, for it was founded on the rock.” The Greek word behind “founded” is τεθεμελίωτο (tethemeliōto, teh-theh-meh-lee-OH-toh) — a perfect passive form, meaning the foundation had already been permanently laid. The storm did not reveal the foundation’s weakness; the storm revealed the foundation’s depth. And those painted houses, persisting in color and posture against a skyline that keeps changing, are a visible sermon about the grace of being built on what does not shift. The choice to stay in while my wife went out to Twin Peaks in the rain and cold carries something worth sitting with honestly. She went. She got beautiful pictures. She came back sick. I was not triumphant about being right. I was glad she had seen the lights, and I went to find the DayQuil. This is covenant care when it is functioning honestly: I hold the space for your choice, I pray against its consequences, and when the consequences come, I go find what is needed without making you feel foolish for having chosen freely. Elijah slept under the juniper tree (1 Kings 19:5) before the angel came with bread. The sequence was not incidental. Rest before renewed mission is not failure; it is divine provision. The pressure inside many Christian communities runs heavily toward visible activity — toward the appearance of maximum engagement, maximum spiritual output, maximum presence. But the body has limits that are not failures. And the covenant partner who holds the space for another’s freedom, without turning it into a lesson, is practicing something closer to the love of God than many more dramatic spiritual gestures. The rain is worth one more sustained moment of attention. It threads through all of these conversations: wet streets earlier, rain every night or every morning, a rainy day, companions with umbrellas — one of which begins to fail almost immediately — pressing on anyway. The Psalms use rain as a recurring image of both divine blessing and divine test. “He shall come down like rain upon the grass before mowing” (Psalm 72:6). “You, O God, sent a plentiful rain” (Psalm 68:9). The prophets spoke of an early rain and a latter rain — the former preparing the soil, the latter completing the harvest (Joel 2:23; James 5:7). I am not declaring a prophetic word about latter rain. I am simply noticing that the pilgrims in these conversations did not stop when it rained. They opened what covering they had — even a failing umbrella — and they walked uphill through the wet. And this is what the saints are called to do in every season: not wait for ideal conditions, but press through with what covering is available, together, finding something almost liturgical in the shared rhythm of swipe, swipe, ready, one two three, swipe — synchrony, mutual accountability, the window not staying open forever, no one going alone. There is one final image the Spirit is pressing on me as I sit with all of this, and I do not want to rush past it. Near the end of one conversation, someone says: “I don’t want to go back to work.” It is said with laughter, but underneath the laughter is the real thing — the tension every pilgrim feels between the mountain and the plain, between the holy interruption and the relentless return of the daily. Moses came down from Sinai. Elijah came down from Carmel. The disciples came down from the Mount of Transfiguration. Jesus Himself, after every withdrawal to the heights in prayer, descended back into the press of crowds and need and opposition. The return is not the end of the holy. It is the test of whether the holy you received on the mountain has been truly received — whether it lives in you now, capable of sanctifying the ordinary it descends into. And what you carry back down is not nothing. You carry what you saw. You carry who you were with. You carry the memory of the fear and the fact that you went anyway, and the broken umbrella, and the bathroom code you repeated until everyone had it, and the church glimpsed on the corner, and the laughter when the English ran out, and the intercessory yes spoken into a serious need in the middle of a tourist morning. You carry all of it back into the ordinary Monday. And the One who descended from the Mount of Transfiguration to heal a demon-oppressed child (Matthew 17:14-18) is the same Lord who descends with you. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge these days press upon me is the assumption that spiritual formation requires a spiritual setting. Much of modern Christian discipleship has been so professionalized that ordinary shared life is not counted as formation at all. We design programs, structure curricula, and schedule environments — and meanwhile, the moments that actually form us slip by unrecognized because they look like a cable car ride or a Waymo trip or a bathroom detour in a Starbucks. Deuteronomy 6 was not describing a discipleship program. It was describing a life — a life in which the transmission of Kingdom reality happens בְּלֶכְתְּךָ בַדֶּרֶךְ (bəlekhtəkha vadderekh, beh-LEKH-teh-khah vah-DEH-rekh), in your going on the road. If we cannot discern the Spirit’s work in the unremarkable hours, we will miss a vast portion of what He is doing — and we will produce disciples who are spiritually present only during designated spiritual hours, which is to say, disciples who are largely absent from the actual texture of their lives. The second challenge is the one embedded in the language that moves so naturally through these conversations — English and Tagalog and Indonesian, mid-sentence, without ceremony. The Body of Christ in America often assumes that cultural and linguistic uniformity is the default of Christian community. But the early church was born in a moment of multilingual proclamation, and the letters of Paul address communities that were ethnically and linguistically diverse. The miracle of Pentecost was not that everyone was made to speak the same language; it was that the Spirit created comprehension across difference. The grace to receive one another across those differences — to laugh when words run out and still know you are loved — is not a social virtue. It is a fruit of the Spirit, and it requires intentional cultivation in communities that have been shaped by cultural homogeneity as the default. The third challenge surfaces in the phone call that broke the tourist morning open. The capacity to hold both the serious and the joyful without losing either is a mark of genuine spiritual maturity, and it is one that modern Western Christianity is particularly poorly equipped to develop — because we have largely separated the “serious” spaces of prayer and intercession from the “light” spaces of daily life and friendship. But the Scripture does not make that division. Ecclesiastes 3 places weeping and laughing, mourning and dancing, in the same breath, without ranking them. The saint formed in Christ does not compartmentalize. He carries the weight of real intercession into the middle of a cable car morning, speaks it honestly, releases it to God, and continues in joy — not because the weight was not real, but because the One who bears weight is greater than all of it. A fourth challenge is the temptation toward efficiency that hides inside what looks like leadership. Several times in these conversations, the pull toward the most direct route surfaces — why don’t we just go to the cable car, why don’t we take the faster path — while other voices are tracking different needs in the group. The tension between efficiency and accompaniment is real and worth naming honestly. Jesus could have healed every sick person in Israel in a single day if efficiency were His governing value. Instead, He stopped for one woman who touched His garment in a crowd. He climbed down from the crowd to stand under Zacchaeus’s tree. He made room for interruption because interruption was often the actual mission. The group that pauses to find the bathroom code is practicing, in miniature, the kind of covenant that makes genuine community possible. Accompaniment is not inefficiency. It is love operating at the speed of the person in front of you. The fifth challenge is about gratitude — the kind that is not performative but simply present. The whole texture of these conversations is saturated with small moments of delight: chocolate wafers and boba tea and cable cars and matching shirts and animals glad to hear a familiar voice coming home. This kind of noticing — seeing the gift in the unremarkable, naming it, enjoying it — is itself a form of worship. The Hebrew תּוֹדָה (todah, toe-DAH) — thanksgiving — was not only a word for formal liturgy. It was the sound of a heart that had learned to receive what God provides as gift rather than entitlement. That posture must be cultivated. It does not come naturally to the flesh. But it is one of the most powerful and most accessible forms of spiritual formation available to us in the ordinary. Finally, a word about the invitational posture that surfaces organically in these conversations — the willingness to say, even as a joke, *what if I need deliverance and fall under the Spirit — will you catch me?* — or to ask a stranger where they’re from, or to learn two words in another person’s language. None of these were evangelistic strategies. They were simply the overflow of hearts that have learned to remain open to the people in front of them. The church has sometimes allowed social anxiety, fear of rejection, or a misguided desire to protect the sacred from the unfamiliar to silence exactly this kind of organic, relational openness. But the Spirit does not need us to curate the guest list. He asks us to open the door — and sometimes, to repeat the code until we are certain everyone has it. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity in Christ is measured not only by what we receive in the high moments of prayer and Scripture and gathered worship, but by what we carry into the low and ordinary moments — travel and inconvenience and meals shared and returns to work we do not want to make. The Spirit is always present. The question is whether we are present enough to perceive Him — whether we have trained our interior attention well enough to recognize the sacred texture of the ordinary moment before it passes. Genuine maturity also includes the capacity to give one another permission to have limits — to rest without guilt, to stay in when the group goes out, to say I’m not going up there tonight without apology — and to hold that space as an act of love rather than judgment. The body has limits that are not failures, and the community that knows this is further along in formation than the one that mistakes maximum visible output for maximum spiritual health. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Kingdom obedience in the everyday looks like the repeated, unspectacular acts of care that characterize these conversations: waiting for the slower walker, finding the code so everyone can get through the door, staying on the phone long enough to actually hear what your brother is carrying and then releasing him with a genuine I’ll pray, crossing a linguistic distance with two words in another person’s language, and choosing accompaniment over efficiency when the need is real, even when it disrupts the plan. This is not spiritual theater. This is love operating in the ordinary with the same Kingdom intention that it carries into the formally sacred hours. The call is to bring unhurried attention to the people across from us at breakfast, and to receive every shared ordinary moment as an act of obedience rather than filler. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophets of Scripture were not only those who stood in courts and declared the Word of the Lord in formal settings. They were men and women who had learned to read the ordinary world as a text written by the hand of God — its geography, its weather, its social tensions, its architectural ambitions, its markets and migrations. To abide prophetically is to walk through a city and remain awake to what God is saying through its very structure. It is to notice a church on a steep street and feel something stir. It is to hear a joke about deliverance and recognize the real testimony inside it. It is to look at a century-long building project and feel the patience of God moving through time toward completion. The saints who will be ready when the greater altitudes of the coming age arrive are those who are training their courage and their fellowship in the smaller altitudes of today. Every hill climbed together is preparation. Every shared meal is practice. Every uphill street walked in the rain with a failing umbrella is teaching the body what it needs to know: that we can go further than we thought, that we can do it together, and that the One who calls us upward does not abandon us on the ascent. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I bring these ordinary, beautiful, rain-soaked days before You with thanksgiving — not because they were extraordinary, but because You are present in the ordinary, and that presence makes the ordinary extraordinary beyond what any natural eye can fully account for. Thank You for the gift of people to climb uphill with — for the laughter in the middle of logistical inconvenience, for bread shared before long journeys, for the crossing of linguistic distances with two words and a laugh, for the church glimpsed on the corner and named and not forgotten, for the mountain that was feared and climbed anyway, for the Waymo governed by an unseen hand, for the Painted Ladies that stood through every storm, for the bathroom code repeated until everyone had it, for the intercessory yes spoken into a serious need in the middle of a tourist morning. I ask You now, as one who stands before You under the covenant of Your Son, as a servant to whom You have given eyes to see and a voice to make agreement between the heavens and the earth — open the eyes of Your people to perceive You in the unhurried texture of their days. Let no moment be wasted. Let no shared meal pass without Your presence recognized in it. Let the grace carried down from the heights sanctify every plain it descends into. And when we say, I don’t want to go back — remind us that the return is not exile; it is mission. The One who descended from the Mount of Transfiguration into the need of a demon-oppressed child is the same Lord who descends with us into every ordinary Monday. May we be found faithful in the uphill and the downhill alike, and may the κοινωνία (koy-no-NEE-ah) we taste in these small shared moments be a foretaste of the table that has no end. Amen and amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |---------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Acts 2:42 | κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-no-NEE-ah) — fellowship / participation in shared reality | The early church’s devotion to fellowship was not casual association but co-participation in life, bread, prayer, and apostolic teaching;
