# 07-06 Call Reflection: Brother Garrison - Loneliness, Psychosis, and the Weight of Presence

**Title:** 07-06 at 21:12 — The Loneliness of the Luminous Mind, and the Weight of Staying Present

**Recorded:** Not provided

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture)

**Classification:** Call Reflection

## Opening Observation

As I listened back to this call with my brother Garrison, something settled heavily in my chest — not grief exactly, but a kind of sacred sobriety. Two men, both carrying weight, both circling around the difficulty of genuine connection, both trying to find a language for things that resist easy articulation. What struck me most was not the conflict embedded in the exchange, but the thread of love that refused to be severed by confusion, accusation, or the sheer strangeness of the moment. Something was present in that conversation that was larger than either of us, and I want to sit with that.

**Why This Matters:**

The Body of Christ is not assembled from the uncomplicated. The Lord does not gather only the stable, the articulate, the emotionally resolved. He gathers the ones who have been through the fire, the ones whose minds work differently, the ones who have known the darkness of psychosis, depression, isolation, and the bewildering loneliness of being misunderstood by nearly everyone around them. If I am going to be a brother in any real scriptural sense — not a social connection, not merely a contact in a phone — then I must learn to sit in the tension of conversations like this one without retreating into advice-giving, religious performance, or the subtle pride of assuming I know what someone needs. The capacity to stay, to remain present even when I do not have the right words, is itself a form of Kingdom witness. And this call is pressing that conviction into me more deeply than I expected.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

From the vantage point of natural reasoning, this call could be assessed in several different ways — none of them entirely wrong, but none of them sufficient. A psychologist might focus on Garrison’s references to psychosis and resentment as symptoms of a fragile mental state requiring clinical intervention, and might read my responses as those of an untrained friend navigating something beyond his capacity. A social analyst might see two men performing relational repair in the clumsy, halting way that men often do — deflecting into humor, into logistics, into plans for future contact — rather than going directly to the wound. A self-help framework might applaud the moment where Garrison acknowledges being in a “good place” at Teen Challenge, concluding that the conversation ended on an encouraging note of personal progress. And on a purely human level, the pregnancy announcement, the new housework demands, the apologies for delayed messages — all of this makes sense as the ordinary friction of adult friendship stretched thin by life circumstance. The world would call this a normal check-in between two people who care about each other but live busy lives.

**The Limitation:**

But natural analysis, however compassionate, cannot perceive what was spiritually alive in this exchange. It cannot see that a man who wakes now blessing the name of the Lord rather than rising in anger represents an interior transformation that no amount of good intentions produces on its own. It cannot recognize that Garrison’s loneliness — which he himself names as structural, rooted in the gap between his mind and the people around him — is not simply a social problem to be solved by better community resources. It cannot hear the sound of love pressing through confusion and unresolved grievance, insisting on itself even when the theological framework to articulate it is absent. The natural mind reaches for resolution; the Spirit reaches for presence. And in this call, it is presence — imperfect, stumbling, honest presence — that carries the greater weight.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

As I sit with this conversation now, the first thing I notice is that Garrison opened not with greeting but with accusation — not in a hostile way, but in the way that someone does when they have been carrying something for a long time and have finally found a moment to set it down in front of another person. He told me that he had associated me with a demonic presence during a period of psychosis. He told me that the cycle of this association — feeling wronged by someone who does not remember or acknowledge what happened — repeats itself across most of his relationships. My first instinct in the moment was to defend myself, to clarify my intent, to locate the specific texts he was referencing. And I see now, looking back, that even the apology I had already sent him was reaching in the right direction but landing slightly off-center — I was apologizing for my own anger, which was real and needed addressing, but Garrison was not primarily grieved by that. He was grieved by something deeper: the experience of being told that the reality he lived through was not real, or was not witnessed, or was not taken seriously.

There is a passage in Proverbs that returns to me here: *“A brother is born for adversity”* (Proverbs 17:17, NKJV). The Hebrew word translated “adversity” is צָרָה (*tsarah*, tsa-RAH) — distress, trouble, a narrow place. The image is of a brother who is not merely present in good seasons but who is specifically *born*, specifically designed, specifically called into being *for* the narrow place. Garrison is in a narrow place. And what I am confronting in myself is whether I have the actual spiritual formation to be what a brother is born to be — or whether I have been reaching for the comfortable language of Christian friendship without accepting the full weight of what that means in a situation this complex.

What compounds the difficulty is that I genuinely do not share Garrison’s experiential framework. He speaks of demons working through people, of synchronicities, of recognizing patterns in texts that feel like coordinated mockery. I cannot verify those experiences from the outside. And I notice that in the conversation, I said something that I believe was both honest and, in a certain sense, Scripturally consistent: “What you experience is what you experienced.” I did not invalidate his perception. But I also could not confirm what I did not know. This is a genuinely difficult pastoral and relational space, and I want to be careful not to spiritualize it too quickly or too easily. The Scripture is clear that there are indeed principalities and powers at work in this age — that spiritual warfare is not a metaphor but a reality (Ephesians 6:12). Paul writes that our struggle is not against flesh and blood, and the Greek word for “struggle” there is πάλη (*palē*, PAH-lay) — a hand-to-hand wrestling, the most intimate and exhausting form of combat. Garrison knows something about this wrestling. Whatever the precise shape of what he experienced, he is not simply confused. He has been in a fight.

What arrests me, though, is the moment in the conversation where Garrison said something that I found unexpectedly clarifying. He told me that the loneliness he carries is not primarily situational — not a lack of people around him, because at Teen Challenge he has community, he has people who care — but is rooted in what he called the gap between his intelligence and the people he encounters. He said, essentially, that he cannot relate to what he called “normies.” And I heard myself respond from something I had actually sat with: that the most intelligent minds are often the most ostracized, the most isolated, the most prone to despair. This is not a secular observation dressed in spiritual clothing — it has a scriptural pattern. The prophets of Israel were not celebrated by their contemporaries. They were mocked, imprisoned, cast out, and in some cases killed. Jeremiah wept alone. Elijah fled into the wilderness and asked God to take his life (1 Kings 19:4). The loneliness of a mind that perceives what others cannot, or refuses to flatten its perception to make others comfortable, is not a modern pathology. It is an ancient wound.

And yet the answer the Spirit gave to Elijah in that wilderness moment was not an explanation but a presence — the still small voice, the *qol demamah daqah* (1 Kings 19:12), which the NKJV renders “a still small voice” but which carries in the Hebrew the sense of a sound of sheer silence, a thin quietness — not the earthquake, not the wind, not the fire, but something that required absolute attentiveness to receive. What Garrison may need from me is not a theological framework for his psychosis, not advice about whether to meditate or how to be less alone. He may need, more than anything, the equivalent of that still small voice — the evidence that someone is actually present and listening, even when they do not fully understand.

This is where I must be honest about my own failure in this exchange and in the texts that preceded it. I said something that Garrison flagged as deeply wrong — that he should “meditate on whether or not he wants to be alone.” He pushed back on this, and I heard what he was saying: *do you think I have a choice?* And underneath that question is something that I think the Spirit wants me to sit with seriously. There is a form of pastoral counsel — often well-intentioned, often delivered out of genuine care — that hands suffering back to the sufferer as though it were a preference. As though the solution to loneliness were simply choosing differently, orienting the will differently, deciding to want connection more clearly. But this is not the Gospel. Paul writes in Romans 8 that the whole creation groans and labors together in birth pangs, and that even we ourselves groan within, waiting for the redemption of our bodies (Romans 8:22-23). The Greek word for “groan” is στενάζω (*stenazō*, sten-AH-zo) — to sigh under a burden, to groan inwardly. This is not the language of choice. This is the language of travail. Garrison is groaning in the deep sense. And the spiritually inadequate response to a groan is advice.

Something else surfaces as I continue to reflect. I told Garrison that I do not wake in anger anymore — that I wake and bless the name of the Lord. This was genuine. It was also, I recognize, not particularly useful to him in that moment. There is a kind of spiritual testimony that, however true, functions in a conversation as an inadvertent comparison — a way of locating myself at a higher point on a journey and narrating my position in a way that, however unintentionally, can make the listener feel the distance between where they are and where I claim to be. I do not think I did this with malice. I think I did it because I was uncomfortable with the weight of what Garrison was describing and reached for something I could offer that felt solid. But Paul’s instruction is precise: *“Rejoice with those who rejoice, and weep with those who weep”* (Romans 12:15, NKJV). The verb is συγχαίρω (*synchairo*, soon-KY-ro) — to rejoice *together with*, and κλαίω (*klaiō*, KLAH-yo) — to weep, to mourn audibly. The preposition matters. It is not *near* those who weep. It is *with* them. The posture is solidarity, not adjacency.

And yet — and I want to hold this carefully — the call did not end in failure. Garrison said he was glad I called. He accepted the offer of occasional check-ins. He let me say “I love you, brother” without deflecting it. Something passed between us that was real, even across all the confusion and unresolved tension. This tells me that love does not require perfect understanding to do its work. It tells me that showing up — even imperfectly, even without the right words — still carries weight. The Lord is not looking for me to be a flawless counselor to Garrison. He is looking for me to be a present brother. And presence, sustained across time, is one of the most powerful instruments of grace available to human beings.

The pregnancy announcement — this new life coming into the world after two years of trying — sits in the background of this entire conversation like an unexpected shaft of light. I find it theologically significant that I received word of new life entering and immediately turned my attention toward someone in the depths of loneliness and confusion. This is not accidental. The promise given to Abraham was not given in a season of ease. It was given when he and Sarah were past the natural time, when hope had been deferred so long it had become almost indistinguishable from resignation. And yet the Lord spoke: *“Is anything too hard for the LORD?”* (Genesis 18:14, NKJV). The Hebrew word translated “hard” is פָּלָא (*pala*, pa-LAH) — to be wonderful, surpassing, extraordinary, beyond ordinary human capacity. Nothing is too *wondrous* for the Lord. Not a pregnancy after two years of waiting. Not the restoration of a mind that has been through psychosis. Not the healing of a friendship fractured by words spoken in confusion and anger.

As I come toward the end of this reflection, I find myself convicted about the nature of spiritual formation in community. Garrison made a comment that pierced me quietly: most of the people he speaks to are frustrating because he believes they did things to him that they will not acknowledge. This cycle — of perceived harm, unacknowledged offense, accumulated resentment — is one of the most spiritually devastating dynamics in the Body of Christ. And the reason it is so devastating is that it can be entirely invisible to one party while being entirely real to the other. I cannot resolve what happened during Garrison’s psychosis. I cannot verify or correct the perceptions that formed during that time. What I can do is refuse to dismiss them, refuse to escalate defensiveness in response to them, and refuse to let the awkwardness of my own position push me toward the comfortable distance of infrequent messages and unanswered calls. Jesus washed feet. He touched lepers. He engaged the demoniac of the Gadarenes — not from a safe relational distance, but face to face, present, unhurried (Mark 5:1-20). That is the pattern I am being called back to.

Finally, I want to name what I saw in Garrison that I do not want to overlook: he is at Teen Challenge, he is working there, and he reports that the community around him cares for him. This is not nothing. This is the Lord at work, even in a season of confusion and loneliness. The fact that Garrison can distinguish between the structural loneliness of his mind and the social loneliness of isolation — and can report that the latter is currently being met — tells me that there is clarity operating alongside the confusion. He is not lost. He is in a difficult passage. And a brother does not abandon someone in a difficult passage simply because the terrain is unfamiliar. A brother stays close enough to call, close enough to be called, close enough for ten or fifteen minutes to matter.

## Implications and Challenges

The modern Western Christian instinct toward relational efficiency — toward keeping connections manageable, emotionally uncomplicated, and spiritually productive — is directly challenged by a call like this one. We gravitate toward relationships that affirm, encourage, and confirm. We are far less practiced at sitting inside the discomfort of a brother who is confused, who has real grievances we cannot fully verify, and who needs presence more than counsel. The implication here is that genuine brotherhood is not a program. It is a costly, long-term posture of availability.

There is also a challenge embedded in how I handled the spiritual dimension of Garrison’s experience. When a brother speaks about demonic influence or psychosis, the instinct of the doctrinally careful Christian is often to either immediately validate everything through a spiritual warfare framework or to gently redirect toward professional help. Both of these can be forms of avoidance — the first because it can bypass discernment, the second because it can dismiss genuine spiritual experience as merely clinical. The Scriptures call for something harder: humble, patient presence with one who suffers, combined with discernment that does not force premature resolution. Job’s friends were not condemned for sitting with him in silence for seven days. They were condemned for speaking when speaking was not yet warranted (Job 2:13, 4:1).

I must also sit with the way I delivered my apology in the earlier texts. There is a pattern in Christian relational culture of performing apology — of crafting the language of remorse without fully inhabiting the other person’s injury. Garrison’s response revealed that he was not primarily grieved by what I thought I was apologizing for. The lesson here is that before offering an apology, it is worth the discomfort of asking: *what specifically did I do that caused harm?* Not to demand that the other person prove their wound, but to genuinely seek to understand the shape of it before attempting to address it.

The loneliness that Garrison named — rooted in intelligence, in a different frame of perceiving reality — is a pastoral challenge the Church has not adequately addressed. Many of the most brilliant and spiritually sensitive people I have encountered sit at the edges of congregations, or have left entirely, because the community’s collective emotional and intellectual range could not accommodate them. This is not merely a social failure; it is a theological one. The Body of Christ is meant to be precisely the community where the parts that seem weaker are given greater honor (1 Corinthians 12:22-24). That does not happen accidentally. It requires intentional, uncomfortable, sustained care.

Finally, I want to address the subtle spiritual danger of narrating my own growth inside a conversation that is not about my growth. When Garrison was describing his pain, I offered testimony of my own transformation. This came from a genuine place, but it risks centering my experience in a moment that called for centering his. The Spirit is pressing me toward a greater capacity for self-forgetfulness in pastoral moments — toward the posture of John the Baptist, who said *“He must increase, but I must decrease”* (John 3:30, NKJV), and who, by extension, also knew how to point away from himself in the moment rather than claiming space for his own story.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

This call is an invitation to deepen the spiritual discipline of listening — not the listening that waits for a gap in order to respond, but the listening that receives what is being said without immediately reaching for the tool of correction or encouragement. Maturity here looks like tolerating the tension of a conversation that does not resolve cleanly, and trusting that the Holy Spirit can work through sustained, honest presence even when eloquence is absent.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

The obedience this calls forth is simple and demanding: stay. Do not allow the awkwardness of Garrison’s situation, or my own sense of inadequacy in responding to it, to become the excuse for increasing distance. The commitment to occasional calls, to checking in, to offering ten or fifteen minutes of genuine attention — these are not small things. They are the architecture of love made visible across time.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

The prophetic community must be capable of holding those who have been through spiritual extremity — those who have wrestled with forces they could not name or control — without reducing their experience to pathology and without inflating it into spectacle. The prophetic posture here is one of watchful companionship: remaining close enough to see, to pray, to speak when the moment arrives, and to be silent when silence is what love requires.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I come before You as one who has just heard himself in a conversation — not always at his best, not always with the right words, not always with the right timing — and I thank You that Your mercies are new every morning and that the blood of Jesus speaks better things than the blood of Abel (Hebrews 12:24). I stand in agreement with Your heart for Garrison — with the word You have spoken over him, with the life You are sustaining in him even through the season of confusion and loneliness he is navigating. I ask You, Lord, to be to him what only You can be: the presence that reaches past psychosis and past the accumulated wounds of feeling unseen and misunderstood. Let the community at Teen Challenge be the Body of Christ in fullness for him — not merely a social resource but a genuine foretaste of the Kingdom where the weak are honored and the weary are sustained. And in me, Lord, build what this call has revealed is still under construction: the patience to listen without fixing, the humility to apologize with precision, the love that stays present even when I don’t know what to say. Let the new life entering my household through my wife’s pregnancy be, among other things, a school of that very love — a training in attentiveness to the small and the helpless and the dependent, which is the posture You call every one of Your servants into. To You alone be all glory, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, now and unto all ages. Amen.
