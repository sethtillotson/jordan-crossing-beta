# 07-11 Call Reflection: Divine Presence in Ordinary Moments — Psalms, Light, and Brotherhood **Title:** 07-11 at 11:09 — A Sliver of Light and the Voice That Followed **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation There is something I keep returning to from this exchange — not the conversation itself in its fullness, but one small, almost offhanded detail that Brother Samuel and I barely paused on before moving forward. I woke at five in the morning, and the first thing I saw was sunlight — not broad, ambient daylight, but a single, precise beam of light forced through a sliver in the blinds by the movement of a cat. And beneath that light, the Psalms were still playing. Something in me will not let that go. **Why This Matters:** The way God speaks to His people has rarely been through the spectacular and the orchestrated. It has been through burning bushes in the corner of a desert, through a still small voice after the wind and earthquake and fire, through a star over a manger that most people walking under it never looked up to see. If we are only attentive to the large and the loud, we will miss the very language Heaven uses most fluently — the specific, the sudden, the small. For a community of saints trying to learn what it means to be led by the Spirit in the ordinary rhythms of life, the discipline of noticing matters enormously. This moment is a meditation on that discipline. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, everything described in this conversation is unremarkable and easily explained. A man fell asleep to audio playing on a device — a common sleep habit — and woke when light happened to hit his eyes at an angle, the result of a restless pet nudging the window blinds. He noted the coincidence with mild interest, shared it with a friend, and the conversation moved on to neighborhoods, cats, and the kind of easy banter that fills the space between brothers who are comfortable with one another. There is nothing here that compels a secular framework to pause. The mind that processes by cause and effect will catalog it as sensory experience, file it under pleasant morning, and move forward without theological freight of any kind. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind operates within a closed system — it reads the world as though God is absent from the ordinary details of it, as though light is merely physics and timing is merely chance. This kind of seeing is not villainous; it is simply incomplete. It lacks the relational grammar that comes from knowing that the God of Scripture is not distant or abstract, but near, personal, and entirely capable of using a sleeping cat and a displaced blind to say something to one of His sons at five in the morning. Without the lens of covenant relationship, the moment remains just a moment. With it, the moment becomes a door. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I keep thinking about the Psalms themselves — that I had fallen asleep to them, that they were still playing when I woke. There is something profoundly right about that. The Psalter is not a book you read quickly and set aside. It is a book that breathes. It was designed to be sung, repeated, meditated upon, and returned to in darkness and in dawn. The Hebrew word often translated *meditate* in Psalm 1 is הָגָה (hagah, hah-GAH) — and it does not mean quiet intellectual reflection. It means to mutter, to moan, to murmur under the breath, to speak a thing repeatedly until it lives in the bones. David and the sons of Korah were not producing material for a morning devotional slot. They were building a practice of continuous, embodied, voiced encounter with the living God. That the Psalms were *literally still sounding* when I opened my eyes feels like more than accident. Then there is the light itself. A single ray, precise and concentrated, directed by what amounts to the smallest of domestic disturbances — a cat, a blind, an inch of separation. And yet it was enough. It was exactly enough to open the eyes of a sleeping man. I find myself sitting with Psalm 36:9 here: *“For with You is the fountain of life; in Your light we see light.”* The light that woke me was not metaphorical — it was literal morning sun. But the Scripture tells me that all light, physical and spiritual, has its source in the God who is Himself light (1 John 1:5). There is a Canon-long thread here that runs from the first words of Genesis — *“Let there be light”* (Genesis 1:3) — through the pillar of fire in the wilderness, through the Transfiguration on Sinai’s echo, Tabor, all the way to the New Jerusalem where *“the city had no need of the sun or of the moon to shine in it, for the glory of God illuminated it”* (Revelation 21:23). Light in Scripture is never merely optical. It is always theological. What arrests me further is the *specificity* of this light. It was not a room slowly brightening with the rise of dawn. It was a single beam, through a single sliver. God, throughout Scripture, is relentlessly specific. He did not call out to the camp of Israel in the wilderness — He called Moses by name from a bush. He did not announce the Incarnation to the general population of Judea — He sent Gabriel to one woman in Nazareth. He did not rebuild the broken man Job with general encouragement — He spoke from the whirlwind in particular, declarative questions that named the foundations of the earth and the binding of the Pleiades. The specificity of divine speech is not incidental; it is a signature of the Shepherd who calls His own sheep by name (John 10:3). Now, as I sat with this conversation later, I also found myself thinking about the broader backdrop that Brother Samuel had briefly mentioned before moving to lighter things — that he and others were at the home of a brother named Donovan’s father, who had passed roughly six or seven months ago. The purpose of the visit was to keep spirits high, to be present with a grieving family. I did not walk through that grief myself — it belongs to others — but listening to my brother mention it as simply and tenderly as he did, something in me recognized what the people of God are called to in those moments. The Psalms again. The Psalter is the Bible’s own grief literature. More than a third of the Psalms are laments — not tidy, not resolved, not quick to pivot to praise. They sit in the weight. *“How long, O LORD? Will You forget me forever?”* (Psalm 13:1). *“My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?”* (Psalm 22:1). Jesus Himself quoted that last one from the cross. The community gathered in that home in the UK, keeping company with a family in their loss, was doing something ancient and holy — something the Psalmist understood and the New Testament confirms: *“Weep with those who weep”* (Romans 12:15). There is a strange and beautiful dissonance in this conversation that I do not want to flatten. In the same call, my brother was walking streets he described as among the most dangerous in the UK — past narrow roads and townhomes and parks with a sobering history of violence — while simultaneously having just come from a house of mourning and simultaneously sharing in the ordinary warmth of brotherhood with me as I cooked for my wife and tried to prevent a cat from getting into the food. This is what the life of the saints actually looks like. It is not a consecrated sequence of elevated spiritual events. It is grief and danger and sunlight and cats and cooking and narrow streets and laughter, all braided together. And the God who speaks through a sliver of light is entirely present in all of it. The Psalms understand this. Psalm 23 does not say the Lord keeps His people from the valley of the shadow of death — it says He is *with* them in it. The promise is presence, not extraction. Psalm 46 says, *“God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble”* (Psalm 46:1). That word *present* in the Hebrew is מָצָא (matza, maht-SAH) in the verbal root — to find, to be found, to be encountered. God is a help that is *found* in trouble, not merely *promised* for after it. My brother walking through one of the UK’s rougher neighborhoods, having just visited a house of mourning, was not outside the canopy of that promise. He was inside it, even if the conversation had moved to banter about the narrow roads. And I find myself returning again and again to the Psalms as the connective tissue of this whole meditation — the five-hour reading I fell asleep to, the AI-generated musical setting still playing at seven in the morning, the ray of light that opened my eyes into a morning already soaked in those ancient songs. The early church father Athanasius wrote to a young man named Marcellinus, describing the Psalter as unlike any other book of Scripture — it is the one book, he said, in which the reader does not merely hear about someone else’s encounter with God, but speaks the words as his own. The words of David become the words of the one who prays them. The lament of Asaph becomes the lament of the saint in the twenty-first century. There is a kind of participatory union in the Psalms that no other genre in the canon accomplishes in quite the same way. To fall asleep in that river and wake in it is not a small thing. I want to say something now about the way the Lord works through the small and the overlooked, because I think it is one of the most consistently countercultural features of the Kingdom. The AI-generated songs of the Psalms — technology at its most impersonal and synthetic — becoming the soundtrack of a genuine encounter with the living God. A cat, an animal with no theological consciousness, becoming the instrument through which a sleeping man is positioned to receive the morning’s first light. God has always been comfortable using the unlikely. Balaam’s donkey. A burning bush. A widow’s jar of oil. Jawbones and slings and a shepherd boy’s five smooth stones. The principle at work is not random — it is deeply covenantal. *“The LORD does not see as man sees; for man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart”* (1 Samuel 16:7). And the God who looks at the heart also moves through the small, the overlooked, the ordinary, and the unassuming to accomplish what the powerful and the orchestrated so often fail to achieve. This is, I think, the deep invitation underneath everything in this exchange. Not simply to notice a ray of light or to appreciate a pleasant morning, but to cultivate a fundamental posture of attentiveness — a trained and prayerful readiness to recognize the voice of God in the texture of ordinary hours. The Greek word νήφω (nēphō, NAY-foh) — often translated *be sober* or *be watchful* — appears in 1 Peter 5:8 and 1 Thessalonians 5:6 in the context of spiritual alertness. It is the posture of a sentinel, not a sleeper. And yet the irony here is beautiful: the encounter I am meditating on came *while I was asleep*, precisely because I had been filling the hours before sleep with the Word. The watchfulness was not anxious vigilance — it was saturated rest. I had soaked in the Psalms for hours and then slept in them, and the Spirit moved while I was fully at rest. This is what Psalm 127:2 may be reaching toward when it speaks of the Lord giving *sleep* to His beloved — not merely physical rest, but a kind of rested trust that remains receptive even in stillness. The conversation eventually moved on, as conversations do. The call ended with Brother Samuel mildly lost in streets that all looked the same. There is something inadvertently parabolic in that too — the familiarity of a place does not always mean we know exactly where we are. Walking with God through the familiar streets of ordinary life requires the same quality of attentiveness as walking through crisis. The light does not always come through the window in dramatic fashion. Sometimes it comes as a sliver. But a sliver is enough to open the eyes of a man who has been sleeping in the Word. ## Implications and Challenges There is a tendency in much of contemporary Christianity to assign spiritual significance only to what is dramatic — the conference moment, the altar experience, the powerful prophetic word delivered in a gathering. This meditation presses against that tendency without dismissing those things. The challenge is not to abandon the extraordinary but to stop being blind to the ordinary. If God speaks through five hours of Psalms still playing at dawn, He is at work in the rhythms of our days in ways our noise and distraction habitually prevent us from perceiving. The practice of filling sleep with Scripture — of letting the Word literally continue sounding while the conscious mind rests — is not a technique or a hack. It is an ancient instinct. The Shema in Deuteronomy 6 instructs Israel to speak the words of God when lying down and when rising up (Deuteronomy 6:7). There is a covenantal logic here: the Word is to bracket the day, to be the last sound before unconsciousness and one of the first orientations upon waking. That I stumbled into this accidentally, through a long YouTube video, does not diminish its weight — it may actually amplify it. God met me where I was, not where I had performed my way to being. There is also a word here for how we accompany one another in grief. Brother Samuel’s mention of visiting a house of mourning was brief and almost casual in the flow of the call — but the act itself was not casual. It was costly presence. The modern Western instinct is to send a message, to perform condolence at a safe emotional distance. But the people of God in Scripture showed up. Job’s three friends, whatever their later failures in theology, *came* and *sat with him* for seven days without speaking (Job 2:13). That silence was ministry. The family my brother was visiting did not need answers or explanations — they needed bodies present, spirits willing to sit in the weight. This is what the Body of Christ is for. The danger of spiritualizing everything is as real as the danger of spiritualizing nothing. Not every ray of light is a word from the Lord. Not every coincidence is a sign. The discipline is not to assign prophetic significance to every random event but to remain open enough that when God does speak through the particular and the small, we are not too sophisticated or too distracted to receive it. Discernment is not paranoia in one direction or dismissal in the other — it is a cultivated sensitivity developed through sustained time in the Word and in prayer. Finally, there is a gentleness in this meditation that I want to name explicitly. The laughter in this call, the banter about cats and streets and Liverpool movies — this is not the enemy of spiritual life. It is part of it. Joy is a fruit of the Spirit. Brotherhood expressed in warmth and humor and easy conversation is a form of love. The saints are not called to be somber instruments of perpetual theological gravity. They are called to bear one another’s burdens, yes — but also to rejoice together, to enjoy one another, to be genuinely *present* to one another in all of life’s registers. This call was all of that at once, and the Spirit was in all of it. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity in the Spirit is not measured by the intensity of our spiritual experiences but by the quality of our attentiveness to God across the full texture of ordinary life. The saint who can recognize the voice of the Shepherd in the small and the specific — and who has trained their ear through sustained time in the Word — is not more mystical than others. They are simply more awake. This meditation calls us toward that kind of wakefulness: not anxious, not manufactured, but rested and receptive, rooted in the Psalms and open to the morning. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience here looks like two things held together: filling our lives with the Word in ways that persist even into rest, and showing up physically and presently for those who are walking through grief and loss. Both are costly. Both are Kingdom. The community of the saints is not an audience gathered for spiritual content — it is a Body that mourns and rejoices together, that visits houses of grief and keeps company with those who have lost something irreplaceable. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic community learns to read the language of the Lord in the small and the specific without projecting meaning onto everything or dismissing the possibility of divine communication in the ordinary. We hold the canon as our grammar, the Spirit as our teacher, and the posture of humble attentiveness as our daily discipline. We are the servants of God who watch and wait — not passively, but with the kind of active readiness that comes from soaking in His Word until even sleep becomes a kind of listening. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I am grateful for mornings that begin with light through a sliver — for the fact that You are not waiting for the grand gesture or the perfectly prepared heart but are entirely willing to speak into a five a.m. awakening, through a cat and a cracked blind and songs of the Psalms still sounding from the night before. You are the fountain of life, and in Your light we see light. I agree with Your will that Your Word would be the last thing on our lips before sleep and the first orientation of our waking — that it would bracket our days and saturate our rest and be the grammar through which we learn to read everything else. I stand in agreement with the purpose of Your Kingdom that Your people would not drift toward the spectacular at the expense of the attentive, that we would be a community who shows up to houses of mourning and keeps company with the grieving and makes space for both lament and laughter without needing to collapse the distance between them. You are near, Lord — near in the narrow streets and near in the kitchen and near in the call with a brother on the other side of the world. Train our ears. Open our eyes through whatever sliver You choose. We are Yours, and we are listening.
