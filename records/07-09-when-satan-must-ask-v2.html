# 07-09 Call Reflection: Divine Permission, Permitted Suffering, and the Lone Sheep **Title:** 07-09 at 15:11 — When Satan Must Ask Permission: The Hidden Authority of the Last Supper and the Lone Sheep Who Carries Too Much **Recorded:** 2026-07-09 15:11:20 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother pour out the weight of an eighteen-year-old life that has already seen divorce papers, sleepless nights without a proper bed, twelve-hour shifts stacking yogurt for minimum wage, and a heart that gives everything to everyone while quietly starving itself — something in me recognized a man being pressed on every side but not yet crushed. In the middle of that conversation, the Spirit opened something I had never seen before in the Last Supper account: a sovereign Jesus not merely predicting betrayal, but *commanding* the enemy who had just entered Judas to move — an echo of the very same divine permission structure that governed the suffering of Job. These two threads — a brother’s breaking point and a King’s quiet authority over darkness — are not separate conversations. They are the same revelation delivered on two frequencies at once. **Why This Matters:** The Church in the West has largely lost the theological category of *permitted suffering under divine authority*. We have been trained to interpret hardship as either divine abandonment or spiritual failure, and so when a faithful saint labors in obscurity, hemorrhages financially for others, holds up the faith of those around him while quietly drowning, and finds no bed to sleep in — the natural conclusion is that something has gone terribly wrong. But Scripture tells a different story. The permission structures of heaven are real, the enemy’s access is bounded, and the sovereign Lord who told Satan “do not take his life” over Job is the same Lord who looked through Judas at the darkness within and said, *What you do, do quickly* — not as a concession to evil, but as a King issuing a timetable. When we understand that nothing moves against the saints without first passing through the throne, the breaking point of the faithful man becomes holy ground rather than evidence of divine absence. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind listening to my brother’s words would hear a young man overwhelmed by compounding stressors: economic precarity, relational collapse, physical exhaustion, emotional suppression, and the psychological toll of chronic self-sacrifice without reciprocity. A counselor might diagnose compassion fatigue or codependency and prescribe better boundary-setting and self-care rhythms. The conversation about Judas and the Last Supper would be filed away as an interesting Bible study tangent — intellectually stimulating, emotionally detached from the real suffering on the table. The natural framework would keep these two things entirely separate: the theological insight in one column, the broken young man in another. It would work on symptoms, offer coping strategies, reframe cognitive distortions, and send my brother back to his twelve-hour shift with a new mental toolkit but no encounter with the living God. **The Limitation:** The natural mind cannot perceive that the theological insight and the human suffering were delivered together *by the same Spirit* in the same hour for the same reason. There is a kind of wisdom that can only be received when the soul is stripped down to its last layer — when the yogurt aisle becomes the threshing floor and the unsigned divorce papers become an altar. The mind of the flesh interprets desperation as disorder; the Spirit recognizes it as preparation. What looks like a young man falling apart is, in the Kingdom’s grammar, a vessel being made ready to hold something it could not have contained while still comfortable. The eyes of the flesh see a problem to be solved; the eyes of the Spirit see a process being completed. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I am sitting with John 13 in a way I had never fully inhabited before, and something has permanently shifted in my reading of it. The sequence in the text is precise and deliberate: Jesus identifies the betrayer through the act of giving bread, Judas receives it, and then the narrative states plainly that Satan entered him. It is only *after* this — after the enemy has taken up residence — that Jesus speaks. And what he says has always read to me as an instruction to Judas. But I am now seeing that it was not. “What you do, do quickly” — ὃ ποιεῖς ποίησον τάχιον (ho poieis poiēson tachion, hoh poy-EIS poy-EE-son TAKH-ee-on), literally *what you are doing, do more quickly* — is the language of a commanding general, not a resigned victim. Jesus was not surrendering. He was issuing a directive to the darkness. This changes everything about how I read the Passion narrative. The cross was not something that happened *to* Jesus. It was something Jesus *initiated* — or more precisely, something the Father authorized and the Son ratified through that table-side command. The bread broken and given to Judas was not merely a dramatic sign identifying the betrayer. It was the moment of commissioning. The Enemy received bread from the hand of the Lord and received with it a timeline. I think about how staggering that is: Satan, who had entered a man and believed himself to be engineering the destruction of the Son of God, was in fact functioning as an unwitting servant of the eternal plan of redemption. He was not outside God’s sovereignty. He was inside it, constrained by it, moving *only as far as he was permitted to move*. And this is precisely the structure I see in the opening chapters of Job. The scene in heaven — Satan presenting himself before the LORD and challenging Job’s integrity — is not a scene of divine weakness. It is a scene of divine governance. Satan cannot touch Job without asking. He must petition. He must receive a boundary. “Do not lay a hand on his person” (Job 1:12) — this is not a negotiation between equals. This is a King setting the terms for a servant’s permitted activity, and the servant has no recourse but to operate within those terms. The suffering that follows is real and devastating and it genuinely breaks Job in many of the ways it would break any of us. But it is bounded. The darkness has a leash. When my brother said half-jokingly, “Would he put me in a wheelchair so you could get me to read Psalm 30?” — I heard something truer than comedy in that line. He was, without fully realizing it, naming the theology of the threshing floor: that sometimes the Lord permits the conditions of immobility precisely so that the Word can do in stillness what busyness has prevented. Psalm 30 is a psalm of David written at the dedication of the house — a song of ascents from the pit to the praise, a declaration that weeping endures for a night but joy comes in the morning (Psalm 30:5). My brother made a joke, but the Spirit was making a point. I am also struck by the convergence of images in our conversation — the absence of a bed, the broken air mattress, the feet that ache from twelve-hour shifts. These are not incidental details. There is something in the image of a man denied horizontal rest while being called to vertical endurance. The patriarchs slept on the ground and dreamed of heaven. Jacob wrestled at the ford of the Jabbok with no comfortable place to lie down and came away with both a limp and a new name. My brother does not yet know what name is being prepared for him, but I notice the Spirit does not seem in a hurry to give him a mattress. He is making something of the man first. What arrested me most in my brother’s confession was this sentence: *I give myself no grace.* He said it quietly, almost as an aside, but it carried the full weight of a theological statement. The Greek word χάρις (charis, KAH-rees) — grace, unmerited favor — is typically the word we deploy in the direction of others or receive from God toward ourselves. But my brother had created an asymmetry: an endless outflow of grace toward everyone around him and a complete withholding of that same grace from himself. This is not humility. Humility is accurate self-assessment before God. This is a subtle form of self-condemnation dressed in the clothing of sacrifice. And I recognized it because the Lord once pressed the same thing into me: *Forgive yourself as I have forgiven you.* That phrase — spoken to me at the end of a long season of repentance and restoration — did not feel like permission to lower my standards. It felt like a command to complete the circuit of grace rather than leaving it open-ended. There is a profound connection here to the parable of the unmerciful servant in Matthew 18. The servant who was forgiven ten thousand talents and then refused to forgive his fellow servant a hundred denarii was not being wicked in an obvious external way. He had genuinely received the forgiveness of an impossible debt. But something in him refused to let that forgiveness flow fully — either outward or, in my brother’s case, inward. When we refuse to extend the grace of God to ourselves, we are not being more righteous than God. We are, in a strange way, setting ourselves above His verdict. He has declared us forgiven. To continue prosecuting the case against ourselves is to contest His judgment. I also want to sit with what I said about the lone sheep, because I believe the Spirit was speaking through that image beyond what I articulated in the moment. Jesus says in John 10:11-12 that the good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep, and that the hired hand — who has no true investment in the flock — flees when the wolf comes. But there is another dimension to the sheep-and-shepherd dynamic that gets less attention: sheep are not merely endangered by predators when alone. They are endangered by themselves. A sheep that has wandered off is not just vulnerable to the wolf; it has lost the ability to navigate, to graze properly, to find water. Sheep in isolation deteriorate. The ποιμήν (poimēn, poy-MANE) — the shepherd — does not merely protect the sheep from external threats. He is the organizing intelligence around which sheep-life becomes coherent at all. My brother was not describing idleness. He was describing isolation. And he was right that idle hands is not quite the right frame — he is working constantly, giving constantly, spending constantly. But he was also right that he was getting into his own head. That is the signature of the wandering sheep: not that it has stopped moving, but that it has lost the reference point of the flock and the shepherd, and so all that motion becomes disoriented, self-consuming labor. The noise of one’s own thoughts grows louder precisely because there is no other voice close enough to calibrate against. And this is where I want to return to the warning I gave about counterfeit voices, because I do not want to pass over it too quickly. When the Lord goes quiet — and He does go quiet, not as abandonment but as the kind of silence that comes before a new word — the enemy does not simply remain quiet in solidarity. He fills the space. He knows the frequency we are tuned to. He knows what the voice of the Lord sounds like to us, and he has centuries of practice at imitation. The Scriptures warn repeatedly: “Do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits” (1 John 4:1). The testing is necessary because the counterfeits are close. My brother named it himself without fully knowing it — when isolated, when in his own head, the voices he was hearing were telling him he was a “whiny little bitch,” that he was selfish, that no one understood him, that he was losing. None of those are the voice of the Shepherd. The Shepherd’s voice convicts without condemning, corrects without crushing, and always leads toward the flock — never further from it. Finally, I want to note what happened at the end of our conversation. My brother had a sales call looming and he asked, almost playfully, whether he should pray for it. I said I always do before calls like that — whatever the outcome is the Lord’s will. That is a small thing, and in the moment it was almost perfunctory. But as I sit with it now, I see the full arc of the conversation closing beautifully: a man who began in divorce papers and debt and broken air mattresses, moved through theological revelation about divine permission and Job’s suffering, wept honestly about his own exhaustion, received a word about self-forgiveness and the lone sheep — and ended with a simple act of prayer before a phone call. That is not a small thing. That is the Kingdom in miniature. The same God who told Satan *do it quickly* at the Last Supper table is the same God who cares about a sales call on a Wednesday afternoon. Sovereignty and intimacy are not in tension in the nature of God. They are the same attribute expressed at different scales. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation raises for me — and for many in the modern Western church — is the near-total absence of a robust theology of *permitted suffering*. We have built enormous doctrinal and emotional infrastructure around the idea that God always wants to bless, deliver, heal, and prosper immediately. When the blessing is delayed, when the bed does not materialize, when the job pays minimum wage despite faithful service, the default interpretation tends to be either lack of faith or lack of favor. But the testimony of Job, the testimony of the Last Supper, and the testimony of my brother all point in a different direction: some seasons of restriction are not the absence of God’s favor but the *presence* of His forming work. The Church needs to recover the vocabulary of the threshing floor without losing the vocabulary of restoration — because both are real, and the timing belongs to the Lord. The second challenge is the way we talk about self-grace within communities of faith. We are quite comfortable speaking about forgiving others, extending mercy outward, bearing one another’s burdens. But there is a strange cultural pressure in many Christian circles — particularly among those who are naturally given and sacrificial — to perform suffering stoically, to never admit the internal cost of obedience, and to interpret any acknowledgment of one’s own pain as weakness or self-pity. My brother was conditioned — by something, somewhere — to immediately label his honest emotional expression as “whining.” That conditioning is not holy. It is a distortion. Honest lament before God is one of the most theologically rich postures in all of Scripture. The Psalms are saturated with it. Jesus himself said, “My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death” (Matthew 26:38). Emotional honesty before God and trusted covenant brothers is not the opposite of faith. It is one of faith’s most necessary expressions. Third, this conversation surfaces the danger of *performative strength* in spiritual community — the posture of being the encourager, the one who reminds others of faith, who says “everything’s going to be okay” while not being convinced of it privately. There is a form of this that is genuinely sacrificial and Kingdom-oriented. But there is also a form that gradually creates a false public self and a hidden private suffering that never gets brought into the light. My brother named it directly: “No one gets to see that.” When the private suffering is never brought to the brethren, it becomes the very isolated territory where counterfeit voices operate without challenge. Covenant community must be the kind of place where the one who preaches faith is also allowed to confess uncertainty — not to undermine faith, but to keep the relationship with God honest and the relationship with the brethren real. Fourth, the theological insight about Jesus addressing Satan rather than Judas deserves more weight than we typically give it in popular preaching and devotional culture. If we read the Last Supper only as a story of human betrayal — Judas’s choice, Judas’s greed, Judas’s failure — we miss the deeper theological architecture underneath it. The Passion of Christ was not a tragedy that God managed to redeem. It was a *plan* that God executed through the very agency of the enemy who thought he was dismantling it. This has enormous implications for how the people of God interpret the hostile movements of darkness in their own lives. The enemy is not winning when he seems to be advancing. He is always already bounded, always already serving a timeline he did not author, always already subject to a voice that can look through any human vessel and say to the darkness inside it: *What you do, do quickly.* Fifth and finally, there is a gentle but necessary challenge to those of us who are the “theological ones” in a friendship — who tend to pivot toward the insight when the person in front of us needs the presence. I want to be honest that there are moments in this conversation where the theological unfolding came at the expense of fully sitting in my brother’s pain. The insight about the Last Supper was genuine and Spirit-given. But my brother had just signed divorce papers. He had been on his feet all day. He was crying for the first time in a long time. The theology that matters most in those moments is not always the theology articulated — it is the theology *embodied* by simply staying in the conversation, not rushing to the resolution, and letting a brother be seen. I believe the Lord was honoring both things in this call. But I hold that tension honestly. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity is not the absence of breaking points. It is the capacity to hold together theological clarity and human honesty in the same conversation — to weep over divorce papers and discover the authority of the Last Supper in the same hour, and to recognize that neither is coincidental. Maturity means we stop performing strength and start practicing the kind of transparency that allows the brethren to see where we actually are, not merely where we aspire to be. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this meditation calls for is both active and receptive. Active: make the practice of counting what is *in hand* a daily discipline — not as denial of what is lacking, but as a deliberate reorientation toward the provision already present, however small. Receptive: submit to the seasons of restriction without immediately interpreting them as spiritual failure. Ask not only “Lord, deliver me from this” but “Lord, what are you forming in me through this?” **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this season requires is one of sovereign trust expressed through small faithfulness. Pray before the sales call. Stay in the herd. Test the voices. Extend to yourself the same grace you have been freely dispensing to everyone around you. And when the Lord goes quiet, do not fill the silence with the first voice that arrives to fill it — wait, test, and remember that the Shepherd who commanded the darkness at the Last Supper table has not lost track of where you are standing. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who has received the covenant of Your Son’s blood and who stands in agreement with what You have already declared over both my life and the life of my brother — that we are not abandoned, not forgotten, not beyond the reach of the same sovereign hand that bounded Satan over Job and looked through Judas at the darkness within and said *do it quickly*, meaning: *I am not surprised, I am not afraid, and this too is Mine*. I thank You for the gift of covenant brotherhood, for conversations that contain both tears and theology, for the grace that covers divorce papers and reveals Last Supper mysteries in the same breath. I intercede now for my brother who is learning — at eighteen, at the yogurt aisle, at the threshing floor of financial pressure and relational loss — what it means to receive the grace he so freely gives. Teach him, Lord, what it sounds like when the real Shepherd speaks, so that the counterfeit voices find no purchase. Restore to him the joy of Your salvation, a bed to sleep in, and the deep settled knowing that nothing pressing against him has moved a single inch without first passing through Your hands. And for both of us — keep us near the flock, near Your voice, near the table where bread is broken and the darkness receives its orders. May You be glorified in what You are building in us, and may the watching world see eventually — even if it cannot yet believe — that the light You have placed in these clay jars cannot be hidden. To You alone be all dominion, honor, and praise. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | John 13:27 — “What you do, do quickly.” | ὃ ποιεῖς ποίησον τάχιον (ho poieis poiēson tachion, hoh poy-EIS poy-EE-son TAKH-ee-on) | The imperative tachion (“more quickly”) frames this as a command, not a concession — Jesus is issuing the timeline of the Passion, not surrendering to it. | | Job 1:12 — “Do not lay a hand on his person.” | יָד (yad, yahd) — hand / power / agency | God’s limitation of Satan’s yad over Job establishes the bounded-permission theology that echoes in the Last Supper narrative. | | John 10:11–12 — “I am the good shepherd.” | ποιμήν (poimēn, poy-MANE) — shepherd / one who leads and organizes the flock | The poimēn is not merely a protector but the organizing center of sheep-life;
