# 07-14 Call Reflection: Prophetic Calling, Ordinary Consecration, and the Watchman’s Burden **Title:** 07-14 at 15:17 — When the Watchman Would Rather Build **Recorded:** 2026-07-14 15:17:54 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture / Phone Call) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to this conversation unfold with my brother Samuel, I was struck by how much Kingdom theology was being worked out in the most ordinary language — containers being washed for tomorrow’s lunch, fuel gauges at zero, alarm clocks set at four-thirty not for productivity but for prayer. The Spirit was threading through all of it: discipline, vocation, calling, the body of Christ, the nature of encounter with God, and the painful grace of being given an office you did not choose. What arrests me most is the quiet admission that broke through near the end — that I do not necessarily *like* the seat Christ placed me in, even though I love the gift and fully accept it. That tension is not weakness. That tension is Gethsemane. **Why This Matters:** The church at large has cultivated a theology of calling that is almost entirely aspirational — as if the Spirit’s assignment will always align with our deepest natural desires, as if vocation is always something we rush toward rather than something we are pressed into. But Scripture tells a different story. Moses wanted to be a shepherd and became a deliverer. Jeremiah declared he would no longer speak and could not stop. Jonah ran the opposite direction entirely. The prophet does not always choose the rampart; sometimes the rampart is simply where they find themselves standing when they wake up. This matters for every believer who is quietly asking why their assignment feels too narrow, too hidden, or too far from what they imagined their life with God would look like. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, this conversation reads as two men working through the ordinary mechanics of life — hustle discipline, content strategy, meal prep, fatigue, gym routines, and career ambitions — with spiritual language layered on top as a kind of motivational overlay. The world would look at the discipline my brother described, up at four in the morning, cooking for the next day, structuring his schedule to protect his energy, and call it productivity optimization. It would look at my own prayer alarms, my watchman language, my discomfort in organized church settings, and call it either religious eccentricity or spiritual arrogance. The world would hear two young men sorting out their lives and would conclude that the God-talk is a coping mechanism, a framework that makes effort feel meaningful, a cultural inheritance being lightly updated for modern ambitions. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind cannot perceive what the Spirit is doing in the seams of an ordinary phone call, because the natural mind only evaluates what is visible on the surface. It cannot see that a washed container is an act of priestly stewardship. It cannot see that a fuel gauge at zero and an answered prayer to get home safely is not coincidence but covenantal faithfulness. It cannot see that a young man setting a four-thirty alarm not to work but to *watch* is participating in something as ancient as the walls of Jerusalem, where Nehemiah set watchmen who held their posts through the night. The flesh categorizes. The Spirit traces. And what the Spirit is tracing through this conversation is a thread of consecration — the offering of ordinary life to an extraordinary God who, as I said plainly in this call, cares about every stroke of the toothbrush. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It There is a phrase that came through in this conversation that I keep returning to: *excellency unto the Lord*. I said it almost in passing — that the Lord pressed me to build a routine of discipline, not for myself, but as excellence offered to Him, because whatever we do, we do as to the Lord and not to men. The apostle Paul planted this truth in Colossians 3:23 — “And whatever you do, do it heartily, as to the Lord and not to men” — and the word translated *heartily* there is ἐκ ψυχῆς (ek psychēs, ek psoo-KAYS), meaning from the soul, out of the inner life, with the full weight of one’s being. This is not the language of grudging obedience. This is the language of total consecration — where the gym, the meal prep, the alarm clock, the container washed at midnight, are all gathered into the same priestly offering that the morning prayers are. What startles me is how resistant the Western Christian mind is to this. We have divided the sacred from the secular so completely that most believers would never consider their morning routine to be an act of worship. We reserve “spiritual” language for the moments we recognize — Sunday services, devotional reading, audible prayer. But the Lord was pressing something different into both my brother and me in this season: that the smallest details of daily life are not peripheral to the Kingdom but constitutive of it. The Shema of Israel — “You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength” (Deuteronomy 6:5) — was never meant to be confined to a morning liturgy. It was meant to saturate the sitting down and the rising up, the walking by the way and the lying down (Deuteronomy 6:7). The Hebrew word translated *strength* in that verse, מְאֹד (me’od, meh-ODE), literally means *muchness* or *abundance* — your very excess, your energy, your full capacity. Every push of effort, every structured week, every refusal to fall into comfortable laziness — these are acts of מְאֹד (meh-ODE) love. I hear my brother describing what I can only call Benedictine wisdom without the monastery — the ancient monastic insight that *ora et labora*, prayer and work, are not opposites but a single rhythm of life given to God. He was not separating his business discipline from his prayer life. He was offering them together. And the Lord was meeting him in both. This is the integrity Paul describes in 1 Thessalonians 5:23, where the whole spirit, soul, and body are kept blameless — not just the Sunday soul, but the whole human being, sanctified through and through. Something in me also stopped when my brother asked what the Scripture says about people who complain without making any conscious effort to change. I reached for an easy answer about prophets who asked God to take their lives, but then he sharpened the question — he was not asking about those crushed under a genuine burden. He was asking about people who mourn a situation they are actively choosing to maintain. The verse that rose in me was Isaiah 29:13, which Jesus quotes directly in Matthew 15:8: “These people draw near to Me with their mouth, and honor Me with their lips, but their heart is far from Me.” The Greek word translated *far* in the Matthean quotation is ἀπέχει (apechei, ah-PEH-khay), which is a commercial term — it means *to hold at a distance*, *to have received in full and sent away*. The lips have completed the transaction. The heart was never part of it. This is the anatomy of self-pity without repentance: a performance of mourning that has already decided the outcome will not change. This led naturally into the conversation about organized religion, and here I want to walk with precision, because I felt — and feel still — two things simultaneously. I felt grief in my spirit walking into that church service. Not contempt. Grief. And there is a critical distinction between them that the Spirit pressed me to maintain even in the middle of that conversation. Contempt says *these people are beneath me*. Grief says *these people are being given less than they deserve*. Grief is the posture of the watchman. Contempt is the posture of the Pharisee. And one of the uncomfortable graces of this season is that the Lord is using my own past trauma with church — the wounds, the disillusionment, the spiritual hunger that went unmet — not to confirm my cynicism but to calibrate my sight. The trauma gives me eyes to see what is missing. The grace keeps me from weaponizing that sight. The κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-noh-NEE-ah) — the deep, participatory fellowship of the Spirit — that I referenced in describing what the church I visited lacked, is not simply warmth or community in the social sense. The word in its New Testament use, especially in Acts 2:42, carries the weight of shared participation in a common life that is larger than any individual. It is the fellowship of the Holy Spirit described in 2 Corinthians 13:14 — a communion that is not manufactured by program or maintained by donuts and coffee, but flows from genuine encounter with the living God. And the tragedy I was naming is not that those people are wrong to gather, but that a gathering can become a container without the contents. The form remains; the Presence is no longer the center. This is what Revelation 2:4 names in the letter to Ephesus — “you have left your first love.” The church at Ephesus still had sound doctrine, labor, and perseverance. They had lost the burning. And yet I hold this loosely — I said so in the call and I mean it. Because encounter can break through anywhere. The organized church, for all its limitations, has been a doorway for many who needed structure before they could receive mystery. It has been a first step for addicts and the broken and the lost. I do not despise the doorway. I only grieve when people are convinced the doorway is the destination. Then came the question that I did not expect to hit me as hard as it did: *Who would you want to help if you were a priest?* And I answered honestly — I want the fullness of the prophetic. Casting out demons. Healing. The word spoken so precisely in the authority of Christ that when I say “Ephphatha” — the Aramaic word Jesus used in Mark 7:34, אֶפְּפָתָח (ephphatha, ef-FAH-thah), meaning *be opened* — the deaf ear opens. That is the ministry I carry in my longing. But then I said something I need to sit with more carefully: that I do not necessarily like the office the Lord gave me. I love the gift. I fully accept it. But if it were my choice, it would have been something different. I would have been the builder. There is a mercy in that confession. It is the same mercy the Lord extended to Moses in Exodus 4 when Moses listed every reason he was unqualified. It is the mercy extended to Jeremiah in Jeremiah 1:6 — “Ah, Lord GOD! Behold, I cannot speak, for I am a youth.” The reluctance of the called is not disqualifying. Sometimes it is the very thing that keeps them honest — keeps the prophet from mistaking his own ambition for the voice of God. The watchman who would rather be building does not romanticize the rampart. He does not perform the watch for an audience. He watches because he was told to watch, and because he knows that what he sees matters even if he never sees the fruit of it himself. Habakkuk 2:2 — “Write the vision and make it plain on tablets, that he may run who reads it” — suggests that the one who writes and the one who runs are not always the same person. The watchman’s work is the writing. Someone else does the running. My brother Samuel and I occupy, as I said in the call, almost perfectly opposite positions in the Kingdom economy. He is the builder who wished he could simply be a farmer — hidden, quiet, off the face of the planet. I am the watchman who wished he could be a builder — creating, funding, constructing something tangible. And here is what I find remarkable: the Lord gave each of us the calling that cuts against our natural comfort. The builder was given the intercession. The intercessor was given the watchtower. This is not cruel. This is surgical. Because the builder who secretly longs for hiddenness will not build an empire for himself. And the watchman who secretly longs to build will not mistake observation for action. The very longing becomes a safeguard against the corruption of the gift. The fuel story my brother shared near the end of the call is the capstone of everything. Zero zero zero on the gauge. A prayer. Getting home. The Lord who cares about every stroke of the toothbrush is also the Lord who sustains the journey when the fuel runs out. This is not a dramatic miracle. It is a quiet one. And quiet miracles are perhaps the most formative, because they do not generate a crowd. They generate intimacy. They teach the believer that God is attending to the small things — that He was in the car, in the washed container, in the four-thirty alarm. He was never absent from the ordinary. We were simply not attentive enough to see Him there. ## Implications and Challenges The conversation I shared with my brother quietly challenges one of the most pervasive assumptions in contemporary Christian culture: that spiritual maturity is measured by the scale of one’s public assignment. We measure prophets by their platforms and intercessors by their conference invitations. But what this call reveals is that the most formative work of God often happens in the hidden discipline of the ordinary — in the meal prepped at midnight, in the alarm set for a prayer that may or may not come, in the container washed not for show but because the Lord sees. The church needs to recover a theology of the hidden that does not equate obscurity with unfruitfulness. There is also a challenge here for how we handle disillusionment with the institutional church. The temptation, once a believer has genuinely encountered the living God in the fullness of the Spirit, is to write off organized Christianity entirely — to nurse the wound into a worldview, to let the grief curdle into contempt. What the Spirit is pressing in me is a third way: to see the limitation clearly, to name it without condemnation, to grieve what is absent without despising those still standing in the doorway. This requires a maturity that is only possible through continued encounter with the God whose compassion is new every morning. The observation about self-pity — mourning a situation you are actively sustaining — is a word that needs to land without cushioning. The Scripture is plain: it is possible to honor God with the lips while the heart is positioned entirely elsewhere. Prayer without repentance is not prayer; it is performance. And the Spirit is not fooled by religious performance, however sincere its emotional register. If we are asking God to change something we have the agency and responsibility to address, we must bring both our prayer and our effort — the knee bowed and the hand open. The admission that I would rather have been a builder is also a challenge to a particular kind of prophetic pride — the subtle exaltation of the prophetic office that places it above the offices of service, administration, and construction. Every function of the Body is needed. Every gift is given by the same Spirit for the same purpose. The watchman who despises the builder, or the builder who dismisses the intercessor, has misunderstood the architecture of the Kingdom. We are not competing towers. We are a single temple, fitly joined together. Finally, the vision of excellence in ordinary life as an offering to God — as מְאֹד (meh-ODE) love — challenges the compartmentalization that has made so many believers spiritually vibrant on Sunday and spiritually unconscious from Monday to Saturday. The Lord is not interested in a portion of the life. The call is total. And the grace He offers is sufficient to make the gym, the food prep, the structured week, the washed container, into an altar upon which something holy is laid daily. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This meditation calls me — and those who walk with me — to stop treating daily life as the space between spiritual moments and start treating it as the spiritual moment itself. The discipline of the body, the stewardship of energy, the structuring of a week to protect what is sacred — these are not peripheral to transformation. They are the clay in the Potter’s hands. Maturity here means allowing the Lord to sanctify the whole — the routine, the rhythm, the unglamorous maintenance of a body and a schedule — so that nothing is withheld from His consecrating touch. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this calls forth is not dramatic. It is consistent. Post the video when it feels foolish. Set the alarm when you do not know if you will need it. Wash the container at midnight. Hold the rampart even when you would rather be building. Write the vision even when you do not know who will run with it. This is the obedience of the servant who does not need to understand the full strategy in order to execute the next faithful step. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation shapes is one of attentive humility — the watchman who is not performing for an audience, not building a platform, not monetizing the gift, but standing at the post because the Lord said to stand. The prophetic is not primarily the spectacular. It is the faithful. And the faithful watchman cultivates the interior life — the secret place, the set-apart time with the Father — with such devotion that when the word comes, it does not originate in human ingenuity but in divine attentiveness. We abide. We watch. We write. We trust the Lord with the timing of the running. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one set upon the rampart by Your own hand — not because I chose the post, but because You knew what this post required and You knew what it would form in me. I am grateful. I offer You this conversation, this brother, this ordinary evening of washed containers and empty fuel tanks and the quiet mercy that carried us both home. Lord, sanctify the small things in us. Let the alarm set for four-thirty be an act of love. Let the meal prepped in faithfulness be an offering laid on Your altar. Let the grief I carry for Your people in the churches be the grief of a shepherd and not the disdain of a critic, for You so loved the world — all of it, organized and disorganized, Spirit-filled and spiritually hungry — that You gave Your only Son. We receive that love. We agree with Your will and not our own. We say with Your Son in Gethsemane: not what we want, but what You want. Train us, form us, post us where You will, and be glorified in the places we would never have chosen for ourselves. May every word written here run in the appointed time, in the appointed place, into the hands of the one who will do something about it. Amen and amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Colossians 3:23 — “Whatever you do, do it heartily, as to the Lord and not to men” | ἐκ ψυχῆς (ek psychēs, ek psoo-KAYS) — from the soul / out of one’s inner life | Paul’s word for wholehearted labor is a commercial and personal term meaning to act from the full depth of one’s being — consecrating work itself as an offering | | Deuteronomy 6:5 — “You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength” | מְאֹד (me’od, meh-ODE) — muchness / abundance / full capacity | The Hebrew does not merely mean “hard effort” — it means the totality of your resource, excess, and energy poured into love toward God | | Matthew 15:8 — “This people honors Me with their lips, but their heart is far from Me” | ἀπέχει (apechei, ah-PEH-khay) — to hold at a distance / to have received in full and sent away | A commercial term indicating the transaction is complete but the relationship was never entered;
