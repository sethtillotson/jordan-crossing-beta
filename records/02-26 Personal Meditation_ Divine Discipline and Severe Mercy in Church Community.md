# 02-23 Meditation: Judas Iscariot — Unbelief, Divine Sovereignty, and Redemptive Paradox

# Meditation: Why Judas? — Unbelief, Divine Sovereignty, and the Paradox of a Love That Planned Everything

## Opening Observation

I find myself sitting with one of the most unsettling figures in all of Scripture — Judas Iscariot — and I cannot shake the question that has been pressing on me: *why?* Why did he do it? Not in the sensational, tabloid sense, but in the deep, theological, soul-searching sense — what was truly at the root of his betrayal? Something has arrested my attention here, and the more I sit with it, the more I realize this question is not ultimately about Judas at all. It is about me. It is about all of us.

**Why This Matters:** The story of Judas is not a cautionary tale about greed or treachery in the way we have been conditioned to read it. If we rush past him toward the cross, we miss the shattering revelation embedded in his life — that proximity to Jesus is not the same as belief in Jesus, and that a man can walk with the Son of God, witness every miracle, hear every parable from the lips of the Living Word Himself, and still leave unchanged. In an age where church attendance, doctrinal familiarity, and religious activity are mistaken for saving faith, the life and death of Judas may be the most urgent word the Spirit is speaking to us.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

From a purely human perspective, the betrayal of Judas is fairly straightforward to explain. Historians and psychologists have long offered frameworks that feel satisfying on the surface: he was motivated by greed — thirty pieces of silver, a tidy sum. Or perhaps he was a disillusioned revolutionary who expected a political messiah and grew bitter when Jesus refused to overthrow Rome. Some secular scholars have even cast Judas as a tragic hero, a necessary cog in an inevitable machine, and have romanticized his act as a reluctant cooperation with destiny. The natural mind gravitates toward motivation analysis — examining trauma, ideology, or economic pressure — because these are the tools available to human reason when confronted with moral failure.

**The Limitation:** These frameworks, compassionately considered, are not wrong in their observations — they are simply blind to the deeper reality beneath. The natural mind, as Paul writes, “does not receive the things of the Spirit of God, for they are foolishness to him; nor can he know them, because they are spiritually discerned” (1 Corinthians 2:14, NKJV). The natural mind can identify *what* happened and even construct plausible theories about *how*, but it cannot see *why* — not in the way the Spirit reveals it. Because the “why” of Judas is not psychological. It is theological. It reaches into the hidden depths of faith, sovereignty, and the terrifying mystery of a love that planned everything before the first atom was spoken into existence.

## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation

I am seeing something I had not fully considered before — that Judas may not have betrayed Jesus simply for the money. As I sit with this, the Spirit is drawing me toward a different conclusion: Judas betrayed Jesus because he could not bring himself to fully *believe*. And I think — I genuinely think — that deep underneath the transaction of thirty silver coins, there was a man who needed proof. A man who, on some level, wanted to *test* whether Jesus truly was who He said He was. I notice something striking here: the very thing that Judas sought — a sign, a concrete confirmation, an undeniable demonstration — is the very thing that faith, by its nature, refuses to demand. The Greek word for faith, πίστις (*pistis*), carries within it the weight of *trust*, *reliance*, and *persuasion* — not the passive intellectual acknowledgment that something might be true, but the active, volitional surrender to One you have come to trust even when the evidence feels insufficient to the natural eye. Judas never arrived there. And that gap — that terrifying canyon between proximity and *pistis* — is what destroyed him.

What wrecks me as I sit with this is the realization that Judas did not kill Jesus. He handed Him over, yes, but the betrayal itself became the very mechanism through which prophecy was fulfilled. Zechariah had written it centuries before: *“So they weighed out for my wages thirty pieces of silver”* (Zechariah 11:12, NKJV). The Hebrew word beneath “weighed out,” שָׁקַל (*shakal*), implies a deliberate, measured transaction — and yet God had already named the price. Which means that what Judas intended as an act of self-interest — or even as a test — was simultaneously an act of cosmic obedience, though entirely unwitting. This is where the narrative transcends anything the natural mind can comfortably hold. A man acted out of unbelief. And God, who is the architect of all things, had written that act of unbelief into the blueprint of redemption before the foundations of the world. I am sitting with the wildness of that. It does not make Judas less culpable — it makes God more sovereign.

And yet Judas was not saved. That is the haunting heartbeat of this passage that I cannot move past. He played his part in the greatest act of redemption in human history. He fulfilled prophecy. He handed over the Lamb who would take away the sins of the world. And he still went to destruction. Jesus Himself says of him, *“It would have been good for that man if he had not been born”* (Matthew 26:24, NKJV). This is not something I can sanitize or theologize away into comfortable ambiguity. The sobering, arresting truth is this: participation in God’s plan does not equal reconciliation with God. Judas’s tragedy is that he was *used* by grace but never *received* grace. And the difference between those two realities is belief — the deep, anchoring, life-restructuring kind. The kind that confesses not only that Jesus *was* real but that He *is* real. Living. Risen. Ascended. Present. The kind that clings to Him in doubt rather than fleeing toward a test.

This is where the Spirit begins to turn the mirror toward all of us. Because I recognize something of Judas in the human condition — in myself, if I am honest — in those moments when faith feels insufficient and I want something more concrete. The disciples asked Jesus to *show them the Father*, and Jesus answered with tender astonishment: *“Have I been with you so long, and yet you have not known Me?”* (John 14:9, NKJV). There is something about the human heart that perpetually wants to press through faith into sight. We want the burning bush. We want the fleece. We want the audible voice. And the Spirit is gently, persistently teaching me that the signs are real — but they are small. They are tucked into the texture of ordinary days. They speak in the language of presence, not performance. Faith is not the absence of doubt; it is the *presence* of trust in the middle of doubt. And that is the narrow road Judas could not bring himself to walk.

The Spirit is also drawing me to something that I find simultaneously comforting and convicting — the contrast between Judas and the Pharisees. Because they, too, missed the Messiah. The Pharisees and Sadducees, those meticulous architects of religious precision, had constructed an elaborate system of over a thousand laws and customs layered on top of the Torah, many of which had no Mosaic foundation whatsoever. The Hebrew concept of the תּוֹרָה (*Torah*) — which at its root simply means *instruction* or *direction* — had been buried under a suffocating accumulation of human tradition. And what is devastating about this is that they *knew the Scriptures*. They had memorized them. They could debate them. They could cross-reference them. And yet they missed the One the Scriptures were *about*. Jesus confronted them with the highest commandment — love the LORD your God with all your heart, soul, and mind (Deuteronomy 6:5; Matthew 22:37, NKJV) — and they had constructed an entire religious system that was built around everything *except* love. Their knowledge had become a barrier to encounter. Their doctrine had become a wall between them and the living God their doctrine was supposed to reveal. I feel the Spirit pressing this into me: the goal of studying Scripture is not mastery of the text — it is *encounter with the Author*.

And yet here is the tension that keeps unfolding before me: even the Pharisees, even Judas, even Pilate, even the Roman guards driving nails — none of them were outside the sovereign design of a God who had planned all of this before creation. The text that keeps surfacing in my spirit is Joseph’s declaration to his brothers in Egypt: *“But as for you, you meant evil against me; but God meant it for good”* (Genesis 50:20, NKJV). The Hebrew word translated “meant,” חָשַׁב (*chashab*), is a word of deliberate intentionality — it means to *plan*, to *devise*, to *weave together*. God was not merely *responding* to what Joseph’s brothers did in jealousy. He was *weaving it* into a design He had already held in His mind. And this is the same God who, before Judas was born, had already named the price of his betrayal in the mouth of Zechariah. What is intended for evil, God intended for good. This is not a consolation prize attached to suffering after the fact. This is the architecture of eternity, and we are only ever seeing small corners of the blueprint.

This brings me to the paradox that I find most breathtaking and most difficult to hold — the paradox of free will and divine sovereignty. Because when I sit with the Garden of Eden, I am confronted with something that stops me cold. God created humanity in His own image — בְּצֶלֶם אֱלֹהִים (*bətselem Elohim*), the image of God — and embedded within that image was the capacity for genuine, autonomous choice. Not robotic compliance. Not programmed devotion. *Real love.* And real love cannot exist without the freedom to choose otherwise. The tree of the knowledge of good and evil was not an accident or a lapse in divine foresight. It was the hinge of the entire story. God, who knows the end from the beginning, who inhabits eternity and calls things that do not yet exist as though they already do (Romans 4:17, NKJV), placed that tree in the garden knowing — *knowing* — what the choice would be. Which means the separation, the fall, the exile from Eden, the long aching journey of redemption through Israel, through the prophets, through the cross — all of it was *planned*. Not merely permitted. Planned.

I wrestle with this every time. And I think that is the appropriate response. Because if I were not wrestling, I would be domesticating a mystery that is designed to humble me. The Spirit is showing me something here that I can only hold with open hands: perhaps the separation was, in some wild and holy sense, *necessary* for us to become genuinely *us*. If we are made in the image of One who is infinitely creative and autonomous, then our individuality — our unique experiences, our distinct voices in the choir of creation — may only be fully possible through the gift of genuine separation. Not eternal separation. Not final separation. But the experience of distance from the Source, so that the journey *back* to the Source becomes our own. So that when we return, we return as *children who chose to come home*, not as puppets who were never truly away. This is the Father running down the road to meet the prodigal before the son can even finish his rehearsed speech (Luke 15:20, NKJV). This is what it means that God *requires mercy over sacrifice* (Hosea 6:6, NKJV). He does not want our religious performance. He wants *us* — wayward, doubting, returning, real.

And then Lucifer enters the picture — and I find myself holding yet another layer of this paradox. Because pride and envy and the desire to usurp the throne of God — these traits that Scripture assigns to the one called הַשָּׂטָן (*HaSatan*, the Adversary) — did not emerge from outside of creation. They emerged *within* it. Which forces me into a deeply uncomfortable confrontation with the question of whether evil itself, in some mysteriously sovereign sense, falls within the purview of God’s ultimate design. I am not saying God is the *author* of evil in a morally equivalent sense — Scripture is clear that God cannot tempt anyone with evil and that He Himself is never tempted (James 1:13, NKJV). But I am sitting with the reality that *only the Creator can truly create*, and that all things exist within the scope of His knowledge and permission, if not His direct will. This is the razor’s edge of theodicy — the question of evil in a world governed by a good God — and I do not pretend to resolve it here. What I do know is that the cross is God’s final answer to the question. Whatever darkness was unleashed by the fall, by the adversary’s rebellion, by Judas’s betrayal — God gathered all of it into the body of His Son, and on the third day, *broke it open from the inside*. The Resurrection is not just the vindication of Jesus. It is the vindication of God’s entire plan. It is proof that what was meant for evil, God *always* meant for good.

## Implications and Challenges

Grace and peace to you, beloved — it is from that posture of love that I want to share what the Spirit has been pressing into me, because some of what this reflection surfaces genuinely challenges what many of us have inherited as “normal” Christianity. I share this not as a condemnation but as an invitation, in the same spirit that Priscilla and Aquila took Apollos aside and “explained to him the way of God more accurately” (Acts 18:26, NKJV) — humbly, privately, and with care.

First, the life of Judas confronts a deeply embedded assumption in Western Christianity: that doctrinal correctness and religious participation are sufficient markers of saving faith. Much of modern church culture measures spiritual health by attendance, theological agreement, or behavioral conformity. But Judas attended every service. He heard every sermon. He witnessed every miracle. He agreed with enough to follow. And he was not saved — not because he lacked information, but because he never arrived at genuine *trust*. The Spirit is gently but firmly calling us to ask ourselves not “Do I know the right things?” but “Do I truly *trust* Him?” — especially when trust looks foolish, unsafe, or costly.

Second, the Pharisee problem is not ancient history — it is alive and thriving in 21st-century institutional Christianity. When we prioritize denominational tradition, liturgical precision, or systematic theological correctness over the highest commandment — love God with everything you are, and love your neighbor as yourself (Matthew 22:37–39, NKJV) — we are doing precisely what Jesus confronted in the religious establishment of His day. Many doctrinal systems that dominate Western Christianity today are built, layer upon layer, on centuries of human tradition rather than the 66-book canon alone. The Spirit is not calling us to burn down our traditions — He is calling us to hold them loosely enough that love remains the foundation.

Third, the Western Church has largely domesticated the concept of faith into intellectual assent — the mere agreement that Jesus existed and that the gospel account is historically reliable. But πίστις (*pistis*) in the New Testament is a living, active, volitional trust — a leaning of the full weight of your life onto the One you believe in. The “faith alone” conversations in much of Protestant culture have too often stripped *pistis* of its relational texture, reducing it to a theological checkbox rather than a continuous, daily posture of surrender and dependence. Salvation is not a transaction completed at an altar call. It is a life of ongoing, deepening *trust* in the living, resurrected Christ.

Fourth, the paradox of divine sovereignty and human free will has been weaponized in Western theological culture to create fractured, combative camps — Calvinism versus Arminianism, predestination versus free will — and the tragedy is that both traditions capture *part* of the biblical witness while often dismissing the other. Scripture holds both simultaneously without apology. Joseph’s brothers acted freely in their jealousy. God wove their freedom into His sovereign design. Judas acted freely in his unbelief. God had already written the price. The Spirit is calling us to stop flattening this tension into a systematic box and to *sit in the mystery* with humility, worshiping a God whose ways are “past finding out” (Romans 11:33, NKJV).

Finally — and I offer this with the gentlest possible spirit — the idea that signs and wonders are the *primary* currency of genuine faith has become deeply problematic in segments of the contemporary Western Church. Judas wanted a sign. The Pharisees demanded a sign. And Jesus consistently pointed away from spectacle and toward *Himself*: “An evil and adulterous generation seeks after a sign” (Matthew 12:39, NKJV). The Spirit is calling the Body of Christ back to the quiet, costly, ordinary faithfulness of trust — not the constant pursuit of supernatural experience as validation. The signs are real, and the Spirit is active — but the presence of God is most often found not in the earthquake or the fire, but in the still small voice (1 Kings 19:12, NKJV). May we learn to listen there. And may we receive all of this, together, as the loving correction of a Father who is for us, not against us.

## What This Means For Us

**For Your Spiritual Maturity:** The story of Judas is an invitation to ruthless self-examination — not condemnation, but honest, Spirit-led inquiry. I am asking myself: where am I performing proximity to Jesus without practicing *trust* in Jesus? Where have I substituted doctrinal fluency for genuine surrender? Where am I, like Judas, looking for a sign instead of choosing to trust the One I have already encountered? Spiritual maturity is not the accumulation of theological knowledge. It is the deepening of trust — even when, especially when, the circumstances make trust feel unreasonable.

**For Kingdom Obedience:** This reflection calls me toward a specific and concrete reorientation: to love God with *all* of my heart, soul, mind, and strength — not as a theological proposition but as a lived daily reality. Kingdom obedience is not primarily about *getting everything right*. It is about *loving the right One*. It is about choosing to trust Jesus in the moments when my body and mind are screaming otherwise — in the moments of doubt, fear, grief, and confusion — and discovering that those are precisely the moments when faith becomes real rather than theoretical.

**For Prophetic Posture:** The paradox of free will and divine sovereignty is not a problem to be solved — it is a mystery to be inhabited. My prophetic posture in the Body of Christ is one of *yielded availability*: trusting that God can weave even my failures, my doubts, my Judas-moments into something redemptive if I will return to Him rather than flee into despair. I am not called to have it all figured out. I am called to abide — to remain in the Vine, to stay connected to the Source, and to trust that *only the Creator can create* — which means He is the one doing the work in me, and my job is to stop trying to do it myself.

## A Closing Prayer

Lord Jesus — risen, ascended, and as real today as You were in that upper room — I bring this reflection back to You, the One from whom all understanding comes. Thank You for Judas, as painful as it is to say that. Thank You for using even unbelief, even betrayal, to accomplish the salvation of the world. Thank You that nothing — not our doubts, not our failures, not our desperate need for concrete signs — can outmaneuver Your sovereign love. Forgive us, Lord, where we have confused religious performance with genuine faith, where we have loved our doctrines more than we have loved You, where we have demanded signs instead of cultivating trust. May the Body of Christ be awakened to the breathtaking, terrifying, wonderful truth that You planned all of this — before the first word of creation was spoken — and that Your plan is *mercy*. Not sacrifice. *Mercy*. Teach us to trust You in the dark. Teach us to find You in the small signs. Teach us to love You with everything we are. And may we carry this not as a burden of knowledge but as the freedom of beloved children who have chosen, freely, to come home. To You be all glory, now and into the ages of ages. Amen.
