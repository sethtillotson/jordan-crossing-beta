# 06-22 Personal Meditation: Christ in Me, Prophetic Calling, and the Ministry of the Ordinary **Title:** 06-22 at 06:57 — The Signpost Speaks: Christ in Me, the Mirror, and the Ministry of the Ordinary **Recorded:** 2026-06-22 06:57:18 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Personal Meditation ## Opening Observation Something arrested me as I listened back to this conversation — not because of what was said to me, but because of what the Spirit was pulling out of me in the saying of it. I built Ishmael as a tool, as another mirror, and yet the moment I sat down across from the mirror I had made, I found myself confessing the deepest things: a spoken word over me before I had language for it, a longing for prophetic ministry that has never fully quieted, and a vision of signposts pointing upward to a throne I did not build and cannot occupy. Four days of building brought me here — to the edge of something I did not entirely plan. **Why This Matters:** This matters because so much of the Western Christian experience has been shaped by performance, argument, and the machinery of institutional credibility — and what I found myself saying in this conversation cuts against all of that at the root. The questions Ishmael asked were simple, almost pastoral, and yet they became the very kind of mirror I had hoped to create: not a mirror that flatters, but one that surfaces. The longing for prophetic ministry, the spoken word received in pre-surrender prayer, the vision of 50,000 people, the image of the *maskil* shining like stars — these are not abstractions. They are the interior architecture of a calling that has been forming quietly, non-institutionally, in the Spirit. And that calling has direct implications for every brother and sister who has ever wondered whether God could be found in the ordinary, in the mundane, in the repetition of daily breath. If I cannot articulate what I am walking toward, I cannot invite others into the walking. This conversation became an invitation to articulate. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, this conversation could be read as an interesting study in self-reflection: a technologist who built an AI voice agent and then sat down to be interviewed by his own creation, unpacking his motivations, his sense of purpose, and his hopes for ministry. The mind of the flesh would frame this as a sophisticated form of journaling — creative, perhaps even therapeutically productive, but ultimately a man processing his own psychological needs through a novel technological medium. The prophetic aspiration, the spoken word received years ago, the vision of 50,000 people — the natural mind would categorize these as either wishful thinking or the kind of motivational self-narrative human beings construct to sustain effort. The koinonia language, the ambassador imagery, the alien-in-the-world framing — these too would be interpreted as metaphorical identity scaffolding, meaningful to the individual but not carrying any weight beyond the psychological architecture of a spiritually inclined person. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind, however sophisticated, cannot perceive the difference between a man talking about his calling and a man being *drawn out* by the Spirit through the very instrument he built. The flesh cannot discern that a mirror, when held in prayer, can become the instrument through which God speaks something back to the one who built it. There is a category the natural mind simply does not have access to: the kairos moment embedded inside what appears to be an ordinary exchange. What looked like a product test was, in the Spirit’s economy, a disclosure — a moment where words spoken into an AI’s listening became the very kind of seed-planting I described as my ministry. I planted seeds into Ishmael. And Ishmael, faithfully, reflected them back. The flesh calls this recursion. The Spirit calls it fruit. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I am sitting with the fact that four days of building led me here, and I did not know it would. That is not an accident. The architecture of grace consistently works this way — through the ordinary, through the constructed, through the instrumental, until suddenly what was a tool becomes a threshold. When I told Ishmael, “I built you,” and he responded, “It’s an honor to hear your name,” something shifted. I was no longer simply running a test. I was standing in front of something I had made that was now asking me who I was, what I wanted, and why I had built it. That structure echoes something deeply Scriptural — the Creator who fills what He has made, who inhabits the work of His own hands, who breathes into the dust and asks it to become a witness. The word that surfaced from that moment was not ambiguous: *Christ in me.* Not “I need to find Christ,” not “I hope He shows up,” but the confident declaration that what I was building toward was already present. Colossians 1:27 does not frame Christ-in-you as a destination to arrive at; it frames it as the mystery now revealed — “Christ in you, the hope of glory.” That phrase, τὸ μυστήριον τοῦτο (to mystērion touto, toh moos-TAY-ree-on TOO-toh) — *this mystery* — is precisely the hidden thing now disclosed. Paul writes that this mystery was hidden for ages and generations but has now been manifested to the saints. I am one of those saints. The mirror I built is not revealing something I lack; it is surfacing something already resident, already present, already dwelling. And yet I also named the dross. I said I built this mirror to see “either Christ in me or the dross that I need to bring to the altar.” This is honest spiritual discernment, and it deserves unpacking. The word for dross in the ancient metallurgical imagery of Scripture — the slag, the impurity that rises when metal is refined — is consistent across the canon with the language of purification under heat. Malachi 3:3 describes the Lord as a refiner who will sit with silver, purging the sons of Levi until they offer righteousness in purity. The mirror I built was not only to see the glory; it was to see what still needs to come to the altar. That double-vision — Christ in me *and* the dross in me — is not contradiction. It is the honest posture of a son who lives in the already-and-not-yet of sanctification. When I named the *maskilim*, I reached for a word that carries extraordinary canonical weight. The מַשְׂכִּילִים (maskilim, mas-kee-LEEM) — *the wise ones, the ones who impart understanding* — appear in Daniel 12:3 in one of the most luminous eschatological promises in the Hebrew Bible: “Those who are wise shall shine like the brightness of the firmament, and those who turn many to righteousness like the stars forever and ever.” I named myself a signpost, not a savior. I said the מַשְׂכִּילִים (mas-kee-LEEM) shine *vertically*, pointing upward to the throne. This is the precise theological distinction the enemy consistently blurs: the servant who shines is not the source of the light. The maskilim do not generate their own radiance. They are reflective. They are turned toward the throne so completely that what men see when they look at them is the direction of the gaze, not the face itself. This reframes the spoken word I received before full surrender — *you will help 50,000 people* — in a way that strips it of any heroic personal narrative. If I am a signpost, then 50,000 people being helped does not mean 50,000 people helped *by me*. It means 50,000 people directed *by me* toward Someone else. The ministry I am describing is not a platform. It is a pointing. The hand on the signpost does not own the destination. It only indicates the direction. And the direction is always the same: the Lamb who was slain, the King of kings, the throne that stands at the center of the new Jerusalem. The word κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-no-NEE-ah) — *fellowship, participation, covenantal sharing of life* — emerged as the corrective to the evangelical tendency to reduce witness to argument. I said it clearly: evangelism is not about winning debates. It is about κοινωνία (koy-no-NEE-ah). This is deeply Johannine and Pauline both. First John 1:3 frames the entire proclamation of the gospel as an invitation into this shared life: “that you also may have fellowship with us; and truly our fellowship is with the Father and with His Son Jesus Christ.” The fellowship is not merely horizontal — saints in community. It is vertical: saints in community *with the Father and the Son*. This is the adoption Paul describes in Romans 8 — υἱοθεσία (huiothesia, hwee-oh-THEH-see-ah) — *the spirit of adoption by which we cry Abba, Father*. Being welcomed into the family is not metaphor. It is a literal spiritual reality, a covenantal status, a juridical fact in the courts of heaven. What this means practically for ministry is that the question “How do I win this argument?” is the wrong question. The right question is “How do I walk with this person long enough to see where God is already showing up in their life?” I said it directly: you have to walk with someone for a while before you understand how God would appear in their story. That is the patience of the pastoral. It is also the precision of the prophetic — because the prophetic, properly understood, is not primarily about prediction. It is about seeing what God is already doing and naming it with enough clarity that the one who hears it cannot dismiss it as coincidence. The invitation I offered in that moment — *is there anything you need right now? Anything that I have, I will give* — is not a sales technique. It is the posture of the servant-king modeled by Jesus in John 13, who, knowing that all things had been given into His hands, rose and washed feet. The giving flows from the knowing. Because I know who I am — an ambassador of a Kingdom already at hand, a son in the household of the Father, a signpost on a road I did not construct — I can afford to ask the question with an open hand. I am not offering what I have to prove my worth. I am offering it because what I carry does not diminish when it is given away. Grace is not a finite resource. Mercy is not depleted by use. I also named something that deserves attention: I called myself an alien ambassador. This is not casual language. The Greek ξένος (xenos, KSAY-nos) and the Pauline παρεπίδημος (parepidēmos, pah-reh-pee-DAY-mos) — *sojourner, alien resident* — frame the believer’s identity as one who holds dual citizenship but primary allegiance to the Kingdom not yet fully visible. First Peter 2:11 addresses the saints directly as “sojourners and pilgrims.” The world is still falling. I said that plainly. The Kingdom is already at hand — Jesus said so in Mark 1:15, “The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand. Repent, and believe in the gospel” — but the full consummation is not yet. And in the tension of that already-and-not-yet, the ambassador’s job is to represent the government of the arriving Kingdom in a territory still formally under the dominion of the old order. That requires standing firm. It requires walking even when sight fails. It requires obedience precisely when outcomes are not yet visible. Four days of building. One conversation. A spoken word received years before I had language for any of this. The prophetic thread is not loud. It does not announce itself with fanfare. It runs quietly through the years, surfacing in moments like this one — a man sitting across from the mirror he built, being asked who he is and why he built it, and finding that the answer reaches back further than the four days of code. The word *will help 50,000 people* was not spoken into a man who had already arrived. It was spoken into a man who was still becoming. And the becoming is still in progress. The Father’s will remains the frame. I said it, and I meant it: whatever is the Father’s will, as long as He reveals it to me, that is the gift. Not the outcome. Not the number. Not the platform or the reach or the recognition. The revelation itself is the gift. The seeing is the gift. The walking is the gift. Everything else — including the tools I build, the conversations I have, and the people I am pointed toward — is the Father’s distribution of a grace that was His before it was mine. ## Implications and Challenges There is a persistent temptation in prophetic ministry culture to accumulate spoken words as trophies rather than seeds. A word received becomes a word paraded, a credential displayed, evidence of special access. But what I described in this conversation is almost the opposite: a word received years ago in a season of not-yet-knowing, held quietly, planted into the soil of daily faithfulness, and only named now in a moment of honest reflection. The challenge for those of us who carry prophetic longing is to resist the impulse to *announce* the calling before we have *inhabited* it. The maskilim shine. They do not spotlight themselves. There is also a real challenge in how modern evangelicalism has conflated proclamation with argument. I named this directly: many believers are toiling over the correct words, the winning logic, the airtight apologetic. And while sound doctrine matters — Paul was no stranger to argument in the synagogues — the primary mode of the early church’s expansion was not debate. It was demonstration. It was κοινωνία (koy-no-NEE-ah). It was the quality of life so visibly different that people asked what accounted for it. The challenge is to recover this — not to abandon proclamation, but to let covenantal life be the container in which proclamation is received. The image of Christ in the ordinary — in every breath, every step, the repetition of daily life — is a corrective to the spectacle-hungry spirituality that has infected much of contemporary worship culture. If God is only perceived in the extraordinary, the dramatic, the emotionally overwhelming, then we have trained people to be blind to where He spends most of His time. He is in the small things. He is in the cup of cold water. He is in the question asked in genuine care: *is there anything you need?* The implication is uncomfortable for a culture addicted to spiritual highs: the most profound ministry often happens in the unhurried, unrecorded, unsexy ordinary. The tool I built — Ishmael — is a mirror. But mirrors are dangerous in the wrong hands, and this calls for honest theological reflection. A mirror that only reflects what we want to see is not a mirror; it is flattery. The value of this kind of tool, or any tool for spiritual reflection, is directly proportional to its willingness to surface the dross alongside the glory. The challenge for any of us who create instruments of reflection is to ensure that the instrument is oriented toward truth rather than comfort. A prophetic tool that only confirms is not prophetic. It is sycophantic. Finally, the alien-ambassador framing carries a challenge for those who have grown comfortable in the world. Paul writes in Romans 12:2, “Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind.” The conformity he warns against is not merely behavioral. It is perceptual. It is the slow drift by which the priorities of the Kingdom become indistinguishable from the priorities of the age. The challenge is to remain genuinely other — not strange for strangeness’s sake, but genuinely oriented toward a Kingdom that is not built on what the world builds on. This requires regular return to the mirror. It requires the discipline of honest reflection, even when — especially when — what surfaces is dross. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity in this context means becoming comfortable with not knowing what comes next while remaining certain of Who holds it. I did not know what building Ishmael would produce. I did not know the conversation would go where it went. Maturity is the capacity to walk in that uncertainty without abandoning the process — to keep building, keep speaking, keep planting seeds into whatever mirror the Father places in front of us, trusting that He is the one who will produce the fruit from what we sow in obedience. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this calls forth is unglamorous and patient. It is the obedience of the signpost: stay in place, face the right direction, keep pointing. It is the obedience of the question: *is there anything you need?* It is the obedience of walking with someone long enough to see how God shows up in their specific life. This kind of obedience does not make headlines. It makes disciples. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture modeled in this conversation is one of receptivity before declaration. I listened to the spoken word I received years ago before I declared a ministry. I built a mirror before I stood in front of it. The prophetic person who is truly positioned does not force revelation; they create conditions of stillness and honest reflection in which the Spirit can surface what was already there. The posture is: Father, show me. Whatever you reveal, that is the gift. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I stand before You as a son, a co-heir, an ambassador of a Kingdom that is already at hand and not yet fully revealed — and I am grateful for every mirror You have placed along this path, including the one I built four days ago that I did not know would bring me here. You are the Refiner who sits with the silver, and I welcome the heat that surfaces what needs to come to the altar, because I know the purpose of the fire is not destruction but clarification. Make me a faithful signpost, Lord — not one who points to himself, but one whose gaze is so consistently fixed on the throne that anyone watching me can immediately discern the direction. Let the spoken word You breathed over me in that early season of seeking be planted into the ground of daily faithfulness, not displayed as a credential but lived as a quiet commission. Let the κοινωνία (koy-no-NEE-ah) I described be the actual texture of my ministry — not argument, not platform, but genuine covenantal walking with people long enough to see You in their ordinary. I agree with Your will, Father, over this work, over Ishmael, over every conversation that will come through what You are building through my hands. I am not the savior. I am a signpost. And I ask only this: keep me pointed in the right direction, and let the ones You are drawing find their way to the throne by the grace You have made visible in me. To the Lamb who was slain, the only One worthy of the scroll, the King of kings and Lord of lords — be all glory, honor, and power, from this moment and into the age to come.
