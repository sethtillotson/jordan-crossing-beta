# 04-25 Call Reflection: Spiritual Weariness, Discouragement, and Renewal — Samuel’s Late-Night Conversation **Title:** 04-25 at 06:38 — When Weariness Speaks Louder Than the Will of God **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture), followed by an appended teaching segment from a third voice (Apostle Cecil) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother Samuel work through the weight of the night — the grief, the exhaustion, the quiet erosion of his appetite for the things of God — something in me recognized a spiritual condition that goes far deeper than circumstance. What began as a late-night call between brothers became an unexpected classroom, and then Apostle Cecil’s voice arrived like a precise surgical instrument, naming what the Spirit had already been pressing upon me through the conversation itself. The diagnosis was weariness — not laziness, not rebellion, not apostasy — but something subtler and more dangerous: the slow draining of strength that happens when expectation meets the silence of unmet reality. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Church in our generation is full of people who have not left the faith so much as they have grown tired inside of it. They are still present in body, still carrying the vocabulary of devotion, but the fire has banked low — and they cannot always say why. Samuel’s honesty in that conversation, his admission that he is angrier, less engaged, skipping church on his one day off not out of rebellion but out of sheer depletion, is not an isolated confession. It is a window into a vast interior landscape that many of God’s people are navigating alone, without language for it and without anyone to name it rightly. If the enemy can reframe weariness as divine redirection — if he can get a man to read his exhaustion as God telling him to break camp — then he can displace an entire generation of servants from their God-ordained assignments without ever raising his voice. The stakes of this conversation are not small. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the vantage point of natural human reasoning, what I heard in Samuel’s voice is entirely predictable and entirely manageable: a young man working grueling twelve-hour shifts, separated from a life he loved, processing the residual grief of a relationship that ended, dealing with the cumulative toll of disrupted sleep, social isolation, and a rhythm of life that does not accommodate recovery. Psychology would call this burnout. Sociology would call it structural deprivation — the absence of community, meaningful leisure, and relational continuity. A career counselor would suggest better work-life balance, perhaps a change of environment. A therapist might explore the attachment wound underneath the sleeplessness, the way that years of close proximity to another person can rewire the nervous system so thoroughly that solitude itself becomes a kind of chronic low-grade suffering. All of this analysis is not wrong, exactly. It is observationally accurate. It correctly identifies the presenting symptoms. And yet it remains entirely on the surface of what is actually happening, because it has no category for the invisible — for the fact that behind the fatigue and the grief and the occupational depletion, there is a named spiritual entity at work, intentional and patient, whose goal is not merely Samuel’s discomfort but his displacement. **The Limitation:** The natural mind is capable of identifying pain but not its spiritual architecture. It can describe what is happening to a man without being able to perceive who is orchestrating it or why. This is the precise blind spot that makes weariness such a sophisticated weapon: because it presents itself through entirely natural and sympathetic means — through real exhaustion, real grief, real unmet longing — it bypasses the defenses that might otherwise alert a man to spiritual danger. A frontal assault of temptation can be resisted. But a slow, patient draining of vitality, administered through circumstances that seem utterly ordinary, is far harder to discern from within the experience itself. The natural mind, even a well-intentioned one, will diagnose the condition and prescribe natural remedies, never asking the more penetrating question: *why now, why this, and what is being protected by keeping this servant too tired to pray?* ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I am sitting with the texture of that phone call, and what strikes me first is the timing. Samuel called in the middle of my night — my early morning, actually, three-thirty a.m., coming in from a late store run before a predawn shift of my own. Neither of us was operating from a position of surplus. And yet the Spirit arranged that exchange with the kind of precision that does not belong to coincidence. Two men, each carrying their own weight of sleeplessness and stretched capacity, finding each other at the exact moment when one of them needed to be seen — and through the hearing, something was illuminated that neither of us had fully named yet. The word that arrested me in the middle of our conversation was one I felt pressed to speak even before Apostle Cecil’s teaching arrived to frame it formally. I said it almost instinctively: *kenosis.* The Greek κένωσις (kenōsis, keh-NO-sis) — the self-emptying, the voluntary pouring out, the act of making room. I used it in a pastoral, almost offhand way, trying to redirect Samuel’s focus from the strategy of forgetting — “the harder I try not to think about it, the more I think about it” — toward the principle of displacement. You do not solve the problem of an empty space by focusing on the emptiness. You solve it by filling it with something of greater weight and greater glory. But as I sit with that word now, I realize it carries far more theological freight than I unpacked in the moment, and the Spirit seems intent on pressing me deeper into it. κένωσις (keh-NO-sis) appears in its verbal form in Philippians 2:7 — Christ “made Himself of no reputation,” or more literally, *emptied Himself* (ἐκένωσεν, ekénōsen). The mystery of the Incarnation is that the Son of God voluntarily evacuated the independent exercise of divine prerogative not because He was forced to, not because the emptiness itself was the goal, but because the emptying was the *precondition* for the filling — the indwelling of full humanity, the vessel through which redemption would flow. The emptying was purposeful. It was directional. It was oriented toward something the fullness alone could not accomplish. And I notice, sitting with Samuel’s situation, that his weariness represents a different kind of emptying: not a voluntary kenosis in the image of Christ, but an involuntary draining — a hemorrhage of strength that has no redemptive architecture around it yet, because it has not yet been named, consecrated, or surrendered into the hands of God. Apostle Cecil’s teaching landed on me with the weight of a prophetic word precisely because it provided that architecture. He said it plainly: *weariness is a spirit.* Not merely a condition. Not simply a physical state. A spirit — something with agency, with intention, with a specific and targeted assignment. And he named its mechanism with devastating precision: *when reality does not match expectation, you are discouraged.* I have been turning this over since I heard it, because it reveals something about the interior geography of Samuel’s struggle that goes beyond pastoral sympathy. Samuel came to his job believing it would bring him closer to God. That expectation was not foolish — it was faith. But when the reality of twelve-hour shifts, isolation, and occupational exhaustion contradicted that expectation, the unmet gap became an open door. The spirit of discouragement does not always announce itself. It enters through disappointment. It consolidates through isolation. And it speaks in the first person: *I am tired. I don’t want to read. I don’t want to go to church.* It borrows the voice of the man it is attacking, which is precisely what makes it so difficult to detect and resist. The book of Isaiah provides the counter-word, and it is no accident that Apostle Cecil reached for it. Isaiah 40:31 — “But those who wait on the LORD shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings like eagles, they shall run and not be weary, they shall walk and not faint.” The Hebrew word here for *wait* is קָוָה (qavah, kah-VAH), and it does not mean passive endurance. It means to bind together, to twist into a cord, to be in a posture of active, expectant tension — like a rope drawn taut. Waiting on the LORD in the biblical idiom is not resignation. It is not simply surviving the season. It is the deliberate, active orientation of one’s whole self toward God in the midst of exhaustion, trusting that the renewal is not something you generate but something He releases into the one who remains positioned before Him. Samuel has been trying to function without this tether, and the rope has gone slack. The strength has drained not because God has abandoned his assignment but because the mechanism of renewal — the patient, active, expectant qavah — has been crowded out by the very exhaustion the enemy engineered to crowd it out. Something else arrests me in what Apostle Cecil said, and it is this: *discouragement can become prophetic direction — in quotes.* He was careful to add those qualifying words, and I think the Spirit put them there deliberately. The danger is not merely that a discouraged man feels bad. The danger is that a discouraged man begins to *interpret* his discouragement as revelation. He begins to read his weariness as a sign that he is in the wrong place, the wrong job, the wrong relationship, the wrong calling. And he may be. But he also may not be. The question is whether the interpretation is coming from the Spirit of God or from the spirit of discouragement wearing the voice of divine guidance. Samuel said himself: “It’s pulling me farther, not closer.” That is a real observation. But Apostle Cecil’s word introduces a crucial pause: *the fact that you have lost inspiration for something does not mean it’s not right. It may be you that’s not right.* The weariness itself is not the oracle. The weariness is the symptom. The question is whether the symptom belongs to an exit or to a battle that must be fought and won on the very ground where you currently stand. I think about the prophet Elijah under the juniper tree in 1 Kings 19. He had just come off one of the most dramatic displays of divine power in the entire Old Testament — fire from heaven, four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal defeated, a nation confronted with the living God. And then Jezebel sent a single threatening message, and the man who had called down fire ran a day’s journey into the wilderness, sat under a tree, and asked God to let him die. “It is enough.” The language of a man who is finished — not by sin, but by exhaustion. By the unmatched gap between what he expected after Carmel and what he actually encountered: more threat, more pursuit, more isolation. The spirit of discouragement does not wait for your worst moments. It comes for you after your greatest ones, when your reserves are lowest and the gap between expectation and reality is widest. And what did God do? He did not rebuke Elijah. He sent an angel — twice — with food and water, and said: “Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you.” The first response to weariness in the economy of God is not always a vision or a word. Sometimes it is bread. Sometimes it is rest. Sometimes it is the quiet, merciful acknowledgment: *the road ahead is real, the weight is real, and I have not forgotten where you are.* And yet — and this is where the word must sharpen — God did not let Elijah stay under the tree. He rose him up, fed him, and sent him forty days deeper into the wilderness, toward Horeb, the mountain of God, where the voice came: “What are you doing here, Elijah?” It was not a condemnation. It was a diagnostic question. What are you doing here? Are you here because I sent you, or are you here because weariness has become your compass? The same question is alive in Samuel’s situation, and I dare not pretend to know its answer for him. But I know the question must be asked — not by me, but by the Spirit, in the secret place, when Samuel is still enough to hear it. I notice something significant in the way our conversation moved. Samuel said: “Every aspect of my life is being pulled away.” That is the testimony of a man whose armor is beginning to separate at the joints. Not a catastrophic collapse, but a slow, systematic loosening. Ephesians 6 describes the full armor of God, and I am arrested by the girdle — the ζώνη (zōnē, ZOH-nay), the belt of truth. In Roman military dress, the belt was the first piece of armor put on because it held everything else together: the breastplate, the sword, the whole apparatus of battle readiness. When a soldier’s belt loosened, everything else began to shift and become unreliable. Samuel’s belt of truth is being attacked — not through outright heresy, but through the more subtle lie that his weariness is permanent, that the distance from God is structural rather than spiritual, that missing church on his day off is simply a reasonable response to exhaustion rather than a concession to a spirit that is actively working to widen the gap between him and the gathered Body of Christ. What I said to Samuel in the moment — that the solidification of the kenotic life comes not through the discipline of emptying but through the indwelling of something greater — was incomplete in the way all pastoral words spoken in the early hours of the morning tend to be incomplete. The fuller word is this: the emptying that Samuel is experiencing has not yet been surrendered to God. It is being experienced passively rather than offered actively. There is a difference between being drained and being poured out. Paul, writing from prison, could say: “I am already being poured out as a drink offering” (2 Timothy 4:6). The σπένδομαι (spendomai, SPEN-doh-my) — the being poured out as a libation — is not the same as being depleted by an enemy. One is an act of worship. The other is an act of war against you. The call to Samuel, and perhaps to every weary saint, is to take the very exhaustion that has been passively accumulating and place it on the altar — to transform the involuntary draining into a conscious, consecrated offering, and in doing so, to change the nature of the experience from defeat into sacrifice. I also want to name the grief underneath the weariness, because it is real and it matters. Samuel is carrying the weight of a life he misses — Tennessee, proximity, belonging, the companionship of someone with whom he spent five years at close range. That grief is legitimate. It is not weakness. And I know something of it, because I said aloud in that call that I carried similar residue — that part of the reason I don’t sleep well may trace back to a season of similar transition, when sleeping alone after years of constant presence became its own kind of suffering. Grief and discouragement are not the same spirit, but they travel together, and the enemy is not above using one to amplify the other. The way through grief is not suppression — Samuel is right that trying not to think about it only fixes it more firmly in the mind, like the pink elephant in the room. The way through is lamentation. The Psalms give us this language. David, weeping in exile. Jeremiah, crying in the ruins of Jerusalem. The biblical tradition has a category for naming loss before God without resolving it artificially — for sitting in the grief and speaking it upward until it becomes prayer, until the lament itself becomes a form of communion with the One who is acquainted with grief and knows sorrow from the inside out. The closing word that I spoke over Samuel before he had to return to his shift was brief — perhaps too brief — but I believe it was accurate: *Don’t be so discouraged about weariness. It is a manifestation of the spirit of discouragement. He’ll get over it.* The pronoun matters. “He” — not in dismissal of Samuel’s pain, but in the prophetic confidence that the spirit of discouragement does not have the last word over a man who belongs to God. The enemy’s timeline is not the divine timeline. Weariness is not a verdict. It is a condition — temporary, navigable, and already under the authority of the One who makes the weary walk and the exhausted mount up on wings. ## Implications and Challenges Let me begin here: the Church is not immune to weariness, and pretending otherwise does not honor the saints who carry it. To suppress the honest testimony of exhaustion in favor of performed enthusiasm is not faith — it is a kind of spiritual gaslighting that drives people further from honest encounter with God. Samuel’s willingness to say *I think I’m depressed* in a conversation with a brother is not a spiritual failure. It is a form of courage that the Body of Christ desperately needs to recover. We must create conditions where this kind of honesty is not punished or papered over with quick Scripture quotes, but met with genuine pastoral presence and Spirit-led discernment. At the same time, the modern Western Christian habit of interpreting exhaustion as guidance is deeply dangerous and must be named with love. We have been conditioned by a therapeutic culture that treats feelings as authoritative — if something feels wrong, it is wrong; if something is draining, it must be discarded. But the Kingdom does not always work this way. The cross was draining. The wilderness was draining. The prison where Paul wrote letters of joy was draining. Weariness inside an assignment is not automatically a sign that the assignment is over. The discernment question — “Is this the Spirit directing me to move, or is this the spirit of discouragement trying to move me?” — requires more than introspection. It requires the counsel of trusted fathers and brothers, the witness of Scripture, and extended seasons of undistracted waiting before God. There is a particular challenge embedded in Samuel’s testimony that must be addressed directly: the structural conditions of modern labor — long hours, social isolation, disrupted rest cycles — are genuinely hostile to spiritual formation. This is not a private problem; it is a systemic one. And yet the answer is not simply to change jobs, as though the spirit of weariness cannot follow a man to his next assignment. The interior renewal — the qavah, the active, taut expectancy before God — must precede and undergird any external change, or the same depleting dynamic will simply reassemble itself in a new location. Running from weariness without addressing its spiritual root is not escape. It is relocation. The misidentification of weariness as divine redirection is perhaps the most subtle and costly error in this entire territory. Apostle Cecil named it carefully: discouragement *can* become prophetic direction *in quotes*. That parenthetical is doing a great deal of work. It is saying: be epistemically humble about what your fatigue is telling you. Your fatigue is real data, but it is not infallible revelation. The same Spirit who led Israel out of Egypt also led them through forty years of wilderness — not as punishment for weariness but as formation through it. The place of testing is often also the place of meeting. To abandon your post because the post is hard is to potentially forfeit the encounter that the hardness was designed to produce. Finally, I want to address the subtle displacement of gathered worship that weariness engineers. Samuel said he doesn’t want to go to church on his day off. I understand it. I have felt the pull of that reasoning. But the writer of Hebrews saw it coming two thousand years ago and warned against it not as a legal obligation but as a matter of survival: “not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together, as is the manner of some, but exhorting one another, and so much the more as you see the Day approaching” (Hebrews 10:25). The gathering of the Body is not optional enrichment for those who have the energy. It is a covenantal lifeline — a place where the strength of the whole sustains the weakness of the one. The man who needs the gathered Body most is precisely the man who feels least like going. The enemy knows this. That is why the spirit of discouragement’s most reliable tactic is to make isolation feel like reasonable self-care. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity in the Spirit requires that we develop a vocabulary for distinguishing between divine redirection and demonic discouragement. These two things can feel remarkably similar from the inside. The mature saint does not simply trust his feelings about his assignment; he submits those feelings to Scripture, to trusted spiritual authority, and to extended, patient waiting before God — refusing to make permanent decisions from temporary conditions. Maturity also means learning to name the spirits that are at work rather than simply managing the symptoms they produce. Samuel needs not only compassion but also the kind of naming that Apostle Cecil’s word provides: this is a spirit, it has an agenda, and it can be resisted. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this calls forth is the obedience of *staying* — staying in prayer when prayer feels dry, staying in the Word when the Word feels distant, staying in community when community feels expensive, staying in the assignment when the assignment feels impossible. This is not stoicism. It is trust — the active, rope-taut trust that the God who assigned you to this field is also present in it, that the renewal is on its way, and that the weariness you are feeling is neither your identity nor your destiny. Kingdom obedience in a season of weariness also means allowing brothers to speak into the condition, as Samuel did in that call — not suffering alone, not performing wellness, but opening the interior struggle to the light of covenant relationship. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this demands is one of patient, sober discernment. Before declaring “God is moving me,” the prophetically attuned saint asks: “Is this the Spirit, or is this depletion speaking?” The prophetic community must recover the practice of testing the spirits that speak through exhaustion, grief, and unmet expectation, rather than simply validating every impression that arrives in an emotionally charged moment. And the prophetic community must also recover the ministry of presence — the willingness to be present to a weary brother at three-thirty in the morning, not with all the answers, but with the Word, with honest engagement, and with the quiet confidence that the God who watches over Israel neither slumbers nor sleeps, even when His servants desperately need to. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who stands in the gap between heaven and earth, as a co-heir of the Kingdom You have established in Your Son, as a servant of the Word — and I make agreement with Your will over my brother Samuel and over every weary saint who finds themselves in the territory this word has described. I thank You that You are not alarmed by our exhaustion. I thank You that You are the God who came to Elijah under the juniper tree with bread and water before You came with vision and instruction — that You meet us in our depletion before You call us to our next mile. I declare, in covenantal agreement with the testimony of Your Word, that the spirit of discouragement does not have dominion over the children of the Most High God. I come against the assignment of weariness that has been set against Your servant — the slow, strategic draining, the narrowing of appetite, the misidentification of exhaustion as Your voice commanding retreat — and I declare it broken, in the authority of the name of Jesus, whose κένωσις (keh-NO-sis) purchased for us not emptiness but fullness, not depletion but abundant life. Renew the qavah in Samuel’s spirit — that active, expectant, rope-taut waiting upon You that is the precondition for the soaring You have promised. Draw him back to the gathered Body, to the Word, to the secret place. Let the weariness that the enemy intended as a weapon be transformed in Your hands into an altar — a place of consecrated offering that becomes the ground of deeper encounter with You. And Lord, keep me sober and watchful, that I may be found faithful in the middle-of-the-night phone calls, in the early morning conversations, in the small moments where brothers need brothers — because this is where the Kingdom moves, quietly and without ceremony, in the darkness before the dawn. To You be all glory, all dominion, all the unshared honor due Your name, from this moment through all ages.
