# 05-27 Call Reflection: Identity in Christ, Grief, and Kingdom Formation **Title:** 05-27 at 16:57 — When You Cannot Hate the One You Loved: Identity, Pain, and the Joy of the Battle **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation (Call Reflection) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother Samuel work through the raw theology of a film about divorce, something arrested me — not the cinematography, not the drama, but the simple, devastating truth that surfaced in that conversation: you cannot hate the one you loved; you can only grieve that you can no longer love them the way you once did. That grief is real, and I have carried it. What I am seeing now, sitting with the weight of that exchange, is that grief of this kind is not a spiritual failure — it is the pressure that can either crush a man or press him deeper into the identity that Christ alone can anchor. The conversation moved through heartbreak, Kingdom purpose, embodied identity, and finally a brother needing rest — and through all of it, the Spirit was threading something together that I want to follow carefully here. **Why This Matters:** The Church often does not know what to do with men who are grieving. We are quick to offer resolution and slow to honor the long, unglamorous journey through pain that actually produces the character Scripture describes. When two men — both marked by loss, both pressing toward something they are only beginning to name — speak honestly in the dark, that conversation is not merely therapeutic. It is potentially formative. It touches the question of who we are when everything we thought we wanted has either dissolved or failed to arrive, and it presses toward the only answer that holds: identity in Christ, not constructed from circumstances but received as inheritance. If that question goes unanswered — or worse, if it gets answered with religious cliché rather than genuine encounter — then an entire generation of men will remain functional orphans, living without the anchor the Kingdom actually provides. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, what Brother Samuel and I shared sounds like two young men doing exactly what the culture encourages: processing emotion through honest conversation, finding resonance in art and media, validating each other’s pain, and moving toward some version of self-discovery. The line from the film — *you can’t hate someone you just loved, you can only hate the fact that you can’t love them anymore* — reads, to the secular mind, as simply good emotional intelligence. The world would nod along: yes, anger is a secondary emotion masking grief; yes, you are not a bad person for loving someone who hurt you; yes, the path forward is self-knowledge and self-actualization. The conversations about the archive, about teaching methods, about Volume Two — those too would be framed psychologically as a man constructing narrative meaning out of personal experience, which is entirely within the domain of what natural human wisdom affirms as healthy. **The Limitation:** But here is where natural reasoning reaches its ceiling. The natural mind can identify that grief is complicated and that anger and love can coexist — but it has no framework for what to *do* with that complexity that does not ultimately circle back to the self. The secular therapeutic model says: *understand yourself better, set better boundaries, choose healthier patterns*. What it cannot offer is the reconciliation of the outer dissolution with an inner identity that remains unshaken — not because the man has achieved psychological mastery, but because he has received it as a gift in Christ. The natural mind can observe that Brother Samuel does not know what he wants or where he is going; it cannot tell him that the very disorientation he is living in is the fertile ground of Kingdom formation — that not-knowing, when brought before the Lord rather than suppressed or medicated, is precisely where the Spirit begins to speak. That movement — from confusion to surrender to anchored identity — is not accessible to the reasoning faculties alone. It requires revelation. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I want to begin where the conversation began — with the line about hatred. “You can’t hate someone you just loved. You can only hate the fact that you can’t love them anymore.” I said to my brother that I carried that for a long time. I still don’t hate my ex-wife. And as I sit with that now, I recognize that the absence of hatred in the wreckage of love is not a psychological phenomenon — it is a trace of the divine image, however marred. We were made in the image of One who, even when His people broke covenant repeatedly and catastrophically, refused to simply abandon them. The prophet Hosea is commanded not merely to understand God’s grief over Israel but to *embody* it — to live it in his own marriage, to love a wife who left him, to pursue her again. The hatred that never comes in that story is not weakness. It is covenant memory made flesh. What my brother and I were circling, without fully naming it, is the distinction between anger and hatred that Paul himself draws when he writes in Ephesians 4:26, “Be angry, and do not sin.” The Greek here is pointed: ὀργίζεσθε (orgizesthe, or-GEE-zes-the) — from ὀργή (orgē, or-GAY), which carries the sense of swelling, of an internal pressure that rises. Anger is a legitimate movement of the inner man toward injustice, toward what was taken, toward what was broken. The command is not to extinguish it but to refuse to let it calcify into something darker before the sun sets. Hatred is what happens when anger is held past the threshold of grace. What I experienced — what I believe my brother is still navigating — is ὀργή (or-GAY) that needs to be brought before the Father rather than resolved through sheer emotional management. And the pain is real. I said it plainly in that call: I didn’t hate her. I just wanted anything to happen other than what was happening. That sentence, unremarkable on its surface, contains a theology. It is the language of Gethsemane. “Father, if it is Your will, take this cup away from Me; nevertheless not My will, but Yours, be done” (Luke 22:42). The Lord of Glory, in the hour of His greatest agony, did not pretend the cup did not exist. He named it. He named His desire for it to be removed. And then He yielded. What I am realizing is that my own season of dissolution was not a detour from formation — it *was* the formation. The cup I wanted removed was the very thing being pressed into the shape of a vessel. Brother Samuel said something that strikes me with fresh force: “I’ve come to learn I’ve got no f—ing idea.” He does not know where he wants to be, who he wants to be with, or what he wants to do. He only knows he does not want to feel the way he feels. That is honest. And it is, I would argue, a spiritually significant threshold. The Psalms are full of this cry — the disorientation of the soul that once knew its ground and now finds the ground has shifted. Psalm 73 opens with Asaph nearly slipping entirely before he enters the sanctuary of God and receives understanding. The sanctuary is the key: the confusion does not resolve through introspection. It resolves through presence. When the disoriented soul steps into the presence of God, perspective floods back in. “My flesh and my heart fail; but God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever” (Psalm 73:26). Asaph does not arrive at certainty through self-discovery. He arrives through encounter. This is what I tried to say to my brother on the call, and I want to say it more fully now: maybe the assignment is to learn how to embody the identity of being *in* Christ so completely, so clearly, that the disorientation loses its power to define you. The New Testament word that keeps pressing on me here is the language of the epistles around καινὴ κτίσις (kainē ktisis, kai-NAY KTEE-sis) — new creation. Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 5:17, “Therefore, if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation; old things have passed away; behold, all things have become new.” The word καινή (KAI-nay) does not mean *newer* as in a revision or upgrade. It means *new in kind* — a category of existence that did not previously exist. The identity that is available in Christ is not a better version of the self that went through divorce or heartbreak. It is a fundamentally different kind of being. The problem is that we spend enormous energy trying to renovate the old self when what we have been given is a new one. I also mentioned in the conversation something the Spirit had been teaching me through hours of Apostle Orokpo Michael’s ministry — specifically the two teaching modes of Jesus. This deserves deeper reflection. Jesus taught the crowds in parables and taught His disciples in explanation. The parable is not a dumbing-down of truth; it is an *invitation*. It creates a threshold. It gives enough to stir hunger without satisfying it prematurely. The person who is merely curious can walk away with an interesting story. The person who is genuinely seeking will press in and ask, “What does this mean?” And then the fuller revelation opens. I wrote Volume Two in that spirit — naming lived experiences alongside scriptural patterns, not to *claim* a one-to-one equivalence, but to offer the invitation. Here is what happened in Elijah. Here is what happened in my life. Come read the Scripture around it. Let the Spirit speak to you directly. The archive is not a proof. It is a threshold. And that threshold principle is itself a scriptural truth about how the Kingdom advances. The mystery of the Kingdom is not withheld out of elitism — Jesus explicitly says in Matthew 13:11 that the mysteries of the Kingdom of Heaven have been *given* to the disciples. The word given is δέδοται (dedotai, de-DO-tai) — a perfect passive indicative, meaning the giving has already occurred and its effect continues. What has been given remains given. The crowd must approach and ask. That asking — that posture of hunger — is itself a grace. The parable format in my writing is not just pedagogical style; it is a participation in the way the Kingdom intentionally creates invitation rather than coercion. What returned to me in the conversation, and what I want to dwell on now, is the moment I described my own morning — going to work angry about gas prices and geopolitical absurdity, feeling the weight of the grind — and then, by the time I arrived, shifting back into a posture of excellence for the Lord. I want to be careful not to romanticize that shift. It was not easy. It is never easy. What happened is not that the circumstances changed or that I achieved some emotional regulation technique. What happened is that I stepped back into an identity. Not into a feeling. Not into a performance. Into a truth: *whatever you do, do it heartily, as to the Lord and not to men* (Colossians 3:23). The word heartily here is ἐκ ψυχῆς (ek psychēs, ek psoo-KHAYS) — from the soul. Not from the surface. Not from obligation. From the interior place that has been claimed by the Kingdom. And this is the tension my brother and I were both sitting inside: the battle between flesh and spirit is not a sign that something is broken. It is a sign that something is alive. Paul writes in Galatians 5:17, “For the flesh lusts against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh; and these are contrary to one another, so that you do not do the things that you wish.” This is not a description of spiritual failure. This is a description of the alive man, the man in whom the Spirit actually dwells. The battle proves the inheritance. The struggle proves the indwelling. And so the joy is not the absence of the fight. The joy is the anchor that holds while the fight is real — the hope of the Kingdom that does not shame us because the love of God has been poured out in our hearts by the Holy Spirit (Romans 5:5). That is the joy. That is what I was trying to name in the call. The battle is the gift that keeps reminding me the Kingdom is real. Finally, I am struck by the simplicity of how the call ended. My brother needed sleep. We prayed over each other simply. God bless. God bless you. Go rest. There is a theology in that, too. The Sabbath was not given as a reward for productivity. It was given as a rhythm of trust — the acknowledgment that the work of the Kingdom does not depend entirely on my wakefulness. Rest is an act of faith. For a man who is being reformed, who does not yet know what he wants or where he is going, who is reading an archive in pieces and listening to AI-voiced summaries while drifting to sleep — that rest is not escape. It is the posture of one who is learning, slowly, to let the Lord carry what he cannot. The Shepherd makes His sheep lie down (Psalm 23:2). Sometimes that is the most prophetic act available: to stop striving and lie down in the green pasture, even when the storm is coming to the UK. ## Implications and Challenges The modern Western church has largely lost a theology of grief. We move men from pain to performance too quickly — pressing them toward ministry, testimony, and usefulness before they have been genuinely formed in the furnace. What this conversation reveals is that grief is not a waiting room outside the Kingdom. It is often the classroom inside it. The challenge is to resist the impulse to resolve pain prematurely, in ourselves and in our brothers. There is also an implicit challenge here to the way we read and share Scripture. Too much of Christian media either condescends to the audience — reducing Scripture to motivational slogans — or overwhelms them with uncontextualized depth that creates no genuine invitation. The parabolic teaching model Jesus employed and that I was attempting to honor in the archive is neither of those. It trusts the audience to come and ask. It creates hunger rather than demanding immediate comprehension. Much of modern ministry would benefit from recovering the discipline of the threshold. The conversation also presses on the question of male community and honest spiritual dialogue. Brother Samuel and I talked about heartbreak, confusion, purpose, lifting weights, storms, sleep, and the archive — all in one call. That kind of conversation is rare. The church often either formalizes male community into programs or leaves men entirely isolated. What we see here is something in between: two brothers holding each other’s honest confusion before the Lord without resolving it falsely. That is itself a model worth honoring. There is a temptation, especially in seasons of disorientation, to mistake *not knowing* for spiritual backwardness. My brother said he does not know what he wants. The culture would immediately prescribe self-discovery exercises. A shallow Christianity might prescribe a prayer checklist. But the deeper word is this: the not-knowing is an invitation into the οἰκονομία (oikonomia, oy-koh-noh-MEE-ah) — the household economy, the divine management — of a Father who does know. Surrendering the need to have the answer is not weakness. It is the beginning of the kind of trust that Scripture calls faith. Finally, the meditation on anger and grief challenges the common Christian suppression of negative emotion. We often feel we must present emotional resolution in order to be spiritually credible. But the Psalms refuse that suppression. Hosea refused it. Jesus in Gethsemane refused it. Naming the cup honestly is not unbelief. It is the precondition for the “nevertheless” — the word of surrender that opens the path through. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Grief does not disqualify us from the Kingdom, and confusion about the future does not mean the Father has abandoned the plan. Maturity means learning to bring both into the sanctuary — not to get a quick answer, but to receive presence. The man who can sit in not-knowing before the Lord without either suppressing it or spiraling in it is a man being formed for something that requires that kind of interior steadiness. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The call to do all things heartily, as to the Lord, is not a passive resignation. It is an active re-entry into identity every single time the flesh rises in complaint. The obedience is the stepping back — again and again — into the truth of who we are in Christ, not the feeling of who we are, but the truth. That re-entry is itself the practice of Kingdom citizenship. **For Prophetic Posture:** The parabolic invitation — speak truth in a form that creates a threshold, that honors the hearer’s agency, that trusts the Spirit to do the deeper work of illumination — is a posture all who carry the witness of Christ must learn. We are not coercers. We are threshold-keepers. We point toward the door and trust the One standing at it to do the opening. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who has sat in the confusion and the grief and the weariness of this life and found that You were there in all of it — not absent, not distant, but present and forming. I thank You for the gift of brothers who will speak honestly in the dark, who will hold each other’s not-knowing without resolving it falsely. I thank You that the battle is the proof of the inheritance, that the struggle confirms the indwelling, and that the cup — every cup I have asked You to take — has been the vessel of formation. I agree with Your will over my brother Samuel now: that his confusion would not consume him but carry him deeper into the sanctuary, that his grief would become the corridor through which he encounters the Father who names him and knows him fully. I ask You, as one of Your servants standing between heaven and earth, to make the witness of this conversation fruitful — let the archive stand as a threshold for those who have ears to hear, let the parable do its work, and let the Lord of the Sabbath give the rest that only He can give. Let every weary man find in You not a program but a presence, not a resolution but a Person, not an answer but the Anchor. To You be all glory, honor, and praise, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, now and into the age that has no end.
