# 06-24 at 12:32 — The Weight of the Word: Language, Waiting, and the Crucible of the Nations **Title:** 06-24 at 12:32 — The Weight of the Word: Language, Waiting, and the Crucible of the Nations **Recorded:** 2026-06-24 12:32:44 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to this conversation unfold — moving from near-death on a highway to tornadoes in Bristol, from the fracture of household covenant to the active posture of waiting on God — I found myself struck not by any single thread but by the weight of all of them together. The Spirit was not speaking in one moment of the call; He was weaving through every moment, pressing something through the ordinary chaos of two brothers catching up across an ocean. What arrests me now, sitting with it afterward, is how much revelation was passing through what could so easily have been dismissed as casual conversation — and how the very *topic* of language and framing became itself a demonstration of what the Spirit was trying to say. **Why This Matters:** The way we speak is not merely a social courtesy or a professional discipline — it is a spiritual architecture. Words build frames, and frames shape expectation, and expectation either aligns with the Kingdom or quietly positions us against it. This matters for every disciple because we live in a moment when the world is speaking constantly — through screens, markets, news cycles, and social signals — and the saints are often speaking the world’s language while wearing Kingdom clothing. If the Spirit is pressing on the very vocabulary of a business conversation, how much more is He pressing on the vocabulary of our faith, our prayers, our declarations, our understanding of waiting, of covenant, of calling? This call touches the nerve of something far larger than one friendship: it touches the question of whether we have ears trained to hear what is actually being said — in the room, in the culture, and in the Spirit. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, this conversation reads as two friends doing what friends do: catching up, sharing stories, venting about close calls, joking about weather, smoking and relaxing, processing the anxieties of a shared business venture, and occasionally drifting into loosely spiritual territory. The near-accident on the highway gets filed under “lucky driving instincts.” The tornado in Bristol and the lightning strikes over London get filed under climate change statistics and anomalous weather systems. The discussion about Native American licensing policy gets filed under political frustration. The observations about language in business get filed under professional communication coaching. The musings about marriage, household roles, and the church get filed under personal philosophy informed by religious background. The reference to *kavah* — waiting on God — gets filed under spiritual hobby, the kind of thing religiously inclined people say when they’re not sure what comes next. Taken through this lens, the whole conversation is pleasant, human, relatable, and ultimately unremarkable — two men navigating ordinary life with occasional God-talk woven through it. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind cannot perceive what the Spirit is doing beneath the surface of a conversation like this, because it lacks the category for *kairos* — for the appointed moment disguised as the mundane. The natural mind evaluates content by its apparent subject matter, not by its spiritual pressure. It does not recognize that a conversation about brake pads and near-death can be, simultaneously, a word about divine preservation and purpose. It does not recognize that a brother asking “how long will the Lord require us to do this?” is actually standing at a threshold of covenant faithfulness, not merely expressing fatigue. It does not recognize that a near-tornado in England, an unprecedented lightning storm over London, and record-breaking heat on the same island that a brother just described as “winning its country back” are pressing against one another in a way that the Book of Revelation does not leave as coincidence. The natural mind sees isolated events. The mind yielded to the Spirit sees a tapestry — and in that tapestry, a Hand. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I want to begin where the conversation began for me spiritually — not at the beginning of the call, but at the moment I said something I almost did not say: *the hand of God.* My brother asked how I survived what happened on that highway, and I answered with years of experience and then, almost as an afterthought, “probably the hand of God.” But I do not believe it was an afterthought in heaven. I am seeing now, sitting with this, that the preservation was not incidental. The tiredness I admitted to — not fully paying attention, exhausted, running on empty — is exactly the kind of condition in which God does not abandon His servant; He *shows up in it*, so that the glory cannot be credited to the servant’s alertness. Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 4:7 that we have this treasure in earthen vessels, “that the excellence of the power may be of God and not of us.” I was an earthen vessel doing seventy miles an hour on a two-lane highway, half-awake, and the hand that parted waters and stopped mouths of lions and held three men in a furnace held that car on that road. This is not dramatic exaggeration. This is simply what the witness of Scripture teaches me to recognize. There is something further I notice in the mechanics of how I navigated that near-collision. I did not simply slam the brakes and surrender to the skid. I slammed, then released to restore traction, then swerved, then corrected, then braked again at the moment of straightening. There is a pattern there that the Spirit will not let me pass over without comment. It is the pattern of Spirit-led navigation in danger: full force applied, then a release — not an abandonment, but a *letting off* so that what you are riding can grip the road again. How many times have I seen saints who, when crisis comes, slam down hard on the brakes of intercession, warfare, or petition — and then keep the foot down so hard that the tires of their own soul spin without traction? There is a rhythm to surviving the unexpected. It requires force *and* release, pressure *and* trust, full engagement *and* the willingness to momentarily let go so that grip can be restored. The conversation then moved to weather — and I want to be careful here, because I am not a prophet projecting what the transcript does not support. But I did say something during the call that I believe bears expanding: when the natural pattern breaks, when earthquakes appear off fault lines and tornadoes form over islands and lightning strikes a city in numbers that break historical records, we are not merely observing meteorological anomaly. The Book of Revelation does speak, in multiple passages, of thunders and lightnings as accompanying the opening of the seals, the sounding of the trumpets, and the pouring of the bowls. Revelation 8:5 — “there were noises, thunderings, lightnings, and an earthquake.” Revelation 11:19 — “there were lightnings, noises, thunderings, an earthquake, and great hail.” Revelation 16:18 — “there were noises and thunderings and lightnings.” This is not the first time in Scripture that atmospheric upheaval accompanies the movement of God’s throne. At Sinai, in Exodus 19:16, “there were thunderings and lightnings, and a thick cloud on the mountain.” The pattern is canonical: when heaven moves toward earth in judgment or in covenant, creation groans and shakes. I am not saying the seven seals are now open. I am saying the pattern is real, it is scriptural, and it deserves sober attention — not sensationalism, but watchfulness. My brother said something that I found myself returning to: *“We’ve just won our country back.”* And I responded in the moment with something I want to develop more carefully now. I said yes and no. The victory is already won — and I mean that precisely as Colossians 2:15 means it, that Jesus “having disarmed principalities and powers, He made a public spectacle of them, triumphing over them in it.” The victory is not being achieved by a political movement; it has already been accomplished in the resurrection. What political and social movements do — when they are genuine — is *respond* to that victory, not produce it. The distinction matters enormously. When the church believes it is *winning* something through social or political recovery, it subtly repositions itself as the agent of victory rather than the *beneficiary* of a victory already secured. The saints are not the army that wins the war; we are the army that occupies in the name of the One who has already won it. This is not semantics. This is the difference between intercession from rest and warfare from anxiety. And then my brother asked me the question that I believe was the hinge of the entire call spiritually: *“How long will the Lord require us to do this? Will we get one mission or multiple?”* And I reached for the Hebrew word כָּוָה (*kavah*, KAH-vah) — muscular, active waiting. I want to stay with that word, because I did not have time on the call to fully unfold it. This word appears in Isaiah 40:31 — “But those who wait on the Lord shall renew their strength.” The English word *wait* implies passivity. The Hebrew כָּוָה (KAH-vah) implies something closer to the posture of a soldier at the wall — eyes open, scanning the horizon, every nerve alert, but the body still. It is not staring at the phone. It is not filling the silence with noise so that you don’t have to feel the weight of not yet knowing. It is the posture of one who is so convinced that something is coming that they cannot afford to look away. And I said during the call that this is what it means to wait on the Lord — to be so expectant that you would be frightened if He actually showed up. That trembling readiness is what the patriarchs and prophets carried. When Jacob wrestled, he did not stop until he received the blessing. When Elijah sat under the juniper tree, the angel touched him not once but twice. Active waiting is not the absence of activity; it is the discipline of directing every faculty toward the One who is coming. The conversation then moved to language and framing in business, and I said something that the Spirit has been pressing on me for a while: *the verbiage and the way that we speak matters.* This is not merely a professional communication principle. It is a deeply scriptural one. Proverbs 18:21 states plainly: “Death and life are in the power of the tongue, and those who love it will eat its fruit.” James 3 devotes an entire section to the tongue as something that, though small, can set a great forest on fire. When I told my brother not to say “you made money” unless that money had already been transferred — because money in motion is not yet in hand — I was operating from a principle that has both financial and spiritual dimensions. Words that celebrate a reality not yet fully secured can create a premature closure of expectation that actually weakens the discipline required to see it through. In the Spirit, this translates: declarations are powerful, but declarations must be rooted in truth, not in desire. To confess what God has said is ἀνθομολογέομαι (anthomologeomai); to confess what we merely hope without grounding is different, and the difference is the integrity of the frame. Then we arrived at what I believe was the deepest current beneath the entire call — the structure of covenant, household, and the Body of Christ. I began speaking about the οἰκονόμος (oikonomos, oy-KON-oh-moss) — the household manager, the one who stewards everything under the master’s roof. In Luke 12:42, Jesus asks, “Who then is that faithful and wise steward, whom his master will make ruler over his household?” The word is precisely this: οἰκονόμος (oy-KON-oh-moss). And Paul uses the same root when he writes in 1 Corinthians 4:1 that ministers of Christ should be regarded as “stewards of the mysteries of God.” The household — the οἶκος (oikos, OY-koss), the family dwelling — is the basic unit of Kingdom life. It is not the megachurch auditorium. It is not the conference stage. It is the table, the threshold, the morning, the children, the neighbor’s door. I said during the call that κοινωνία (koinonia, koy-noh-NEE-ah) — that word we translate as “fellowship” — is not a Sunday program. It is the daily, embodied, inconvenient, knocking-on-your-neighbor’s-door life of the early church. Acts 2:46 says they broke bread “from house to house” — not from auditorium to auditorium. The οἶκος (OY-koss) is where κοινωνία (koy-noh-NEE-ah) happens, and we have largely lost it. I want to be honest here about what I said regarding marriage and household roles — because it matters and because I said it imperfectly in the moment. I spoke about the distinction between what a wife needs and what a husband needs. What I was reaching for is what Ephesians 5 actually teaches: husbands are commanded to love their wives as Christ loved the church — a sacrificial, attentive, knowing love. Wives are called to respect and honor their husbands as the church honors Christ. This is not a hierarchy of worth; it is a covenant of function. The reason I believe this matters so much right now is that the attack on the canonical family structure — which I mentioned during the call — is not merely a cultural or political phenomenon. It is a spiritual one, because the family is typological. The household is a living parable of the relationship between Christ and His bride. When that structure fractures, the parable fractures, and people lose the capacity to understand what the covenant between God and humanity actually looks like in the flesh. Paul says in Ephesians 5:32, “This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church.” The mystery is not incidental to the marriage; the marriage *is the mystery*. I said toward the end of the call something I almost did not finish: *only in our weakness can God’s glory shine.* This is the word of 2 Corinthians 12:9 — “My grace is sufficient for you, for My strength is made perfect in weakness.” And I said it in the context of parenting — of acknowledging that I will unknowingly pass things on to my children that I have not yet had broken off of me, just as my parents did to me. And rather than receiving that as condemnation, I said it is part of the design. This is the refiner’s fire that my brother named — and I believe he named it accurately. The fire does not destroy what is gold; it only removes what is not. The father who enters the scripture broken and incomplete is not disqualified from fatherhood. He is exactly the kind of father through whom God’s sufficiency becomes visible to a child. My weakness is not the obstacle to my children’s faith. My willingness to be broken, corrected, and reformed in front of them may be the very evidence of God’s faithfulness that forms their faith. My brother said something that I am still sitting with: *“The Bible is the best prep story for any father.”* And he said it in the context of observing that God seemed to call me into deep Scripture reading just before fatherhood was upon me. I received that as a true word. And then he said something I want to close this reflection on: *“You were in the refiner’s fire.”* I want to hold that phrase against the opening of this call — the heat warnings, the record temperatures, the tornado, the crucible weather over an island nation that is, in some sense, finding its way back to its first love. There is a word in Malachi 3:3 about the refiner sitting with silver, watching it until he can see his own face in it. The heat is not punishment. The heat is the process by which the Refiner’s reflection becomes visible in what He is forming. Whether it is an individual on a highway, a nation under a heat warning, or a church being called back from religious performance to living κοινωνία (koy-noh-NEE-ah) — the fire is purposeful, the Refiner is present, and He does not walk away from the crucible until what He is making is ready. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation presses on me — and on us — is the challenge of *attentive speech*. I said it during the call and I feel it more deeply now: the way we frame things matters. This is not about policing vocabulary or performing spiritual correctness. It is about whether our words are anchored in the reality of what God has actually done and is actually doing, or whether they are floating on the surface of what we wish were true. The prosperity gospel has done enormous damage here, not because it believes in blessing — Scripture commands us to expect blessing — but because it taught a generation to speak as though desired outcomes were already secured before the faithfulness required to secure them had actually been rendered. Let our speech be honest, grounded, and expectant — but honest first. The second challenge is the loss of the κοινωνία (koy-noh-NEE-ah) culture in the Western church. The institutional church has largely replaced daily, household-level community with weekly programming. I am not calling for the abolition of gathered worship — Scripture commands it and I love it — but I am pressing this: if the only time your neighbors know you are a Christian is when you post something on a screen, the οἶκος (OY-koss) is not functioning. Knock on a door this week. Not to evangelize strategically, but because you actually care what’s happening behind it. The third challenge concerns how we speak about national or political recovery. My brother’s excitement about his country finding its way back is real and I share it — but I want to press the saints gently here: do not let any political movement become a substitute for genuine spiritual revival. Political shifts can create conditions for revival; they cannot produce it. Only the Holy Spirit produces it. And He produces it in the people who have not confused the two. The fourth challenge is about waiting — about כָּוָה (KAH-vah). The church has largely lost the discipline of active, muscular, expectant waiting on God. We have replaced it with activity — programs, initiatives, vision statements, strategic plans — and these things are not wrong, but they are incomplete without the posture of the watchman on the wall. Isaiah 21:8 — “Then he cried, ‘A lion, my Lord! I stand continually on the watchtower in the daytime; I have sat at my post every night.’” That is the posture. Are we sitting at our post? The fifth challenge is personal and I will name it plainly: I was tired on the road this morning. I was not fully present. And I survived anyway — by grace and by instinct and by a Hand that I do not take lightly. But I want to name the warning embedded in the mercy: the grace that preserved me was not an endorsement of diminished attentiveness. It was a gift given in a moment of human limitation. Do not presume upon it. Rest when rest is needed. Be present when presence is required. The saints who will navigate what is coming in the earth will need to be rested, alert, and attuned — not running on fumes through a grace they’ve mistaken for permission. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity means learning to read what is actually happening — in a conversation, on a highway, in the weather, in a nation — through the interpretive lens of Scripture rather than the default lens of cultural assumption. It means not dismissing a near-death experience as luck, not dismissing unprecedented weather as mere climate data, and not dismissing a brother’s casual question as small talk when it is actually a threshold moment of covenant faithfulness. Maturity is the willingness to slow down after the call and ask: *what was the Spirit actually saying through that?* **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience here calls us to three things: speak with integrity and precision, especially when money and partnership are involved; invest in the daily fabric of household-level community rather than only institutional participation; and cultivate the discipline of active waiting — scanning the horizon with full expectation, not filling the silence with noise. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation calls for is the posture of the watchman and the steward together — eyes open to what is happening in the earth, ears attuned to what the Spirit is saying through it, and hands faithful to the stewardship assigned in this season. Do not announce what you have not been given. Do not suppress what you have. And do not confuse the fire you are in with abandonment. The Refiner is present. The silver is in His hands. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who should not have made it home this morning and did — not by skill alone, but by the weight of Your hand on that road. I am grateful beyond what words can carry. I thank You for the brother across the ocean, for a conversation that moved through the ordinary and the sacred without distinguishing between them the way we so often do — as though You only inhabit the spiritual parts and leave us alone in the rest. You do not. You were in the near-miss on the highway. You were in the question about how long we carry this mission. You were in the word about language and the word about waiting and the word about fire. Lord, I ask that You continue to press on my tongue — not just to clean it of profanity, but to align it with truth, with covenant, with the reality of what You are actually doing rather than what I want to be true. I ask that You restore in us the culture of κοινωνία (koy-noh-NEE-ah) — the daily, embodied, door-knocking, table-sharing life of Your people. I ask that You teach us to כָּוָה (KAH-vah) — to wait on You with every nerve awake, with eyes on the horizon, with the trembling readiness of those who know You are coming. And for the nations under heat and thunder and wind right now — for England, for America, for every people You are pressing into the crucible — I agree with Your refining work. Sit with the silver, Lord. Do not leave until You see Your face in it. In the name of Jesus Christ, who is the same yesterday, today, and forever — the One in whom all things hold together — amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | 2 Corinthians 4:7 — “We have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the excellence of the power may be of God and not of us.” | σκεύη ὀστράκινα (skeuē ostrakina, SKYOO-ay oss-TRAK-ee-nah) — clay vessels / earthenware | Paul’s point is that the fragility is intentional — it is the proof that the power is divine, not human. | | Isaiah 40:31 — “Those who wait on the LORD shall renew their strength.” | כָּוָה (kavah, KAH-vah) — to wait / to bind together / to hope with tension | Active, muscular waiting; not passive idleness but alert, bound expectation directed toward God. | | Revelation 8:5 — “there were noises, thunderings, lightnings, and an earthquake” | βρονταί, ἀστραπαί (brontai, astrapaí, bron-TIE, as-trah-PIE) — thunders, lightnings | Canonical pattern: atmospheric upheaval accompanies divine movement at Sinai (Ex. 19:16) and throughout Revelation; not coincidental but typological. | | Colossians 2:15 — “Having disarmed principalities and powers, He made a public spectacle of them, triumphing over them in it.” | ἀπεκδυσάμενος (apekdusamenos, ah-pek-DOO-sah-meh-noss) — having stripped off / disarmed | The victory is past tense, completed in the cross and resurrection.
