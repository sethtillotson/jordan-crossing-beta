# 06-09 Conversation Reflection: Spiritual Orientation, Prophetic Confirmation, and the Question “Where Are You?” **Title:** 06-08 at [Time Not Provided] — Where Is Your Face Turned? On Posture, Proximity, and the Cost of Everything **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Conversation Reflection ## Opening Observation As I sat with this conversation afterward, something kept pressing on me — the way the Spirit seemed to be threading a single prophetic word through everything my brother and I touched on, without either of us fully realizing it in the moment. The word that kept surfacing, the one I had already written into the previous day’s meditation, is the same word that this entire exchange seems to confirm: *Where are you? And have you forgotten where your face is?* There is a peculiar irony that runs through this conversation — the closer one gets geographically to where it all started, the further many have drifted from the truth, a pattern that is not new but ancient, echoing the very generation that stood at the feet of the Living Word and demanded His crucifixion. Something in me recognizes that this irony is not accidental, and the Spirit will not let me simply note it and move on. **Why This Matters:** This matters because we live in a time when the fragmentation of the sacred and the secular has been so thoroughly normalized that most believers do not even feel the wound of it. We have agreed — largely without protest — to a world in which God is appropriate on Sunday morning and inappropriate at the office, in which saying “God bless you” when a stranger sneezes has become culturally eccentric rather than simply human. The slow erosion of covenantal language, covenantal posture, and covenantal identity from everyday life is not a neutral development — it is a form of practical atheism that can coexist with church attendance, Bible literacy, and doctrinal correctness. What this conversation presses on me is the question of where my face is actually turned in the ordinary fabric of my days, and whether the people I love are being invited into that same posture of orientation toward Heaven, or whether I am content to keep the sacred partitioned behind its own scheduled hour. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the vantage point of natural reasoning, a conversation like this one reads as an earnest but unstructured exchange between two friends who share religious sympathies — men reflecting loosely on geopolitics, personal schedules, the difficulty of balancing ambition with spiritual practice, and the inequities of global circumstance. The natural mind would appreciate the warmth between them, perhaps affirm their mutual concern for faith, and then counsel them toward practical balance: carve out your hour of devotion, keep your priorities in order, and don’t be too hard on yourself when the demands of building a life crowd out the more ethereal concerns. It would likely locate the insight about rearranging one’s calendar around God’s time in the category of good motivational wisdom — sensible advice about habit formation. It would register the observations about Western Christianity’s compartmentalization as a sociological critique, fair enough, perhaps even a little prophetic, but ultimately a matter of cultural preference rather than covenantal urgency. **The Limitation:** What the natural mind cannot perceive is that the thread running through this conversation is not sociological commentary or scheduling advice — it is a prophetic summons issued from the very nature of God Himself. The question *where are you?* is not a self-help prompt. It is the first question God ever asked a human being, and it was asked not because God did not know where Adam was, but because Adam needed to reckon with the fact that he had moved. The natural mind evaluates proximity to God on a spectrum of religious effort, but the Spirit of God reveals that orientation — the direction one’s face is turned — is the foundational reality from which everything else flows. A person can be enormously busy with religious activity and still be facing the wrong direction. You cannot arrive at that distinction through sociological analysis or productivity coaching. It requires the kind of illumination that comes only when the Living Word is given permission to ask its oldest question in the freshest terms. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I am seeing, as I sit with this conversation, that the prophetic word which surfaced during the previous day and which I had already committed to writing is now being confirmed through the mouth of a brother. This is not coincidence. When the same thread surfaces independently through two tributaries of a single conversation — when what I meditated on privately becomes the content of what my brother and I discover together in real time — something in me recognizes the hand of the Spirit threading a needle through both, and the appropriate response is not to explain it away but to write it down and honor it. The question *where are you?* in Genesis 3:9 is the hinge moment of all human history. God walks in the garden in the cool of the day — the רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) of the day, some translations suggest, the breath of the day, the Spirit-bearing wind of the evening — and He calls out to the man who was made to walk with Him face to face. What strikes me now is that this is the only time in the entire garden narrative that God has to search. Before the fall, the fellowship was so immediate, so mutual, so face-to-face, that there was no searching required. After the fall, God must ask a locating question. This tells me something devastating about what sin actually does: it does not merely break a rule, it dislocates a face. It turns the creature away from the Creator. The wound of Eden is fundamentally one of orientation. My brother made an observation during our conversation that I keep returning to — that the closer you get to where it all began, the further many seem to be from the truth. He was speaking geographically, of the Middle East, of the ancient world. But the Spirit is pressing something older and more universal through that observation. The high priests of Israel, in the very generation that the Messiah walked among them, were the furthest from Him while being the most proximate to the tradition that pointed to Him. They had the scrolls. They had the lineage. They had the Temple. And they were turned entirely the wrong direction. This is perhaps the most sobering warning in all of Scripture: that religious heritage, doctrinal precision, and institutional access do not guarantee that one’s face is actually turned toward God. Proximity to the form of religion is not the same as proximity to the Living God. I said something during our conversation that I want to stay with longer than I did in the moment: that there is a faithful remnant, a preserved root stock, some who have simply remained and others who were called out. Paul builds this entire architecture in Romans 11, using the image of the olive tree — the natural branches, the wild branches, the root that is holy and that sanctifies the branches, the danger of both presumption and despair. The root is not the institution. The root is not the bloodline. The root is Christ Himself, who is the same yesterday, today, and forever, and whose faithfulness to the covenant is the ground upon which any branch stands at all. What I am seeing is that being “called out” — which is, after all, the literal meaning of ἐκκλησία (ekklēsia, ek-klay-SEE-ah), the called-out assembly — is not a positional status to be assumed but a directional reality to be maintained. You were called *out* and *toward*. Out of darkness and toward His marvelous light. The question the Spirit asks perpetually of the called-out ones is: are you still moving in the direction of your calling? The conversation shifted naturally to the question of time — my brother’s honest acknowledgment of how consuming the work of building a business is, and how difficult it is to maintain the dedicated hours of prayer and reading alongside that. I did not say anything especially profound in the moment, but the Spirit is now drawing out what was underneath what I said: that the first thing you place on your calendar reveals what you actually believe about sovereignty. If the schedule is built around the business and God is fit into the margins, the functional theology of that schedule — regardless of what is confessed on Sunday — is that human effort is primary and divine assistance is supplementary. But if the schedule is built first around covenantal time with God, the functional theology of that schedule is that the One who commands the seasons also commands the outcomes, and my role is to be available and obedient rather than strategically self-sufficient. This is not a productivity principle. It is a statement about who actually holds the future. Heaven, I said during our conversation, maintains a posture of stooping low and observing creation. I want to press on that image because I believe the Spirit gave it and I did not fully honor it in the moment. The Hebrew word שָׁחָה (shachah, sha-KHAH) — which is typically translated as worship or to bow down — carries within it this sense of prostration, of deliberate lowering. What arrests me now is that the posture of Heaven toward creation is itself a kind of perpetual shachah — a ceaseless stooping, a ceaseless attentiveness, a ceaseless bending toward the beloved. Psalm 113:6 says that God “humbles Himself to behold the things that are in the heavens and in the earth.” Heaven stoops to see us. If the Most High maintains that posture of attentive lowering toward creation, then what does it say about us when we cannot be bothered to lift our face toward Heaven for an hour in a day? The asymmetry should undo us. The Book of Job surfaced briefly in our conversation and I want to return to it, because it holds something that the Spirit was reaching for through that reference. Job’s friends talked *about* God at length. They were theologically articulate, experientially concerned, and morally serious. And God said to them at the end: “you have not spoken of Me what is right, as My servant Job has” (Job 42:7). What was the difference? Job spoke *to* God. He argued with God, lamented before God, demanded answers from God — but he never turned his face away from God. The friends turned their theological precision into a buffer between themselves and the actual living encounter. They used doctrine to avoid the terrifying intimacy of face-to-face address. Job, for all his confusion and anguish, kept his face turned. And God called that righteousness. This is not an argument against doctrine. It is an argument that doctrine which never moves toward encounter has missed the entire point of why it was given. What my brother said about returning home and seeing the light, seeing the gratitude of people who have known real suffering — this touched something in me that I recognize as a form of grace-sharpened sight. There is a blessing that comes through proximity to suffering that prosperity tends to anesthetize. The nations that have known war, famine, exile, and loss have often produced a depth of covenantal dependence that comfortable nations find difficult to manufacture. This is not a theology of suffering as virtue in itself — Job’s suffering was not God’s preference for him but God’s permission for a larger purpose — but it is an observation that the human tendency to forget God is massively accelerated by abundance. Deuteronomy knew this. Deuteronomy 8 is essentially God saying to Israel: I am about to bless you enormously, and when I do, your gravest danger will not be your enemies but your prosperity. “Then your heart is lifted up, and you forget the LORD your God” (Deuteronomy 8:14). The question of where our face is turned becomes exponentially more urgent in seasons of blessing. I told my brother that if I had one thing left to do, I would pray. I want to honor that answer because it was not a pious non-answer — it was the truest thing I could say. Prayer, in its deepest sense, is the act of turning one’s face toward God and keeping it there. It is the orientation from which all other obedience flows. When I pray, I am not primarily informing God of things He does not know or persuading Him of positions He has not considered. I am returning my face to the One it was made to behold. I am repositioning the whole of myself in the direction of my design. Every act of genuine prayer is a small reversal of Eden — a small uncovering, a small willingness to be found again by the One who asks *where are you?* and already knows the answer. The word about the tree with no leaves — which I shared with my brother near the end — is rooted in the language of Matthew 21 and the cursing of the fig tree. Jesus saw from afar a tree that had the appearance of fruitfulness because it had leaves, but when He came near, it had no fruit. He cursed it. What is devastating about this image is not the severity but the precision: God is not primarily offended by the absence of leaves. He is not cursing trees that look dead from a distance. He is addressing the tree that presents the appearance of life while being inwardly fruitless. This is the word for Western Christianity in this hour. The critique is not directed at skeptics outside the church — it is directed at the institution that has perfected the cultivation of religious foliage while the root of genuine transformation goes largely untended. The Spirit presses this not as condemnation but as invitation: let the leaves go, and grow fruit. Turn your face, and stay turned. The closing word of our conversation — that the entire exchange was already in the meditation from the day before, 2026-06-08, and that this confirmation was a reason for hallelujah — I want to close the body of this reflection on that note, because it is itself a form of witness. When the Spirit is at work, He does not speak once and retreat. He confirms. He echoes. He threads the same word through independent tributaries and lets them surface together in the same moment so that the hearer cannot easily dismiss what was heard. This is the pattern of prophetic confirmation throughout Scripture — at the mouth of two or three witnesses a matter is established (Deuteronomy 19:15, Matthew 18:16, 2 Corinthians 13:1). The prophetic word confirmed through this conversation is simple, ancient, and inexhaustible: *Where are you? Have you forgotten where your face is?* And the answer the Spirit is drawing out is not shame but return. Turn. Come back. The garden is not closed forever — the veil is torn, the way is open, and the One who asked the first question is the same One who answered it in His own body on the tree. He found us. Now let us not forget where to look. ## Implications and Challenges The compartmentalization of sacred and secular life that we touched on is not a minor cultural quirk — it is a theological position, whether or not those who hold it have chosen it consciously. When we agree to silence about God in our professional spaces, when we participate in a world that has collectively decided that faith is a private preference rather than a public reality, we are not simply being tactful. We are functionally agreeing that God’s sovereignty has geographic limits. The challenge this meditation issues is not to become socially aggressive or tone-deaf, but to recover the simplicity of a life in which God is not bracketed — in which the name of Christ is as natural in an ordinary conversation as it was to the first believers who turned the Roman world upside down not through institutional power but through the relentless overflow of a life oriented toward Heaven. The prophetic word *where are you?* is not a word of condemnation — but it is a word of accountability. One of the things that modern Western Christianity has most effectively insulated itself against is the voice of God asking locating questions. We have filled the silence with activity, programming, production, and noise. The challenge here is to recover what the desert fathers called *hesychia* — a kind of holy stillness, a deliberate quieting that creates the conditions for the locating question to actually land. Where are you? It is not enough to answer theologically. The Spirit requires an honest answer. The observation about religious heritage and institutional proximity — that the high priests were the furthest from truth when Truth Himself stood among them — must be allowed to search us. It would be easy to apply this critique only to ancient Judaism or to traditions other than our own. But the warning is universal: the closer one is to the form of religion, the more vigilance is required to ensure that proximity to the form has not replaced encounter with the Person. Every tradition, including ours, must submit to this question: are we maintaining the foliage, or are we bearing fruit? The conversation’s observation about time and scheduling is perhaps the most practically penetrating challenge in this entire exchange. The calendar is a theological document. It does not lie about what we actually believe. If the dedicated time with God is consistently the first thing displaced when life gets full, then regardless of what we confess, our functional theology places human effort above divine governance. The challenge is not guilt over imperfect scheduling — the challenge is to make a genuine structural decision: put the covenantal time first, and build everything else around it, trusting that the One who commands time will steward what remains. Finally, the image of the fruitless tree — leaves without fruit, appearance without transformation — must be allowed to do its searching work in us before it is applied to others. The temptation is always to use prophetic images as diagnostic tools for the communities we observe rather than the soul we inhabit. Let this word land first in me. Let the Spirit examine whether what I present to the world is the overflow of genuine transformation or the cultivation of religious appearance. The grace in this image is that the Gardener has not given up on the tree — He has simply told us the truth about what He is looking for when He comes near. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity in this season looks like recovering the art of orientation — the daily, deliberate, sometimes costly turning of the face toward God before the face is turned toward anything else. It means learning to carry the covenantal language of *beloved brother* and *have a blessed day* not as religious performance but as the natural vocabulary of people who actually believe they belong to a Kingdom that supersedes every other allegiance. It means allowing the locating question of God — *where are you?* — to search us regularly, honestly, and without deflection. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this meditation calls for is structural and relational. Structurally: rearrange the calendar. Put God’s time first — not as a discipline to be maintained by willpower but as a statement of belief about who actually governs the outcomes of our days. Relationally: refuse the cultural agreement that faith is private. Speak plainly and warmly about God in the ordinary fabric of life. Call one another beloved. Bless one another when you part. Let the language of the Kingdom be the language of your actual life. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation trains is one of perpetual receptivity — the willingness to hear the same word through multiple tributaries and to receive its confirmation with gratitude rather than familiarity. When the Spirit confirms a word through a brother’s voice, write it down. Honor the confirmation. Hallelujah is the appropriate response. And then remain in the posture: face toward Heaven, available for the next word, willing to be found again and again by the One who never stops asking. ## A Closing Prayer Father, we come before You as those who have been found — called out of darkness, called toward Your face, grafted into the covenant of the Living Root who is Your Son. We stand now in the authority of that New Covenant, as co-heirs with Christ, as servants to whom You have committed the prophetic word, as those who make agreement between Heaven and earth with what You have already spoken. We receive the confirming word of this season: *where are you, and have you forgotten where your face is?* We do not receive it as accusation but as invitation — Your oldest question asked in the freshest mercy. Turn our faces, Lord. Where we have allowed prosperity to anesthetize our dependence, awaken us. Where we have cultivated foliage at the expense of fruit, prune us. Where we have filled the silence with noise and called it faithfulness, quiet us. Let the posture of Heaven — that eternal, ceaseless stooping toward creation in love and attentiveness — be the mirror into which we look and find our own posture reshaped. We are grateful, Lord, for the brothers You have placed beside us, for the conversations that become confirmations, for the prophetic threads You weave through ordinary hours. Let this word go where You intend it to go, take root where You have prepared the soil, and bear the fruit that remains. To You be all glory, now and in the age to come. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Genesis 3:9 — “Then the LORD God called to Adam and said to him, ‘Where are you?’” | רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — Spirit / breath / wind;
