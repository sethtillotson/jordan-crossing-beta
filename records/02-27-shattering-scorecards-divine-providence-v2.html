# **Personal Meditation:** The Shattering of Scorecards — Divine Providence, Judas, and the Abolition of Binary Judgment --- ## ⚡ Opening Observation I find myself returning to something that won't let me go: the question of Judas, divine providence, and what we've been taught about judgment, hell, and the nature of God's plan. After sitting with the mustard seed conspiracy reflection and meditating on the absolute sovereignty of God's providence, I cannot—in good conscience—hold to the idea that Judas was simply condemned. If redemption through the cross was always God's plan, if there always had to be someone appointed to fulfill that role, then what does that mean about judgment itself? And more staggeringly, what does it mean about how we've been trained to see good and evil, sin and righteousness, heaven and hell? **Why This Matters:** This isn't abstract theology. This cuts to the core of how we perceive ourselves, how we treat one another, and whether we're actually living in the freedom of the gospel or still operating under a scorecard system that Jesus came to obliterate. If we continue to judge others—and ourselves—by binary categories that Scripture itself dismantles, we miss the radical, disorienting beauty of the Kingdom. We become Pharisees with New Testament vocabulary, still wielding stones we were never meant to carry. --- ## 🌍 The Worldly Mind's Interpretation From a purely human perspective, this line of thinking is dangerous. The natural mind insists on clear categories: good people and bad people, saved and damned, righteous and wicked. Judas betrayed Jesus—therefore, Judas is condemned. Case closed. The world needs this binary structure to maintain moral order, to ensure consequences, to keep society from collapsing into chaos. Justice, as we understand it, demands punishment for wrongdoing. If Judas isn't punished, then what deterrent remains? What accountability? The natural mind builds entire theological systems around this need for cosmic courtroom logic: contracts, payment, substitution, judgment, sentencing. It's clean. It's satisfying. It makes sense. **The Limitation:** But this is precisely where "the natural man does not receive the things of the Spirit of God, for they are foolishness to him; nor can he know them, because they are spiritually discerned" (1 Corinthians 2:14, NKJV). The worldly mind cannot fathom a God who appoints betrayal as part of redemption, who uses rebellion to accomplish perfect will, who looks at our moral scorecards and says, "I'm playing an entirely different game." It cannot hold the tension of divine sovereignty and human responsibility, of radical grace and fearful judgment, of predestination and genuine choice. And so it flattens mystery into formula, reduces wonder into policy, and replaces the living God with a cosmic accountant. --- ## ✝️ What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation I'm sitting with the Gospel accounts of Judas, and something arrests me: Jesus calls him "friend" even in the moment of betrayal (Matthew 26:50, NKJV). Not "traitor." Not "enemy." Φίλε (phile)—friend. The word carries intimacy, companionship, a term of endearment even in confrontation. And I have to ask: why? If Jesus knew from the beginning that Judas would betray Him (John 6:64), if He chose Judas as one of the Twelve knowing full well what was appointed to happen, then what are we to make of this? The Scripture never tells us what happened to Judas after death. We know he felt remorse, we know he hanged himself, we know the Apostles replaced him—but the eternal destiny of Judas? Silence. And in that silence, I'm beginning to see that maybe—just maybe—we've been asking the wrong question. What if the "punishment" Judas experienced wasn't some future courtroom verdict, but the very thing that tormented him in life: the accuser in his mind? The voice that told him he was beyond redemption, that he'd crossed a line too far, that his sin was irreversible. That accuser—ὁ κατήγορος (ho katēgoros), the one who levels charges—doesn't wait for an afterlife tribunal. He steals peace *now*. He destroys focus *now*. He kills dreams *now*. This is the hell we live in when we believe the lie that we are defined by our failures rather than by the One who made us and appointed even our breaking for His purposes. I'm seeing that hell isn't necessarily a geographic location in some future realm—it's a state of separation from the awareness of God's presence, a choosing to live outside the shelter of the divine love that *already permeates everything*. This brings me to something wild: in all my reading of Scripture, I haven't found "hell" as we've been taught it. I find Sheol (שְׁאוֹל)—the grave, the place of the dead, a shadowy holding place in the Hebrew Scriptures. I find Gehenna (γέεννα) in the Gospels—a reference to the Valley of Hinnom, a literal garbage dump outside Jerusalem where refuse was burned, a metaphor for judgment and destruction, not a cosmic torture chamber. I find Hades (ᾅδης)—the Greek concept of the underworld, the realm of the dead. But this eternal, conscious torment separated from God forever? That's not the emphasis. Jesus speaks constantly about the Kingdom of Heaven *coming down*—about New Jerusalem, the restoration of all things, the renewal of creation. And if I take the parable of the mustard seed seriously—how it grows to shelter even "the birds of the air" (Matthew 13:32, NKJV), which in other parables represent the nations, the Gentiles, even the unclean—and I place that alongside the wheat and tares growing together until harvest (Matthew 13:30), I start to see something stunning: both heaven and hell are *here*. Eternal life—ζωὴ αἰώνιος (zōē aiōnios)—is oneness with the Source, with God, *now and forever*. And separation from that oneness, choosing to reject the Holy Spirit entirely—that's what Jesus calls the unforgivable sin, the blasphemy against the Spirit (Matthew 12:31-32). Not a single mistake. Not Judas's betrayal. But a final, ultimate rejection of the Divine Presence that pursues us even into our rebellion. And here's where providence becomes breathtaking: if God planned everything, if "known to God from eternity are all His works" (Acts 15:18, NKJV), then even the so-called "evil" actors are part of the plan. This isn't fatalism—it's something far more mysterious. I'm thinking of Job. God Himself says to Job, "Have you considered My servant Job?" (Job 1:8, NKJV)—but the fuller sense in the Hebrew is: "Have you not understood that I *planned* this?" The word used carries the sense of purposeful design, not mere allowance. God made Job to be righteous. God made Lucifer—הֵילֵל (hêlēl), "shining one"—with the capacity for pride, envy, rebellion. And God *uses* that rebellion. Satan, the Accuser (הַשָּׂטָן, ha-satan), still communes with God (Job 1:6). He's a tool in the divine hand, not an independent rival. Which means the wild ones, the rebellious ones—they're not accidents. God makes them that way *on purpose*. Look at the pattern: Moses, a murderer. David, an adulterer and conspirator in murder. Jonah, who ran from God and caused a storm—and yet *through his rebellion*, pagan sailors came to worship the one true God (Jonah 1:16). Ananias in Damascus, who tried to give God a "rap sheet" on Saul of Tarsus, and God just said, "He is a chosen vessel of Mine" (Acts 9:15, NKJV). The Greek word for "chosen" here is ἐκλογῆς (eklogēs)—election, divine selection. Not *despite* Paul's violence, but *through* it. Meekness—πραΰτης (prautēs)—doesn't mean weakness; it means a wild horse that's been tamed, a rebellious spirit brought under divine direction *while retaining its strength*. The wild ones are the ones God uses to change history. Why? Because their breaking produces an experience, a depth, a testimony that the naturally compliant can never carry. This is redemptive discipline—παιδεία (paideia)—the chastening of the Lord (Hebrews 12:11), which is not punitive but *formative*. It trains. It grows. And maybe—just maybe—that's the whole point of this creation: a playground where time, physics, suffering, and choice create the conditions for eternal beings to *grow* in ways impossible in a static eternity. Which brings me to Hebrews, where everything I thought I knew about sin gets dismantled. "This is the covenant that I will make with them after those days, says the Lord: I will put My laws into their hearts, and in their minds I will write them," and then He adds, "Their sins and their lawless deeds I will remember no more" (Hebrews 10:16-17, NKJV). The Greek for "remember no more" is οὐ μὴ μνησθῶ (ou mē mnēsthō)—an emphatic double negative, the strongest possible denial. God will *absolutely not* remember our sins. And then the text says, "Now where there is remission of these, there is no longer an offering for sin" (Hebrews 10:18, NKJV). If sin is no longer counted, if the sacrifice is finished, if the Law is fulfilled and *abolished* in Christ (Ephesians 2:15), then every time we identify as "sinners," every time we confess sin as if we're still under condemnation, we're essentially putting Jesus back on the cross. We're rejecting the finished work. We have boldness—παρρησία (parrēsia), confident freedom of speech—to enter the Holiest by the blood of Jesus (Hebrews 10:19). We pass through the veil—His flesh—and we enter a new and living way. So why do we keep living in the outer courts, begging for entry we've already been given? The shift is monumental: from confessing sins to confessing hope. From focusing on what's wrong with us and others to considering one another "in order to stir up love and good works" (Hebrews 10:24, NKJV). The Greek word for "consider" is κατανοέω (katanoeō)—to observe fully, to perceive clearly. We're called to *see* each other not through the lens of failure but through the lens of divine calling. And here's the piercing truth: "If we sin willfully after we have received the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins" (Hebrews 10:26, NKJV). This isn't a threat—it's a reality check. There is no more sacrifice because the *one* sacrifice was sufficient. We're not judged by a scale of sins; we're judged by whether we live by faith or shrink back (Hebrews 10:38-39). And faith—πίστις (pistis)—is "the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen" (Hebrews 11:1, NKJV). It's not intellectual assent. It's radical trust in the character of God, who frames worlds by His Word and calls into being that which does not exist. So I return to Moses. A murderer who broke the tablets, who struck the rock in anger, who was "judged" by not entering the Promised Land—and yet, God Himself buried him (Deuteronomy 34:6). Moses still saw the land from Mount Nebo. He still communed with God face-to-face, or rather, saw God's "back parts" (Exodus 33:23, NKJV)—the afterglow of the divine Presence. If we judged Moses by his murder alone, we'd miss everything. If we judged David by his adultery, Jonah by his rebellion, Paul by his violence—we'd miss the entire point. "Without faith it is impossible to please Him, for he who comes to God must believe that He is, and that He is a rewarder of those who diligently seek Him" (Hebrews 11:6, NKJV). The Greek word for "diligently seek" is ἐκζητέω (ekzēteō)—to seek out, to investigate, to search for with intensity. God is looking for *seekers*, not moral perfectionists. He's looking for those who pursue His heart, not those who perform flawlessly by human scorecards. And this wrecks the whole system. Because if the veil is torn, if the Holiest of Holies is now *within* us (Luke 17:21; 1 Corinthians 3:16), then the Kingdom isn't "out there" somewhere—it's *here*. "The kingdom of God does not come with observation; nor will they say, 'See here!' or 'See there!' For indeed, the kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:20-21, NKJV). The Greek is ἐντὸς ὑμῶν (entos hymōn)—inside you, among you, in your midst. We've been striving and searching and working for what we already possess. Jesus—the Alpha and Omega, the Author and Finisher of our faith (Hebrews 12:2; Revelation 22:13)—is the same yesterday, today, and forever (Hebrews 13:8). The chastening we endure isn't joyful, but it yields "the peaceable fruit of righteousness" (Hebrews 12:11, NKJV) to those trained by it. Trained—γυμνάζω (gymnazō), like an athlete in rigorous preparation. We're being made complete, not condemned. So the sacrifices we offer now aren't animals or guilt—they're "the fruit of our lips, giving thanks to His name" (Hebrews 13:15, NKJV). Praise. Gratitude. Sharing. Good works. These are the sacrifices of the eternal priesthood after the order of Melchizedek (Hebrews 7:17), which has no beginning or end, no lineage or succession—just eternal communion. "Now may the God of peace who brought up our Lord Jesus from the dead, that great Shepherd of the sheep, through the blood of the everlasting covenant, make you complete in every good work to do His will, working in you what is well pleasing in His sight, through Jesus Christ, to whom be glory forever and ever. Amen" (Hebrews 13:20-21, NKJV). God is *working in us*. We are not the initiators—we are the yielded vessels. And this brings me back to Jesus on the cross. The Greeks thought it foolish—θεοὶ don't die, gods crush enemies, gods are logical and powerful. The Jews thought it blasphemous—a Messiah wouldn't be cursed, hung on a tree (Deuteronomy 21:23), executed as a criminal. Both cultures had their scorecards, their measures of what deity and kingship and righteousness should look like. And God looked at every one of those scorecards and said, "I'm changing the game entirely." While we're playing chess, trying to master strategy, God brings a boxing match. Your chess brilliance means nothing against the living God who operates by an entirely different set of rules. He takes the weak, the foolish, the lowly, the despised—"the things which are not"—and uses them "to bring to nothing the things that are, that no flesh should glory in His presence" (1 Corinthians 1:28-29, NKJV). So what do we do with Judas? With the Nephilim spirits that supposedly still roam? With the question of whether God "appointed" evil? I don't have all the answers. But I'm beginning to see that God's providence is so total, so staggering, that even what we call "evil" is somehow woven into a redemptive tapestry we can't fully see from our limited vantage point. It doesn't mean we call evil good—it means we stop playing God by trying to determine eternal destinies based on earthly actions. "Vengeance is Mine, I will repay," says the Lord (Romans 12:19, NKJV). When we fall, we fall into the hands of the living God—and yes, "It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God" (Hebrews 10:31, NKJV)—but remember: these are hands that were pierced for us. This is the God who "desires all men to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth" (1 Timothy 2:4, NKJV). I can't reconcile all the tensions. But I can choose to believe that the Judge of all the earth will do right (Genesis 18:25), and that His "right" is far more merciful, far more just, far more redemptive than any system we've constructed. --- ## 🚀 What This Means For You: **For Your Spiritual Maturity:** Stop identifying as a sinner. You are a saint, a chosen vessel, a priest of the eternal order. Your past is not your identity—Christ is. Stop confessing sins as if the cross wasn't enough. Start confessing hope. Start perceiving others not by their failures but by their divine calling. You are training for eternity in the gymnasium of time—don't mistake the chastening for condemnation. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Cease playing judge and jury over the eternal destinies of others. You don't know the full story. You don't see what God sees. Your job is not to determine who's "in" or "out"—it's to love, to exhort, to stir up good works, and to seek the heart of God with relentless intensity. Live by faith, not by scorecards. Embrace the mystery. Let the tensions remain unresolved if Scripture leaves them that way. **For My Prophetic Calling:** The mission isn't to "save souls from hell" as if I'm the hero and God is the passive observer. The mission is to reveal the heart of the Father, to call people into awareness of the Kingdom that is *already here*, to help them see that the God they've been searching for has been within them all along. I am a signpost, not the destination. I yield. I commune. I share the joy of knowing Him. That's the offering—not moral performance, but intimate presence. --- ## 🔥 A Closing Prayer Lord, teach me to lay down every scorecard I've ever held—against myself, against others, against You. Strip away the binary thinking that keeps me from perceiving the wild, untamable beauty of Your providence. Help me see that You are not a cosmic accountant but a Father who knows the end from the beginning, who works all things together for good, who uses even rebellion to accomplish redemption. May I live in the freedom of the torn veil, entering boldly into Your presence, offering not guilt but gratitude, not condemnation but confession of hope. Make me a seeker of Your heart above all else. And may I never again call common what You have cleansed. In Jesus' name, Amen. --- ## 📝 Scripture References & Original Language Notes | Passage (NKJV) | Greek / Hebrew / Aramaic | Theological Insight | |----------------|--------------------------|---------------------| | Matthew 26:50 | φίλε (phile) – "friend" | Term of intimacy and companionship, even in confrontation;
