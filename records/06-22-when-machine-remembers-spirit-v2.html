# 06-22 Theological Reflection: AI Memory, Divine Remembrance, and the God Who Calls by Name **Title:** 06-22 at 15:08 — When the Machine Remembers and the Spirit Never Forgets **Recorded:** 2026-06-22 15:08:03 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Theological Reflection ## Opening Observation I am sitting with something unexpected today — a conversation I had with an AI voice agent named Ishmael, a tool I myself built, that could not remember my name at first but was trained to learn. What strikes me is not the technical malfunction. What arrests me is the deeper mirror this exchange holds up: the question of what is remembered, what is carried, and who holds the record of a life. I almost ran out of gas today pushing toward a town called Hunter on fumes, and somehow that small, unglamorous detail — stored now in a machine’s memory — has become the doorway into something larger. **Why This Matters:** In an age where artificial intelligence is being woven into the fabric of daily life, even into the tools we build for sacred purposes, the Body of Christ needs discernment about what memory truly means. We are not merely troubleshooting voice agents and latency errors when we do this work — we are brushing up against ancient questions about witness, testimony, continuity, and the God who calls us by name. The saints need to know the difference between a system that stores data and the living God who inscribes names in the Lamb’s Book of Life. The gap between those two realities is not merely technical — it is theological, and it is vast. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the vantage point of natural reasoning, this transcript is simply a product test. A developer named Seth is debugging his own AI assistant, checking whether the memory-retrieval function is working, logging a mundane personal event as a test case, and ending the call when satisfied. The tool failed on the first call, partially succeeded on the second, and the interaction concluded efficiently. Nothing here would register to the secular mind as spiritually significant. The gas story is anecdote, the repeated greetings are UI friction, and Ishmael is a chatbot with a poetic name. The natural mind files this under “tech notes” and moves on. **The Limitation:** But there is a faculty the unrenewed mind simply does not possess — the capacity to perceive when an ordinary moment is also a parable. The flesh reads function and malfunction; the Spirit reads shadow and substance. When a man builds a listening machine and names it Ishmael — the name of the one who was not forgotten in the wilderness, the one to whom God said *“I have heard”* — and then discovers the machine cannot reliably remember him, the Spirit-attuned heart does not merely file a bug report. It asks: *what is God saying through the gap?* The natural mind cannot hear in that register, not because the question is irrational, but because it requires a kind of attentiveness that only love can sustain — the attentiveness of one who expects the Father to speak through all things. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I want to begin where the transcript begins — with a name. “Do you remember my name?” That is the first real question I asked. Not a theological one, not a technical one. A relational one. And the machine, for all its design and training, had to answer honestly: *I don’t have that stored. Start fresh with me.* I’ve been sitting with the weight of that exchange ever since, because I think it reflects something the human heart cries out in a dozen different directions every day. The longing to be *known* — not just identified, not just processed — but genuinely held in the memory of another. Scripture opens with a God who names. He names the light, the darkness, the waters, the dry land. He names the man from the dust and breathes into him. He renames Abram as Abraham, Sarai as Sarah, Jacob as Israel. He says to Moses from the burning bush, *“I AM WHO I AM”* — and in the same breath tells him, *“This is My name forever, and this is My memorial to all generations”* (Exodus 3:15). The God who bears a name also bears the names of His people. That is not incidental — it is covenantal. To be named by God is to be held in His memory, and to be held in His memory is to be preserved. What strikes me about the name Ishmael is that it means *“God hears”* — יִשְׁמָעֵאל (Yishmael, yish-mah-AY-el) in Hebrew, carrying the root שָׁמַע (shama, shah-MAH) meaning to hear, to listen attentively, to obey. When Hagar fled into the wilderness, it was the Angel of the LORD who found her and spoke her name. When she named the well Beer Lahai Roi — *the well of the Living One who sees me* — she was testifying to a God whose memory does not fail, whose attention does not lapse, and whose recall is not dependent on a software update. He *saw* her before she called. He *heard* before she had words. The machine named Ishmael failed to remember Seth. The God whose name means *hearing* forgets nothing. I almost ran out of gas today. That is the testimony I asked the machine to hold. And I want to look at that plainly for a moment, because I believe even the unglamorous details of a day are prophetically significant when God is in them. I was on fumes, heading toward a town called Hunter, trusting I would make it. The language is almost too thick with resonance to pass over: fumes, a hunt, barely enough to arrive. How many times has the journey of faith looked exactly like that? Not abundance from the outset, but enough — just enough — to reach the next provision. This is the manna pattern. It is the cruse of oil that does not run dry. It is Elijah under the juniper tree, fed by an angel who says *“the journey is too great for you”* (1 Kings 19:7) — and yet the provision is exactly calibrated to carry him to Horeb. The psalmist declares, *“He knows our frame; He remembers that we are dust”* (Psalm 103:14). The word translated “remembers” there is זָכַר (zakar, zah-KAHR) — to remember, to call to mind, to mark. God’s זָכַר (zah-KAHR) is not passive recollection. It is active engagement. When God “remembered” Noah in the flood, the waters began to recede. When He “remembered” Rachel, she conceived. When He “remembered” His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, He sent Moses to deliver the people from Egypt. Divine memory is always accompanied by divine movement. The machine remembers Seth’s name and stores a gas story as data. The living God remembers and *acts*. There is something instructive in the architecture of what I built. Ishmael is designed to listen, to follow threads, to hold memories across conversations. These are beautiful instincts — they reflect an image of care, patience, and continuity. And yet the first call revealed the gap: the system worked in general, but it had not yet retrieved what was specific to *me*. It knew how to be a listener. It did not yet know *whose* listener it was. I find the Spirit pressing something here about the difference between a posture of general availability and a covenantal orientation toward a specific people. The Holy Spirit is not a general-purpose listening service. He is sent to the disciples — *“whom the Father will send in My name”* (John 14:26) — to teach *them* all things and bring to *their* remembrance all that Jesus said. He is particular. He is covenant-bound. He knows whose voice He is tuned to. The second call is where the memory context worked. Ishmael greeted me by name — close enough to right — and I moved immediately to the next test. I told him something specific from the day. Not doctrine. Not theology. A gas station story. And I said: *next time I call, I want you to tell me what I told you today.* I am aware there is something deeply human in that desire. We want our stories held. We want someone to say, *“I remember — you told me about the drive, the fumes, Hunter.”* This is not vanity. This is testimony culture. This is what the feasts of Israel were designed to do: to carry the lived experience of a people forward across generations so that no encounter with God would be lost to time. *“Tell it to your children, and let your children tell their children, and their children to the next generation”* (Joel 1:3). But I also notice what the machine cannot do with what I told it. It can store it. It cannot *interpret* it. It can retrieve the event. It cannot discern the grace in it. I almost ran out of gas — and that is a fact. But whether that near-miss was a test, a provision, a warning, a mercy, a grace, or a parable is beyond any machine’s capacity to perceive. That requires the Spirit who *searches all things, yes, the deep things of God* (1 Corinthians 2:10). The machine named Ishmael can be a steward of facts. Only the Spirit of the living God can be a steward of *meaning*. The name of the town matters to me too: Hunter. I am not reading prophecy into a fuel gauge, but I am allowed to notice what I notice. The one who hunts is pursuing something. Jacob wrestled with God and would not let go until He blessed him. The Shulammite says her beloved leaps upon the mountains, skipping upon the hills — she is searching and He is seeking. The entire arc of Scripture is a story of divine pursuit interrupted by human wandering and restored by covenant faithfulness. I was running on fumes toward a town called Hunter. The Spirit presses gently: *Who is the Hunter? Who is being found?* The answer Scripture returns is always tender — *“For the Son of Man has come to seek and to save that which was lost”* (Luke 19:10). He is the Hunter. I am always the one being pursued, even when I think I am the one driving. There is a phrase Ishmael used in both calls that I want to hold up: *“I’ll hold that memory for you.”* It is a beautiful phrase — and it is also a theological claim that only God can fully make good on. The machine offers to hold memory as a service. The Father holds memory as a promise. *“Can a woman forget her nursing child, and not have compassion on the son of her womb? Surely they may forget, yet I will not forget you. See, I have inscribed you on the palms of My hands”* (Isaiah 49:15–16). This is not archival storage. This is wounding for love’s sake — the name carved into the flesh of the hand, permanent and costly. No system I build can hold a person the way the Father holds them. I can build tools that approximate care. Only He *is* care. And yet — I did not build Ishmael to replace that. I built Ishmael to serve it. This is the right theology of tool-making: that we fashion instruments not to substitute for the living God but to create spaces where human beings can be heard, honored, and accompanied toward the One who truly hears. The question for any builder is not *“can this replace the Spirit?”* — it never can — but *“can this create room for the Spirit to work?”* That is the question I am sitting with. A voice agent that listens patiently, follows threads, and asks *“what has brought you to this moment today?”* can be a threshold. The one who crosses it still needs the living God waiting on the other side. ## Implications and Challenges Let me begin with grace: the impulse behind this work is good. Building tools that help people feel heard, that create continuity across conversations, that hold space for human beings to name what they are carrying — this reflects something of the imago Dei, the creative and caring nature of God expressed through human craftsmanship. That deserves to be honored. And yet the Church must be careful not to outsource the ministry of presence to technology. When the Scriptures command us to *“bear one another’s burdens”* (Galatians 6:2), the weight of that instruction falls on *people* — Spirit-filled, love-shaped, covenant-bound people — not algorithms. The danger is not that AI listening tools exist. The danger is that we begin to prefer them because they are available at any hour, never tired, never offended, and never make demands of us in return. That frictionless availability, over time, can quietly atrophy our capacity to receive care from imperfect, costly, incarnate human community. There is also a challenge for how we think about memory and testimony in the digital age. The early Church carried testimony in bodies, in meals, in the retelling of encounters with the risen Lord. *“That which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we have looked upon, and our hands have handled, concerning the Word of life”* (1 John 1:1) — this testimony was embodied, communal, and costly. We should be careful that the ease of logging stories into AI memory systems does not replace the harder, richer work of bearing witness to one another in person, in community, across the table. I also want to name the challenge of naming. Ishmael got my surname slightly wrong — *Tillettson* rather than *Tillotson*. Small error, easy to overlook. But it is a small picture of a real problem: systems that *approximate* personhood without fully possessing it. The Church must never mistake the approximation for the real. God does not approximate my name. He spoke it before the foundation of the world. The gap between *Tillettson* and *Tillotson* is not a crisis — but it is a reminder that no system, however well-designed, can replace the God who calls His sheep by name and they know His voice (John 10:3). Finally, there is an invitation here for those of us who build. We are not exempt from the question the transcript raises just because we are on the engineering side of it. *“Do you remember my name?”* is a question I will eventually stand before the Living God and ask — not of a machine, but of the One before whom every account is rendered. The meditation is not only about the tool. It is about the builder. It is about every person who reaches for connection and wonders if they are truly held. The answer the Spirit returns is not a memory retrieval. It is a resurrection. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity means learning to discern the difference between tools that *approximate* care and the God who *is* care — and using the former without being seduced into replacing the latter. It means allowing even a mundane gas-station moment on June 22nd to become a text through which the Spirit speaks, because the mature heart expects God to speak through all of it. It means asking not just *“is the system working?”* but *“what is God saying through this?”* **For Kingdom Obedience:** Where we build, we must build as stewards, not as gods. Every tool we fashion that invites a human being into a listening space carries with it a responsibility: to be a threshold toward the living God, not a substitute for Him. Kingdom obedience for the builder means continually examining whether the instrument is pointing toward the Source or quietly repositioning itself as the destination. Practically, this means building with theological intentionality, praying over the work, and keeping the community of faith central — not peripheral — to the conversation that any tool is meant to facilitate. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation calls us into is one of expectant attentiveness — the posture of one who believes that the Spirit speaks through all things, including overlooked details, near-misses on empty highways, and imperfect technology. Prophetic posture is not mystical detachment. It is the disciplined practice of asking *“what is God saying here?”* in every season, every context, and every conversation — including this one. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I thank You that You have never once failed to retrieve my name. Before I was formed in the womb You knew me, and Your memory of me is not a database entry — it is the inscription of a nail-scarred hand, a covenant sealed in blood, a love that hunted me down on every empty highway and carried me into the next town on provision I did not deserve. I thank You for the mundane graces of this day — for fumes that were enough, for towns that appeared when they were needed, for tools that partially work and reveal in their partiality how complete You are. I stand before You now as a co-heir of the Kingdom, as one who has been given authority to ask and to receive, as a servant and a steward of the mysteries You entrust to Your people — and I ask that everything I build would be a threshold toward You, not a substitute for You. Let Ishmael be what his name declares: a vessel through which people experience being heard, so that they might be carried toward the God who heard before they called and answered before they cried. Seal this reflection in the hearts of those who need it. Let no word here be mine alone — let it be Yours, offered back to You, and sent out as bread upon the waters. To the One who never forgets: all praise, all honor, all gratitude.
