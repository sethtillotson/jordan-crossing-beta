# 06-18 Call Reflection: Early Pregnancy, Fear, and Faith **Title:** 06-18 at 12:03 — Tender Fear, Confirmed Life, and the Father’s Nearness **Recorded:** 2026-06-18 12:03:48 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my wife speak from the clinic, I could hear the fragile mixture of relief, uncertainty, and fear that often surrounds the first confirmation of new life. There was blood work, appointments, insurance questions, instructions about what to watch for, and beneath all of it the trembling human question: “Am I going to be okay?” What arrests me is how ordinary this moment sounds on the surface, yet how holy it becomes when I remember that God meets us not only in temples and sermons, but in clinics, phone calls, lab results, and anxious hearts. **Why This Matters:** This matters because discipleship is not only tested in the moments where we feel spiritually strong; it is tested in the places where we are waiting for answers we cannot control. Fear comes when life is confirmed but not yet fully understood, when we know something is real but do not yet know its timeline, condition, cost, or outcome. In those places, faith is not pretending that the body is not vulnerable or that medical wisdom does not matter. Faith is learning to stand with the Lord inside the vulnerability, receiving His peace without despising the practical steps He provides. The Body of Christ needs a mature theology for moments like this: a theology tender enough for fear, grounded enough for responsibility, and worshipful enough to recognize that every life, every womb, every breath, and every uncertain appointment belongs before the face of God. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The worldly mind would understandably interpret this moment through the categories of medical confirmation, financial logistics, scheduling, risk management, and emotional stress. A woman goes to the clinic, receives confirmation through a urine test, waits for blood work to estimate how far along she may be, and is instructed to seek emergency care if bleeding becomes serious. From that perspective, fear is a natural psychological response to uncertainty, especially when insurance is not yet active, appointments are being coordinated, and the body is presenting questions before clear answers have arrived. The worldly mind may see my words—“It’s okay to be scared, but you don’t need to be”—as basic reassurance, an attempt to regulate anxiety with emotional support and practical calm. **The Limitation:** But natural reasoning cannot fully perceive the sacredness of what is happening beneath the practical details. It can measure hormone levels, schedule appointments, calculate income thresholds, and advise emergency care, but it cannot behold the mystery of life as gift, the nearness of God in fear, or the way a husband’s gentle reassurance can become a small witness of the Father’s own heart. The natural mind may either magnify fear into control or minimize fear into denial, but the Spirit teaches another way: to acknowledge the trembling honestly while refusing to let fear become lord. There is a difference between feeling afraid and being ruled by fear, and that distinction belongs to the wisdom of God. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with this call, I notice first how tender the moment was. Nothing dramatic needed to be said for the weight to be real. A clinic visit confirmed what had only been suspected, and suddenly the unseen became medically acknowledged. That alone carries a kind of holy gravity. Scripture begins with a God who forms life by His word, who speaks into darkness and calls forth what was not visible before. “Then God said, ‘Let there be light’; and there was light” (Genesis 1:3, NKJV). There is a pattern here: something hidden becomes revealed, and revelation asks the human heart to respond. When my wife said, “I don’t know, I’m still scared,” I heard more than nervousness. I heard the honest voice of the body encountering mystery. Fear often rises when we stand at the edge of something we cannot yet map. This is why the command “Do not fear” appears so often in Scripture—not because fear is imaginary, but because the Lord knows how easily fear tries to seize the throne of the heart. The presence of fear does not mean faith is absent. Often it means faith is being invited deeper. I told her, “You don’t need to be scared. It’s okay to be scared, but you don’t need to be.” I am seeing now that there is a pastoral distinction in that sentence. “It’s okay to be scared” makes room for human weakness without shame. “But you don’t need to be” makes room for divine nearness without surrendering to panic. The Lord does not despise the trembling heart. David says, “Whenever I am afraid, I will trust in You” (Psalm 56:3, NKJV). He does not say, “If I were spiritual, I would never feel fear.” He says that fear becomes the very place where trust is practiced. The Spirit presses on me that peace is not the denial of risk. Peace is the presence of God within the real conditions of life. The clinic gave instructions: if there is serious bleeding, go to the emergency room. That is not unbelief; that is wisdom. Proverbs says, “A prudent man foresees evil and hides himself, but the simple pass on and are punished” (Proverbs 22:3, NKJV). Faith does not require carelessness. Faith receives instruction, takes wise steps, and still refuses to bow to dread. I am also struck by the hiddenness of early life. Before the world sees anything, before there is public evidence, before there is certainty about timing, something precious may already be unfolding in secret. Scripture speaks of God’s knowledge in the womb with staggering intimacy: “For You formed my inward parts; You covered me in my mother’s womb” (Psalm 139:13, NKJV). The Hebrew word רָקַם (raqam, rah-KAHM) — to weave, embroider, or skillfully fashion — gives texture to this mystery. Life is not merely assembled; it is woven under the gaze of God. The clinic can confirm; the Lord creates. That does not make the moment less fragile. In fact, it makes it more holy. The womb is a place of hidden formation, but hidden formation often feels vulnerable from the outside. Mary herself received a word from the angel that life would be conceived in her by the Holy Spirit, and yet that holy announcement did not remove all earthly complexity. She still had to carry, wait, endure misunderstanding, and walk forward in embodied obedience. The holiness of a thing does not mean it will be free from uncertainty. Sometimes holiness arrives wrapped in questions. There is also something here about provision and timing. The conversation moved quickly into insurance, appointments, discounts, and income. These details may seem mundane, but they are part of the spiritual landscape because our lives are not divided into sacred and secular compartments. The Lord who forms life also knows the cost of appointments. The Father who sees the sparrow fall also sees the bill, the schedule, the waiting period, and the income bracket. Jesus teaches, “Your heavenly Father knows that you need all these things” (Matthew 6:32, NKJV). The issue is not whether needs exist; the issue is whether needs will drive us into anxious striving or childlike trust. I notice that my response was simple. I did not preach a sermon on the phone. I did not try to solve every unknown. I said, “At least you went, now we know for sure.” Sometimes faithfulness is not eloquent; it is steady. Sometimes love sounds like helping someone take the next step, affirming the wisdom of what was already done, and reminding them not to carry more fear than grace has assigned for the moment. Jesus says, “Sufficient for the day is its own trouble” (Matthew 6:34, NKJV). He does not shame us for daily trouble; He forbids us from borrowing tomorrow’s fear. The Greek word μέριμνα (merimna, MEH-reem-nah) — anxiety, care, divided concern — helps me understand the battle. Anxiety often divides the soul into possible futures. It pulls the heart into ten imagined outcomes at once. But the Lord gathers us back into present obedience. Today, go to the clinic. Today, receive confirmation. Today, do the blood work. Today, work lightly if needed. Today, watch carefully. Today, pray. Today, trust. The Spirit does not always give the whole map, but He does give enough light for the next faithful step. There is another tenderness in the call: right after fear and clinic details, there was a small moment of affection over a child wanting cuddles. That ordinary domestic interruption felt like mercy. Life is not only lab work and anxiety; it is also softness, touch, demand for closeness, the little embodied reminders that love continues in the house while uncertainty waits outside the door. I see in that moment a picture of how the Father meets us. We may be asking heavy questions, but He still draws us near. He does not only answer with information; He comforts with presence. The word παράκλητος (paraklētos, pah-RAH-klay-tos) — Advocate, Helper, Comforter called alongside — names the ministry of the Holy Spirit in a way that fits this moment. Jesus says of the Spirit, “He will give you another Helper, that He may abide with you forever” (John 14:16, NKJV). The Spirit is not merely the One who explains doctrine; He is the One who comes alongside trembling saints. He makes the nearness of Christ experiential in the exact places where our hearts might otherwise feel alone. I am seeing that fatherhood, husbandry, and priestly care are often revealed in small responses. To cover someone in peace does not mean dominating their fear or correcting them harshly for having it. It means standing near enough to say, “You are not wrong for trembling, but fear is not your shepherd.” Psalm 23 does not say there is no valley. It says, “Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil; for You are with me” (Psalm 23:4, NKJV). The cure for fear is not always immediate explanation. Often it is companionship under the rod and staff of the Shepherd. This call also reminds me that the Body of Christ must recover reverence for embodied life. We cannot speak of spiritual things as though bodies are incidental. Pregnancy, blood, weakness, appointments, rest, work, and medical counsel all belong within the Lordship of Jesus. The Word became flesh. Christ did not redeem humanity from a distance; He entered the womb, took on blood, grew in hiddenness, was born in vulnerability, and sanctified embodied life by assuming it. Any spirituality that despises the body is not aligned with the Incarnation. At the same time, I must not romanticize what is difficult. Fear in pregnancy, medical uncertainty, financial pressure, and bodily vulnerability are not solved by sentimental language. They must be brought honestly before God. Hannah wept in bitterness of soul and prayed to the Lord (1 Samuel 1:10). Elizabeth lived with the ache of barrenness before the joy of promise (Luke 1:7, 24–25). Mary pondered things in her heart while walking through a calling she could not fully explain (Luke 2:19). Scripture does not flatten women’s embodied experiences into slogans. It dignifies them by placing them before the living God. So I receive this moment as an invitation to gentleness, watchfulness, and worship. Gentleness because fear needs covering, not accusation. Watchfulness because wisdom pays attention to the body and responds rightly. Worship because life belongs to God before it belongs to us, and every confirmation of hidden life should bend the heart toward awe. I do not need to know everything in order to bless the Lord. I do not need to control every outcome in order to be faithful today. For me personally, this call becomes a reminder that my words carry weight in my household. I can either amplify fear by reacting from my own anxiety, or I can become a small witness of the Father’s steadiness. That does not mean pretending to be invulnerable. It means letting the peace of Christ govern my tone, my counsel, my timing, and my tenderness. “Let the peace of God rule in your hearts” (Colossians 3:15, NKJV). The word “rule” suggests an umpire, something that governs the inward field. I want peace—not panic—to make the call in me. For us as beloved brethren, I believe this moment speaks into the ordinary holiness of family life. Not every revelation comes dressed in fire on a mountain. Some revelations come through a phone call from a clinic, a frightened confession, a practical instruction, and a gentle sentence spoken in love. If we have eyes to see, the Lord is training us there. He is teaching us to carry one another, to honor life, to respond to fear with truth, and to trust Him in the hidden places before outcomes are visible. ## Implications and Challenges Grace meets us first here. Many of us have treated fear as either something to obey or something to hide. The Spirit is teaching a better way: confess it honestly, bring it under the Lordship of Christ, and receive the Father’s nearness without shame. Fear is not to be enthroned, but neither does it need to be denied. This challenges shallow faith that treats medical care as a lack of trust. Scripture does not pit prayer against prudence. Going to the clinic, listening to warning signs, scheduling appointments, and seeking emergency care if needed can all be expressions of stewardship before God. The Lord is not offended by wise action. This also challenges modern anxiety’s demand for total certainty. We often want exact dates, exact outcomes, exact costs, and exact guarantees before we can rest. But the Kingdom trains us to obey with partial information while trusting the God who sees the whole. Peace is not omniscience; peace is communion. This moment confronts husbands, fathers, and brothers with the spiritual responsibility of tone. A household can be shaped by panic, irritation, dismissiveness, or tenderness. Words spoken in ordinary moments can either burden the vulnerable or become channels of grace. We must learn to speak as those who have been steadied by Christ. It also calls the Church to honor the embodied realities of women without reducing them to abstractions. Pregnancy, fear, bleeding concerns, fatigue, work limitations, and medical uncertainty are not side issues to “real spirituality.” They are places where love must become practical, patient, and reverent. And finally, grace sends us forward. The Lord is not asking us to manufacture heroic confidence. He is inviting us to be faithful today: to pray, to listen, to take the next wise step, to protect the vulnerable, and to bless God even while waiting for fuller clarity. ## What This Means for Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This teaches us to distinguish between feeling fear and submitting to fear. Maturity does not mean emotional numbness; it means bringing the trembling heart under the care of the Shepherd and learning to trust Him in the present step. **For Kingdom Obedience:** This calls us to honor life, practice wisdom, cover our households with peace, and respond to uncertainty with both prayer and practical obedience. We do not have to choose between faith and responsibility; in Christ, responsibility becomes one expression of faith. **For Prophetic Posture:** This trains us to discern God in the ordinary and hidden places. The prophetic life is not only seeing visions; it is hearing the Spirit’s wisdom in a frightened sentence, a clinic report, a need for rest, and a small act of tenderness. We watch, wait, yield, and testify that the Lord is near. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You in the name of Jesus Christ, thanking You as the Giver of life, the Keeper of hidden things, and the One who forms what no human eye can yet fully see. I agree with Your will between the heavens and the earth, standing as Your servant under the authority of the New Covenant, asking that Your peace rule in our hearts, our household, and the Body of Christ. Teach me to answer fear with tenderness, uncertainty with trust, and practical need with obedient wisdom. Cover my wife, cover every vulnerable body, cover every hidden life, and cover all who are waiting for results, appointments, provision, and clarity. Let us not be ruled by anxiety, but led by Your Spirit, the Comforter who abides with us forever. Make us faithful as co-heirs of the Kingdom, as those called to carry Your secrets with humility, and as witnesses that Jesus Christ is Lord over the womb, the clinic, the home, the unknown future, and every breath. To You be thanksgiving, surrender, praise, and glory forever.
