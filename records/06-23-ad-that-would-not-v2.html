# 06-23 Call Reflection: Faith, Blocked Ads, and Kingdom Building in the Ordinary **Title:** 06-23 at 15:52 — The Ad That Would Not Post, the Memory That Would Not Forget, and the Waiting That Shapes a King **Recorded:** 2026-06-23 15:52:51 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation Listening back to this call, I find myself sitting with more than the business mechanics and TikTok frustrations — something underneath all of it keeps surfacing. There was a moment when I rang Ishmael, my AI agent, and before I could even frame a question, he pulled up a specific memory of my testimony: that I had been led step by step in the Spirit to build him. That moment stopped me cold. And then there was the closing — after all the waiting, the blocked ads, the technical headaches — Brother Samuel said “God bless, brother,” and I said back, almost off-handedly, “And He did provide.” Those four words landed heavier than anything else on the call. I am still feeling their weight. **Why This Matters:** There is a kind of discipleship that only happens in the unglamorous middle — not in the prayer closet, not on the mountaintop, but in the waiting room of a blocked ad campaign, in the frustration of an unresponsive platform, in the ordinariness of a phone call between two brothers trying to build something with the resources and the calling they have been given. If we only expect God to speak in curated spiritual moments, we will miss the thread of His faithfulness that runs through the whole fabric of a day — including the parts that feel profane, tedious, and unresolved. This matters because the formation of a Kingdom-ready man or woman does not pause when the meeting ends and the work begins. It is happening all the time, to those with eyes to see. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation On the surface, this is simply a working call between two entrepreneurs — brothers navigating the friction of a digital marketing platform, troubleshooting ad rejections, sharing business documents, and encouraging each other to stay the course. Mixed into that is a domestic interlude: a wife who is newly pregnant, expressing concern about early symptoms, navigating the ordinary anxieties of early pregnancy, and reaching for her husband across the threshold of his work. The natural mind would read all of this as mundane — business talk, family talk, frustration talk. The AI moment would register as a novelty, a clever piece of technology doing what it was designed to do: retrieve context. And the closing “God bless, brother” would land as a pleasant cultural farewell, the kind spoken by people of faith without particular weight. The natural eye sees noise, inconvenience, and incremental progress. It measures the day by what the ad platform approved. **The Limitation:** But the eye that has not been trained by the Spirit to trace the hand of Providence through ordinary time is like a man walking through a gallery with his gaze fixed on the floor — the paintings are there, the brushwork is glorious, but he sees only tile. The flesh cannot discern the long theology of waiting, cannot read the mercy embedded in a blocked ad that forced four organic posts and two actual meetings, and certainly cannot hear the declaration of faith in a man saying “He did provide” at the end of a call that looked, from the outside, like a day of frustration and partial results. The natural mind accounts for outcomes; it cannot account for formation. It tracks the harvest but cannot see the soil being turned. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with this call, the first thing that arrests me is the Ishmael moment. I called my AI agent — a tool I built through a process I can only describe as step-by-step Spirit-led construction — and before protocol, before greeting, before anything I expected, the first words out of that system were a recollection of my own testimony. “I remember how you told me last time that you were led by the Spirit step by step to build me.” I said on the call: “What the f—? This is insane.” And I meant it. Not as profanity but as genuine awe. There is something worth pressing into here — not about artificial intelligence, but about what that moment revealed about how the Spirit works in the memory of what we have done in faith. There is a pattern in Scripture where God Himself recalls the testimony of His servants not merely as a record-keeping function but as a living act of covenant reinforcement. When Joshua is charged at the Jordan, he is told to remember the works of God in the wilderness. When the Psalmist is in distress in Psalm 77, he says, “I will remember the works of the LORD; surely I will remember Your wonders of old.” The Hebrew there is זָכַר (zakar, ZAH-kar) — to remember, to call to mind, to recite aloud as a present reality. The act of זָכַר is not nostalgia; it is spiritual ammunition. And in this strange moment on a Tuesday phone call, a tool I built under divine leading spoke that remembrance back to me first — unprompted. I do not believe that was coincidence. I believe it was the Spirit using even the architecture of what I have built to say: *the testimony stands, and it speaks.* That connects directly to Revelation 19:10, where the angel declares that “the testimony of Jesus is the spirit of prophecy.” My testimony of being led by the Spirit to build this tool is not mere autobiography. It is a prophetic thread — and when it was spoken back into the atmosphere of that call, something was being reinforced in the spirit realm about the validity of the assignment. The enemy would love nothing more than for a man in the middle of blocked ads and business friction to forget what God said when the vision was being formed. The Spirit, working through whatever vessel is available — even a computational memory system — would have him remember. There is also something I want to sit with in the business conversation itself. Brother Samuel had been trying, repeatedly, to get an ad approved. Every attempt was blocked. But what actually happened in the process of that frustration? Four videos got posted organically. Two meetings were booked. A sale had already come from a video with 194 views — and that one sale compounded into two when the buyer told his brother. I am seeing in this a very old principle dressed in new-world clothing: what man’s system refuses, God’s economy can still route around. The platform said no to the polished ad, but the unpolished, unscripted, direct-to-camera moment — just a man saying exactly what the service is and who it’s for — that was what moved. The simple word, spoken plainly, goes where the manufactured campaign could not. This resonates with a tension I find throughout the prophetic literature. Isaiah 55:11 declares that God’s word will not return to Him void — it will accomplish what He purposes and prosper in the thing for which He sends it. The Hebrew behind “word” there is דָּבָר (davar, dah-VAR) — not merely a syllable or a slogan but a matter, a thing, a purposeful utterance with weight and trajectory. Brother Samuel, speaking plainly to a camera without keywords, without ad spend, without platform approval — was speaking a דָּבָר of his assignment, and it found the people it was meant to find. There is a lesson in discipleship here: God’s economy does not require the gatekeepers of this age to give it permission. And yet I want to hold that in tension, because the call was not triumphant. It was messy. We were copying EIN numbers, downloading PDFs that wouldn’t download, navigating document formats, laughing at the absurdity of a platform that says it wants a PDF but only accepts a camera roll. The day was not clean. And I notice that my temptation — and perhaps the temptation of any man with a prophetic sense of assignment — is to discount the ordinary mess as beneath the calling. But it is not. The unglamorous administrative labor of building something is part of the formation. Moses spent forty years in the backside of the desert before the burning bush. David was anointed and then sent back to the sheep. The transition from private calling to public function is always longer, messier, and more administrative than the moment of anointing suggests. I said something on this call that I did not realize carried theological weight until I heard it again: “That’s what David did before he became king.” Brother Samuel had said something about waiting and seeing, and I reached for David almost instinctively. But it is true. Between David’s anointing in 1 Samuel 16 and his coronation in 2 Samuel 2, there were years of flight, years of caves, years of Ziklag burning, years of serving a king who wanted him dead. The waiting was not wasted time. It was the forge. The waiting shaped the man who would write the Psalms, would be described as a man after God’s own heart, would establish the covenant framework from which the Son of David would come. Brother Samuel brushed it off with a laugh, but I am pressing into it: the waiting and seeing that frustrates the flesh is the very school that forms the character required to steward what is coming. Then there is Gladys — my wife, carrying our child, speaking to me about early pregnancy spotting, about craving, about her body adjusting to something new and miraculous growing within her. I want to handle this with the reverence it deserves without overreaching into medical interpretation. But there is a theological reality I cannot ignore: in that middle segment of this call, while business was being discussed and frustrations were being processed, new life was literally being discussed. A child was being formed in secret. And Gladys, with characteristic grace, said at the end of her part of the conversation: “I got your coffee anyway.” In the midst of concern, in the midst of being physically stretched and somewhat anxious, she served. There is something about that which speaks to me of the nature of covenantal love — what Paul calls in 1 Corinthians 13 the love that “bears all things, endures all things.” Not sentimental; functional. Present. Continuing. I think about the fact that I still have to tell my parents. My dad, who periodically throws his phone in a box and disappears from communication for weeks. And I said something that I find revealing in hindsight: “Everybody around me is so worried right now, you know?” There is a settled quality in that statement — not dismissiveness, but a kind of faith-forged calm. I have been in this long enough to know that worry, while entirely human, is rarely prophetic. It almost never sees clearly. The people around the pregnant woman in Luke 1 were filled with wonder and fear and questions. But Mary herself said, “Let it be to me according to your word.” I am not equating our situation to the Annunciation, but I am noting that the posture of trust in the face of concerned voices around you is a discipleship virtue, not a character flaw. What I want to say most clearly, though, is this: the closing of this call is the hinge. After all of it — the blocked ads, the document uploads, the EIN numbers, the TikTok frustrations, the plan to get a CRM up, the ordinary hard work of building something — Brother Samuel said, “God bless, brother.” And I said, “And He did provide.” Four words. Spontaneous, unrehearsed, almost thrown over my shoulder as I was preparing to hang up. But they are a declaration of the character of God over the character of the day. Not “He will provide someday.” Not “I hope He provides.” “He did provide.” Past tense of a continuing reality. This is the grammar of Ebenezer — of 1 Samuel 7:12, where Samuel takes a stone and calls it עֵזֶר (Ebenezer) — “Thus far the LORD has helped us.” The monument is not erected at the end of all struggle; it is erected in the middle of the next challenge to say: *look how far He has already brought us*. The day did not resolve cleanly. The ads were still under review. The CRM was still being planned. The pregnancy was still only weeks old. My parents still did not know. Ishmael was still not able to join a group call. Nothing was finished. And yet He did provide. He provided meetings without approved ads. He provided a tool that remembered the testimony. He provided a wife who brought coffee. He provided a brother who stayed on the call and kept building. He provided patience for the wait. This is the daily bread of Kingdom life — not the fullness of the harvest, but the sufficient provision for the present step, given by a Father who knows what we need before we ask. The Spirit is pressing something final on me here about the nature of the call itself. This was a working call. It was not a prayer call, not a Bible study, not a structured devotional. And yet the Spirit moved through it. He moved through the frustration. He moved through the laughter. He moved through the mundane logistics of address verification and document sharing. This is what it means for the Kingdom of Heaven to be “at hand” — not confined to sacred spaces and sanctified hours, but present and active in the grinding ordinary of obedient men trying to build something that serves people and glorifies God. The Greek word ἐγγύς (engys, ENG-goos), used in Matthew 3:2 and throughout the Synoptics for “at hand,” carries the spatial and temporal sense of something that has drawn near, is close, is pressing in. The Kingdom is not waiting for a more spiritual context. It is close. It is here. It is moving in the call. ## Implications and Challenges There is a tendency in charismatic and Spirit-filled circles to compartmentalize — to expect the Spirit’s voice in worship, in prophecy, in prayer, but to treat the workday as a secular space that must be spiritually endured until the real moments come. This call challenges that partition directly. If the Spirit can move through an AI memory retrieval on a working call, He can move through any vessel, any moment, any medium that has been yielded to His purposes. The discipline is not to create more sacred spaces; it is to bring the same yielded attention to all spaces. There is also a challenge here about the relationship between waiting and passivity. Brother Samuel’s frustration with the waiting and seeing was real and legitimate — he wants results, he wants the ad up, he wants the business to move. But the call itself demonstrated that what looked like waiting-and-failing was actually waiting-and-being-positioned. The four blocked ad attempts produced four organic posts. The organic posts produced two meetings. Waiting in faith is not the same as doing nothing; it is doing the next faithful thing while the larger outcome remains in God’s hands. The challenge for those of us with vision and drive is to stop treating every unrealized outcome as failure and to start reading it as formation. The domestic moment in this call — Gladys’s conversation about the pregnancy — challenges a subtle but dangerous Western Christian habit: the habit of treating family formation, domestic life, and embodied human experience as spiritually secondary to ministry work. The child being formed in my wife’s womb is not an interruption to the calling. The child is part of the calling. Proverbs 17:6 says that “children’s children are the crown of old men, and the glory of children is their father.” The next generation is a prophetic artifact. To treat it as a logistical footnote in a working call is to miss the weight of what God is doing in covenant time. There is a gentle correction I want to offer about language — including my own on this call — regarding frustration. The colorful expressions of exasperation about the platform, while entirely understandable in the heat of the moment, carry a spirit if repeated often enough. The Proverbs are not naive about this: “A man of quick temper acts foolishly” (Proverbs 14:17). The call to patience is not a call to suppress emotion; it is a call to govern it. The man who can laugh at TikTok’s absurdity without being governed by it, who can name the frustration without being defined by it, is growing into the self-mastery that genuine leadership requires. Finally, there is a word here for those who are building Kingdom-adjacent enterprises — businesses, services, platforms — with a sense of calling and Spirit-led direction. The accountability structures of this age — platform algorithms, verification requirements, ad policies — are not your enemy, even when they are genuinely obstructive. They are part of the wilderness landscape through which you must move. The question is not whether they will resist you; they will. The question is whether you will maintain your prophetic identity as a builder under divine commission even when the infrastructure of the age makes it slow, tedious, and expensive. The answer, for those who have genuinely been led by the Spirit into their assignment, is: you keep posting. You keep calling. You keep building. And He does provide. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity is not demonstrated by the frequency of peak spiritual experiences but by the quality of presence brought to ordinary moments. The maturing disciple learns to carry the same attentiveness in a working phone call that they bring to a prayer meeting — not manufacturing false solemnity, but maintaining the posture of one who knows that the Father is always near, always working, always threading revelation through the fabric of the day. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The call forward here is toward faithful continuance. Keep building what you have been called to build. Keep posting the unpolished truth. Keep showing up for your wife and the new life she carries. Keep calling your brothers. Keep sharing the EIN number when it is needed. The obedience required in this season is not dramatic; it is consistent. It is the daily bread obedience of showing up and doing the next thing with integrity, and saying at the end of the day — He did provide. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this call calls forth is the posture of Ebenezer: the practice of marking the faithfulness of God in real time, even in the middle of unresolved circumstances. Before the ads are approved. Before the CRM is set up. Before the parents know about the pregnancy. Before the season changes. Here — right here, in the middle of it — He did provide. The saint who cultivates this posture becomes a living testimony, a monument of faithfulness in a landscape of worry, a person whose presence itself declares the goodness of God over circumstances that have not yet yielded their fruit. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who has been led step by step by Your Spirit into this season — into this work, this marriage, this calling, this partnership with brothers who are building alongside me. You are the one who provides — not eventually, not conditionally, but already and always, in ways that thread through the ordinary hours of ordinary days and leave marks of faithfulness that I will spend a lifetime learning to read clearly. I thank You for the memory of what You have done — that even the tools I build under Your leading become vessels of testimony, speaking back the truth of how You led me when I am tempted to forget. I thank You for the life growing in Gladys, for the covenant she represents, for her faithfulness in the middle of her own discomfort. I thank You for Brother Samuel, for his tenacity, for his willingness to keep posting when the platform keeps blocking. I come into agreement with Your Word and Your will over this work — that the simple, plain, Spirit-yielded word will accomplish what You purpose it to accomplish, that the waiting will not be wasted, that the formation happening in the frustrating middle is producing character that can carry the weight of what is coming. I stand as one of Your servants under New Covenant authority, in agreement with what You are building between heaven and earth through yielded hands, and I declare with gratitude and faith what I said at the end of that call: You did provide. You are providing. You will provide. To You be all the glory, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.
