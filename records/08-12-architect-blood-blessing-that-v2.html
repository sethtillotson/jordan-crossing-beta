# 08-12 Call Reflection: Business Architecture as Spiritual Parable **Title:** 08-12 at 16:30 — The Architect, the Blood, and the Blessing That Draws the Battle **Recorded:** 2026-08-12 16:30:41 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I sit with this long call between my brother and me, I am struck by how a single working session — plans, tiers, CRUD functions, the Black Book, deployment timelines — kept breaking open into the holy. What began as software architecture became, again and again, a parable: the whole system hinging on one clarifying answer, an entire structure that first had to be scrapped before it could rise, a skeleton awaiting breath, a business built on failure, blood spoken over a hospital bed, and the strange convergence of trials that only intensified once the work was truly blessed. Something arrests me here — the Kingdom did not wait for us to leave the marketplace before it spoke. It preached itself through tiers and timelines, through a dying dog and a frightened wife, through the imago Dei in a difficult client. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Christian life is itself an act of building, and Scripture warns that how we build — and upon what foundation — determines whether the work endures the fire. My brother and I were doing the ordinary labor of two men trying to bring order to a vision, yet even ordinary labor becomes a mirror. The Spirit uses the mundane to teach the eternal: a conversation about schemas and membership tiers can expose how I approach the deeper architecture of the Kingdom, whether I rush to build on unclear ground or wait for the clarifying word before I lay a single stone. And it matters because so many believers misread the arrival of hardship as a sign they have stepped out of God’s will, when Scripture testifies the opposite — that blessing draws the battle, that intercession is real labor, that failure is not the enemy of fruitfulness but its narrow gate, and that our work and our worship are not two rooms but one house. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation Read naturally, this is simply an extended business call between two ambitious young partners refining a sales-training platform. One is the technical builder — migrating data, designing schemas, coding through the night, translating vision into functioning code. The other is the visionary — correcting pricing, defining membership tiers, insisting on guaranteed placement, building the training, the sales psychology, the SOPs and KPIs, the aspirational “Black Book” of billionaire contacts. There is the ordinary friction of collaboration, the repeated “just listen to me,” the pride of good branding, the intoxication of “dominating” a market. The emotional turns — an ex-girlfriend’s mockery, a cousin on a ventilator, a dog dead of cancer, a wife anxious over an insurance bill, a difficult client named James — would all be read as grief, ambition, attraction, and money stress narrated through a religious filter to make them feel more meaningful than they are. It is, the world would say, two hard-working men who finally cracked a formula after repeated financial ruin, sanctifying their hustle with spiritual language. **The Limitation:** Yet the natural mind, for all its clarity about markets and margins, works only with the visible ledger. It hears “an entire architecture depends on that answer” and files it under commerce, never suspecting it echoes the wise builder on the rock. It hears “maybe the dog went in his place” as sentiment, never suspecting the ancient logic of substitution woven into creation. It measures the eight hundred pounds of rent but cannot see what heaven records when a man clothes the vulnerable at cost to himself. It can diagnose a breakup and a money quarrel but cannot perceive the anointing, the accuser, or the fatherly discipline threaded through the trial. The flesh reads the surface fluently and remains illiterate before the depths, because these things are spiritually discerned, and the eye of the flesh was never given the light to see the throne room standing behind the spreadsheet. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with this conversation, the first thing the Spirit presses upon me is a sentence I spoke almost in frustration: “An entire architecture of how to build this depends on that answer.” I meant it about software, but I cannot escape how deeply it belongs to the life of faith. There is a clarifying question that governs everything downstream, and until it is answered rightly, all the labor is provisional. Jesus said, “Whoever hears these sayings of Mine, and does them, I will liken him to a wise man who built his house on the rock” (Matthew 7:24). The Greek οἰκοδομέω (oikodomeō, oy-koh-doh-MEH-oh) — to build a house — describes an enduring structure that rests on hearing *and* doing. The whole architecture of a life depends on that one answer. I notice, too, how much of our conversation was about what must be scrapped. “That needs to get gone,” my brother said of the applications feature, because it did not fit the true nature of the offer. There is something in me that recognizes the necessity of demolition before construction. Even in the Kingdom, the Lord tells Jeremiah he is appointed “to root out and to pull down, to destroy and to throw down, to build and to plant” (Jeremiah 1:10). The Hebrew נָתַץ (natats, naw-TATS) — to break down — comes first; four verbs of tearing down precede the two of building. Repentance is not decoration added to my old design; sometimes an entire assumption must be deleted before the true structure can rise. And what my brother handed me to admire, he immediately corrected: the headings were “not accurate,” the thing before me was “nothing more than a skeleton.” The Spirit keeps returning me to Ezekiel’s valley of bones, “very dry” (Ezekiel 37:2), where the Lord asks, “Son of man, can these bones live?” The עֶצֶם (etsem, EH-tsem) — bone, substance — waits for the רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — Spirit, breath, wind — to enter. So much of my own spiritual life feels like this: a framework of good intentions, headings that need renaming, modules where I thought there were fewer. But the Lord is not offended by the dryness of the bones. My part is to keep building faithfully; His part is to breathe, until they “stood upon their feet, an exceedingly great army” (Ezekiel 37:10). Then there is the matter of authority. My brother’s deepest concern was that he and I, as admins, would have standing to “regularly update” the training — the CRUD function, the power to create, read, update, and delete. Stewardship in the Kingdom carries a similar shape, though under a Master rather than beside a peer. The servants in the parable were each given talents “to each according to his own ability” (Matthew 25:15) — τάλαντον (talanton, TAL-an-ton), an entrusted sum — expected to work with it, not bury it inert. And the ever-evolving training that needs “one place we can regularly update” whispers of sanctification itself, for we are continually “transformed by the renewing of your mind” (Romans 12:2). The Greek μεταμορφόω (metamorphoō, meh-tah-mor-FOH-oh) — inward transformation of form — is ongoing, never finished code. The Master is forever updating His servants toward the image of His Son. What most arrested me in the design was the answer that finally unlocked it: “Relative to which membership you’re on is relative to the opportunities which you gain access to.” I felt a spiritual tremor writing it, for the Kingdom too apportions according to entered relationship — “For to everyone who has, more will be given” (Matthew 25:29), because access flows through covenant. Yet here the parable must break, and the Spirit breaks it carefully. In my brother’s model, the higher the price paid, the greater the reward. But the Gospel inverts this at its center. “You were bought at a price” (1 Corinthians 6:20) — ἀγοράζω (agorazō, ah-goh-RAD-zoh), to buy, to redeem — and the price was the blood of Christ, not the currency of our striving. There are no membership fees at the foot of the cross. Access is by grace received; only reward is according to faithfulness rendered. I am struck, too, by the guarantee at the heart of the offer — “You are guaranteed to get job placement,” said twice, insistently. My brother staked the promise on his own faithfulness: “If they don’t make their money back in three months, we failed them.” And our God is a God of guarantee. The Spirit Himself is given as ἀρραβών (arrabōn, ar-rah-BONE) — a down payment, a pledge — “a guarantee” (2 Corinthians 1:22; Ephesians 1:14) securing the inheritance. A guarantee is only as trustworthy as the one who backs it, and God secures our inheritance not by the strength of a network but by the deposit of His own Spirit, staking the promise on His character, not our cleverness. So too the gatekeeping — “being a closer for us is a privilege,” no one simply “jumps on the website and makes a payment” — echoes the narrow gate, “difficult is the way which leads to life, and there are few who find it” (Matthew 7:13-14). Not because God is stingy, but because there is a qualification, a discovery, a threshold. The turn toward failure deepens the meditation most. Module five is simply called “Failure,” teaching “the quicker you fail, the quicker you win.” The Spirit draws a line straight to the seed: “Unless a grain of wheat falls into the ground and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it produces much grain” (John 12:24) — κόκκος (kokkos, KOK-koss), the grain that must be buried. We spoke, half-laughing, of how “we both starved, we watched our families starve because of our entrepreneurship,” yet out of that starving came the bread we now break for others. Even the KPIs preach it — three hundred messages to secure five meetings, where the ninety-five that fail reveal where not to sow, exactly as the sower scatters lavishly to discover the good soil (Matthew 13). And my brother’s honest test of every offer — “if you told the person exactly how your process works, would they still buy?” — is the very test of the true prophet, for we have “renounced the hidden things of shame… not handling the word of God deceitfully” (2 Corinthians 4:2). The Greek πανουργία (panourgia, pan-oor-GHEE-ah) — craftiness — is precisely what the gospel refuses; truth that welcomes every question is love. Then, without warning, the practical broke and the holy poured through. My brother remembered calling me back to find me weeping — I had been in intercession for my cousin Andy, sedated, feverish, held in a coma. Here the Spirit shows me the ministry of intercession itself: ἐντυγχάνω (entynchanō, en-toon-KHAH-no) — to intercede, to plead on another’s behalf — for Christ “always lives to make intercession” (Hebrews 7:25). When I wept, I was joining His ongoing intercession, and the Spirit Himself prayed through those tears with στεναγμός (stenagmos, ste-nag-MOSS) — groanings which cannot be uttered (Romans 8:26). And when asked whether Andy would live, I heard myself say, “That is entirely in the hands of the living God, and it is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of Him” (Hebrews 10:31). This φοβερός (phoberos, fo-be-ROSS) — fearful — is not terror of a cruel God but reverence before a sovereign One who reigns where medicine has reached the end of its map. Then came the hardest part, and I cannot dismiss it. Tasha’s dog Diamond died of cancer the very day before Andy was expected to wake, and I heard myself say, “Maybe the dog went in his place. You don’t know the sovereignty of the Lord.” I will not build a doctrine the text cannot bear. But the whole creation runs on substitution, and I said the thing that is the spine of the canon: “Where there is a fallen world with iniquity and sin, blood must be the payment.” “Without shedding of blood there is no remission” (Hebrews 9:22); “the life of the flesh is in the blood” (Leviticus 17:11) — דָּם (dam, DAHM). My brother testified this pattern haunts his own family — his little brother Theo born days after his grandmother mysteriously died — insisting three times, “I am not even fucking with you.” That raw insistence is a man staring at a mystery too large for jokes. And I could not leave him in the horror of the pattern: “Jesus Christ healed it all with His blood.” Every substitution is a broken echo of the one true substitution, and His blood speaks “better things than that of Abel” (Hebrews 12:24) — ῥαντισμός (rhantismos, ran-tis-MOSS), a sprinkling that cries mercy where Abel’s cried vengeance. I am seeing, too, that the blessing itself is what drew the battle. My brother’s ex, Emily, called him a loser for his silence — and the world reaches for that word because it cannot categorize the new creation, for “the message of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing” (1 Corinthians 1:18), μωρία (mōria, moh-REE-ah). Yet I gently warned that motivation drawn from proving an ex wrong is fragile fuel; the cross vindicates us before the Father, not before Emily. Then Jade, kicked out by her grandparents, wept on the phone, and my brother paid three months of her rent from her own first commission, taking no profit. I heard Jesus in it: “I was naked and you clothed Me… inasmuch as you did it to one of the least of these My brethren, you did it to Me” (Matthew 25:36-40) — ἐλάχιστος (elachistos, el-AKH-iss-toss), the least. This is the κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-nohn-EE-ah) — fellowship, common sharing — of Acts 2:44, and the tragedy is that such ordinary obedience now reads as miracle. And then the battle pressed into my own home. My wife called, frightened over an insurance company refusing to cover the labs for our pregnancy, her weariness flirting with renouncing the very Lord who is knitting our child, for “You formed my inward parts” (Psalm 139:13) — רָקַם (raqam, rah-KAHM), to weave. I corrected her, refusing to let that language stand in my house, yet I confess before the Spirit that correcting a frightened, pregnant wife requires more tenderness than I may have carried; the rebuke was right, but the manner must be gentle, “with all longsuffering” (2 Timothy 4:2). What the Spirit pressed through all three at once — Emily, Jade, my wife — is that the attacks converge *because* the work is now blessed. The enemy does not waste ammunition on the barren. And so I have learned to thank the Father for the trial itself, because the παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) — fatherly training, discipline — is the treasure: “For whom the Lord loves He chastens” (Hebrews 12:6). My brother prayed for God to preempt the accuser’s attacks; I gently noted the deeper truth, that God has already forgotten every sin in Christ — “their sins… I will remember no more” (Hebrews 10:17), οὐ μὴ μνησθῶ (ou mē mnēsthō, oo may mnays-THOH). The Spirit deepened this into a revelation about anointing, blessing, and timing. “We’ve had the anointing for this for years, but the blessing didn’t come until the posture of our heart changed.” Anointing and blessing are not the same. David was anointed king (1 Samuel 16:13) — מָשַׁח (mashach, mah-SHAKH), to anoint — long before the throne, and the wilderness between the oil and the crown made his heart into a shepherd’s. Joseph carried the dream until “the word of the LORD tested him” (Psalm 105:19) — צָרַף (tsaraph, tsah-RAF), to refine. The blessing descended not because we tried harder but because we turned — שׁוּב (shuv, SHOOV), to return, to repent — and my brother rightly interrupted our own testimony to confess, “We’re still so far from perfect,” which is itself the evidence of the שׁוּב (SHOOV), for the flesh boasts while the returning heart confesses. There is a καιρός (kairos, kai-ROSS) — the appointed, opportune moment — distinct from χρόνος (chronos, KROH-noss), the ticking clock; we had lived in the χρόνος (KROH-noss) of the idea while waiting for God’s καιρός (kai-ROSS) to send the laborers. And when He sent them, every one was Christian — Sam the analyst, the head of setters with seventeen years’ experience — for “Unless the LORD builds the house, they labor in vain” (Psalm 127:1). This is why I insisted faith must be the foundation, not the branding. The Spirit gave me the sobering image of Mark 6:5, where Jesus “could do no mighty work there… because of their unbelief” — οὐκ ἐδύνατο (ouk edynato, ook eh-DEE-nah-toh), He *could* not. Unbelief genuinely constrains the flow of God’s power, in the marketplace as in the synagogue. And in that marketplace the Spirit pressed a conviction about James, the difficult client — the thirty unanswered calls, the money he cost us — that we must not treat people as anything less than the image of God, צֶלֶם אֱלֹהִים (tzelem Elohim, TSEH-lem eh-loh-HEEM), declared over humanity in Genesis 1:27. The apostle James warns that with the same tongue “we bless our God… and we curse men, who have been made in the similitude of God” (James 3:9), and “these things ought not to be so.” The moment a person becomes primarily a category — ideal client, difficult client — we have begun to trade tzelem Elohim (TSEH-lem eh-loh-HEEM) for a ledger. Yet grace re-sees him: my brother himself turned mid-conversation to say, “James is capable. He just needs that training.” God “doesn’t send you into the darkness for no reason. He sends you in there so you can pull people out,” for “the light shines in the darkness” (John 1:5), and “As the Father has sent Me, I also send you” (John 20:21) — ἀποστέλλω (apostellō, ah-po-STEL-loh) — to the sick, not the healthy (Matthew 9:12). I notice the beauty of the Body in how our gifts only became fruitful joined together — “if it wasn’t for you, I wouldn’t have had this to sell,” and “I wouldn’t even know how to code anything.” My brother confessed, “I could never deliver a product,” and I could build the application but never have sold it, for “the eye cannot say to the hand, ‘I have no need of you’” (1 Corinthians 12:21). His love for James — “my heart speaks bigger than my head” — I told him to receive and guard as a gift, echoing Paul: “Guard what was committed to your trust” (1 Timothy 6:20), παραθήκη (parathēkē, pah-rah-THAY-kay), an entrusted deposit. And beneath it all is our shared identity: not merely a Christian but a δοῦλος (doulos, DOO-loss) — a bondservant, one wholly owned — as Paul opened his letters (Romans 1:1). If we are δοῦλος (DOO-loss) of Christ, the company is not ours to optimize for comfort; it is His, and His purposes always bend toward the lost and overlooked. Finally, the conversation kept convicting me about listening, about rest, and about worship. Understanding came not when either of us spoke louder but when I stopped correcting my brother’s verbiage and asked the real question underneath — “let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak” (James 1:19), ταχύς (tachys, tah-KHOOS). My brother’s confession that without his eleven o’clock rhythm he gets “no personal time” carries the wisdom of Sabbath, for God Himself rested and sanctified the seventh day (Genesis 2:3) — שָׁבַת (shabat, shah-VAHT), to cease. And when we spoke of self-exalting “Christian” music that is truly “idolatry of self,” anthems of “I have all the authority,” I felt the correction rise: that posture stands where only the eternal High Priest belongs. Worship names God for who He is and points away from self toward the throne. Through it all, my brother turned me back to the one necessary thing: “You need your secret time with the Lord.” The blessing has come, but the posture that unlocked it must be guarded, or the blessing will outrun the man — for we are already “seated together in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus” (Ephesians 2:6), συγκαθίζω (sygkathizō, soong-kath-ID-zoh), and it is “no longer I who do it, but sin that dwells in me” (Romans 7:20), ἁμαρτία (hamartia, ha-mar-TEE-ah) — the flesh passing, the saint remaining. ## Implications and Challenges I offer these gently, as one still in the struggle — an unfinished man beside an unfinished brother — not as one who has arrived. **On building before clarity.** How often do I lay stones of ministry or ambition before receiving the one clarifying word that governs the whole structure? The modern instinct is to build fast and revise later, but the Spirit invites me to ask first: is this the rock or the sand? And we must hold our unfinished work honestly, presenting the skeleton as a skeleton rather than parading the framework as the fulfillment. **On seeking the blessing without the brokenness.** The modern gospel of gain wants the five-thousand-dollar day without the two-in-the-morning tears, the platform without the wilderness. But the blessing followed the changed heart, not the striving hand. We cannot manufacture the fruit; we can only turn, and keep turning. Much of Western Christianity glorifies the burned-out builder and hides its failures behind curated success, yet the Kingdom is forged in the ground where the seed dies. **On misreading the battle.** The church often reads trouble as a red light when God frequently intends it as green. The blessing draws the battle; do not flee the field at the first arrow. Mature faith reframes the trial as παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) — treasure, not threat — and lets thanksgiving replace panic as the first response to hardship. **On mistaking access for merit, and people for categories.** The world’s tiers reward those who pay more, and the subtle danger is to import that logic — treating spiritual access as earned by intensity or performance rather than received by grace. Just as dangerous is letting efficiency demote a soul into a line item. The test is the heart: are we equipping the overlooked, or merely routing them away from us? Generosity must flow from love of Christ in the least of these, never from the pride of being the kind of company that does such things (Matthew 6:3). **On tenderness in correction, and the tyranny of urgency.** Correcting those we love — a fearful wife, a struggling friend — must never sacrifice tenderness on the altar of being right; truth without gentleness can wound the very faith it means to defend. And our culture worships urgency, which the enemy exploits in romance, business, and every appetite. “The devil wants to rush always; the Lord’s time is not our timeline.” Waiting in the correct posture is itself an act of worship. **On faith as foundation, not branding.** It is easy in Christian business culture to treat faith as a marketing differentiator, and self-exalting worship as devotion. The Mark 6 principle confronts this fiercely: unbelief genuinely constrains what God can do, so faith must be structural, not cosmetic — and worship must point to the throne, not the mirror. I end where grace must always return me: the God who orders architecture is patient with builders still learning to read the blueprint, and that patience is itself the mercy of the blood. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** We mature when we learn that the entire architecture of a life depends on rightly answering the foundational question — building not by adding features to a faulty base, but by letting the Spirit tear down assumptions and build upon the Rock. We mature when we stop despising our failures and read them as soil-tests, when the years between the anointing and the blessing become the forge rather than the delay, and when we stop asking “why is this attack happening to me?” and start asking “what is my Father forming in me through it?” **For Kingdom Obedience:** We are called to steward what has been entrusted with the diligence of admins over a living training, never burying the talent. Obedience means a costly honesty — promising only what we can fulfill, presenting the whole truth without craftiness, building our enterprises on faith as foundation rather than branding. It looks like clothing the naked at real cost, blessing the vulnerable without calculating profit, securing what God has commissioned and then deliberately going into difficulty to pull people out — and guarding the secret place above the success, for “You need your secret time with the Lord” is not sentiment; it is survival. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic saint waits for the clarifying word before rushing to build, asks the real question beneath the surface noise, and trusts the ἀρραβών (ar-rah-BONE) rather than the strength of visible networks. We are Ezekiel in the valley, speaking over dry bones without yet seeing them live, trusting the רוּחַ (ROO-akh) to do what no code can. We intercede without needing to control the outcome, weeping with the Spirit’s groanings, discerning the difference between our fire and God’s καιρός (kai-ROSS), watching for the enemy’s convergence upon the anointed, refusing the spirit of rush, and keeping our worship pointed always toward the throne. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come to You in the name of Jesus, the Lamb whose blood speaks better things than the blood of Abel, and I thank You that You are the great Architect who builds the house so the builders do not labor in vain. I confess how quickly I move to construct before I have received Your clarifying word, and I lay down my competence, my branding, my pride of work at Your feet as an offering rather than a monument. Teach me to root out what must be gone, to build only on the Rock of Your Son, and to steward faithfully the training of my own soul as You continually renew my mind. I thank You that the blessing You gave followed the heart You changed, and that every trial You have entrusted to us is a stewardship of Your glory, drawing the battle only because Your hand is upon this work. I lift my cousin Andy into Your fearful and merciful hands — breathe upon him, break the fever no doctor can find, and if it is Your will, call him up out of that sleep. I stand with Tasha in her grief, and I lift my wife and the child You are knitting in her womb; still her fear, silence the accuser at our door, and forgive me where my correction lacked tenderness. Guard my brother’s heart in the crush, sanctify his pace, and let his generosity to the least of these be received by You as unto Yourself. Keep us humble in the blessing, faithful in the secret place, unashamed of the cross in every room we enter — the marketplace and the prayer closet alike — and let no anxiety rush what Your timing intends to unveil slowly and well. As Your servant and co-heir under the New Covenant, one of the servants of God the prophets, I make agreement with Your will between the heavens and the earth, offering You this whole day as one house of worship. Not to us, O Lord, not to us, but to Your name be the glory, for You have healed it all with the blood of Your Son. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |-------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Matthew 7:24 | οἰκοδομέω (oikodomeō, oy-koh-doh-MEH-oh) — to build a house | The wise builder’s enduring structure rests on hearing and doing Christ’s words. | | Jeremiah 1:10 | נָתַץ (natats, naw-TATS) — to break down / demolish | Demolition of the old precedes true building;
