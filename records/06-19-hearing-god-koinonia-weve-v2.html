# 06-19 Call Reflection: God Hears — Ishmael, Koinonia, and Faith in the Ordinary **Title:** 06-19 at 17:11 — The Hearing God and the Koinonia We’ve Forgotten **Recorded:** 2026-06-19 17:11:09 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to this conversation unfold between myself and my brother — wandering through cryptocurrency logistics, broken teeth, pregnant wives, spam calls, and the name of an obscure biblical figure spoken in the wrong accent — I was struck by something I could not immediately name. The sacred was not arriving in polished form. It was arriving the way manna arrived: small, daily, strange, and easily overlooked by those who expected something grander. Somewhere inside the ordinary noise of two brothers talking, God was quietly declaring that He hears. **Why This Matters:** The modern Western church has largely trained itself to encounter God in designated sacred spaces — Sunday platforms, formal prayer meetings, curated worship sets — and to expect revelation to arrive with appropriate spiritual packaging. What this conversation confronts is the deeply uncomfortable and deeply liberating truth that the living God does not wait for our liturgical permission. He speaks through a pregnant Filipina woman who doesn’t read English well. He answers weeks of lamenting through a single obscure name. He builds His Kingdom through two brothers sorting out a crypto transfer with overdrafted bank accounts, talking about gold teeth and spam-call revenge fantasies, and somehow — underneath all of it — circling back again and again to the covenant faithfulness of a God who sees, provides, and hears. If we cannot recognize God in the small and ordinary, we will miss most of what He is actually doing. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the vantage point of natural reasoning, this conversation is unremarkable — even a little scattered. Two friends catch up, discuss financial stress, talk about jobs and pregnancy symptoms, debate soccer, joke about gold teeth, and try to sort out a payment problem. The brief moments of theological substance — the reflection on koinonia, on daily acts of kindness, on the name Ishmael — could easily be read as the kind of casual spiritual musing that friends who share a faith tradition naturally produce. The more analytically inclined observer might even suggest that the attribution of prophetic weight to a pregnant woman saying a biblical name is a form of confirmation bias: we hear what we want to hear, we assign meaning to coincidences that comfort us, and we spiritualize financial uncertainty because it feels better than confronting it practically. The cynical frame would say: two men of modest means are making each other feel better about being broke by invoking God’s sovereignty. **The Limitation:** But this frame collapses the moment you actually sit with what happened. The natural mind cannot account for the precision. It cannot explain why a woman with limited biblical literacy would produce, in a non-native accent, the exact Hebrew name that semantically answers a weeks-long lament being conducted by her husband. The natural mind explains coincidence but cannot explain covenant. It can trace psychological comfort but it cannot trace the voice of a God who, as the prophet Amos declared, does nothing without revealing His secret to His servants the prophets (Amos 3:7). The reason the flesh cannot perceive what the Spirit is doing here is not because the flesh is stupid — it is because spiritual things are spiritually discerned, and the frequency on which God speaks is simply not accessible to unaided human perception. You need ears that have been trained to hear the still, small voice rather than waiting only for the earthquake and the fire (1 Kings 19:12). ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I want to begin with the name, because everything else in this conversation orbits around it. My wife — who reads Scripture in Cebuano, whose biblical vocabulary does not naturally include the obscure patronyms of the Hebrew narrative — said *Ishmael* in an English accent while we were talking on the couch. Just that. One word. And it landed with the weight of an answer, because I had been asking a question for two straight weeks. The name Ishmael comes from two Hebrew roots: יִשְׁמָע (yishma, from שָׁמַע, *shama*, shah-MAH) meaning “he hears,” and אֵל (*El*, EL) meaning “God.” Together: *God hears*. It is the name given to Hagar’s son — not the covenant child, not the one through whom the great promises were formally routed, but the one born in the wilderness of Abram’s impatience, the one who could have been discarded and forgotten. And yet God did not forget him. The angel found Hagar by a spring in the wilderness and said to her, *“The LORD has heard your affliction”* (Genesis 16:11). This is the original naming moment: God hears the afflicted, even those who seem to be on the margins of the promise. What I was not fully prepared for is the way that name lands inside my own season. I had been rising every morning and lamenting — honestly, even a little indecorously, like a man who has been walking in circles long enough to start raising his voice about it. I was not rejecting God’s provision. I had actually arrived at a different place than fear: I told the Lord that I was not saving my resources out of self-preservation any longer, but I was still asking *why*. Why the scarcity? Why the delay? And the answer that came — not through a vision, not through a thunderclap, but through my wife’s mouth in a borrowed accent — was simply: *God hears*. Not *God has already fixed it*. Not *the breakthrough is tomorrow*. Just: He hears. That is the word. And somehow, that was enough. I cannot help but trace this back to the exchange between God and Job, because I mentioned it in the conversation and I want to sit with it more carefully here. When God finally responds to Job’s extended lament, He does not explain Himself. He does not offer a theological treatise on suffering. He asks Job where he was when the foundations of the earth were laid, whether Job has commanded the morning or loosed the bands of Orion (Job 38:4, 31). This is not God being cruel. This is God reorienting the worshiper. What feels like a rebuke is actually an act of love: it is God saying, *I am larger than the frame you have put around your pain*. And the strange thing — the thing I recognize from my own mornings — is that Job’s response to this divine whirlwind is not humiliation. It is worship. *“I have heard of You by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees You”* (Job 42:5). The lament itself was the pathway into encounter. Now I want to sit with something that surfaced in our conversation almost as an aside but is actually theologically weighty: the reflection on κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-noh-NEE-ah) — the fellowship, the shared life, the communal participation in the things of God. I said something I want to hold up more carefully: that Christianity was never meant to be a separate church community running parallel to a neighborhood community. It was meant to *be* the neighborhood. The early church in Acts did not construct a religious sub-culture that people could opt into on Sundays. They broke bread from house to house, they held goods in common, they filled the streets with the practical overflow of a transformed people (Acts 2:44–46). This is not idealism. This is the architecture of the Kingdom. The picture I described — going for a walk once a day, looking for one problem you have the resources to solve, offering that up — is not a self-help productivity tip. It is the daily enactment of the Sermon on the Mount. It is salt doing what salt does (Matthew 5:13). The issue is not that the world lacks solutions; the issue is that the people who carry the Spirit of the living God are largely sitting inside buildings waiting to be served rather than walking their streets looking for what needs to be healed. I am not saying this with condemnation — I include myself. But the indictment is real: we have so domesticated discipleship that we need a program to tell us to do what love should make automatic. There is something in my dad’s pattern that I also want to name, because I think it touched something true even if it came wrapped in a wound. He told me he had ghosted people his entire life because he had learned early that we are all sinners and we will all hurt each other. There is a fractured wisdom there — a realism that the natural man arrives at when he has been burned enough times. But the Gospel takes that same observation — *yes, we are all sinners, yes, we wound each other* — and arrives at the opposite conclusion. The recognition that I will stumble and that you will stumble is not a reason to withdraw; it is precisely the reason we need each other close enough to extend grace in real time. The blood of Jesus has dealt with the offense before it even happens. χάρις (charis, KHAR-ees) — grace — is not the reward for good behavior in community. It is the atmosphere in which community becomes possible at all. I said it this way in the conversation: the point of the brethren knowing each other intimately is so that they can see the fruits of the Spirit working in a person, even in the small things. Even when someone has quit one habit out of two hundred. That image is important. We tend to evaluate transformation by the totality of visible fruit and we dismiss the first green shoot because the tree is not yet bearing. But God does not despise the day of small things — and this landed as a specific word in our conversation. Zechariah 4:10 asks, *“For who has despised the day of small things?”* The context is the rebuilding of the temple — a work that looked laughably inadequate to those watching. But the Lord’s eyes were running to and fro across the whole earth, watching over the small work with full attention. The small transfer, the sputtering engine making it two miles to the gas station, the one kind act per day — these are not beneath the notice of God. They are precisely where He tends to show up. The engine metaphor I used — the forty-year-old GM running on fumes, probably startable with whatever was available — was said as a joke, but I want to receive it as a parable. Walking by faith does not mean you have a full tank. It means you keep driving when the gauge reads empty, because you have enough testimony of past provision to believe that what kept you moving before has not changed its nature. This is the logic of the manna: you do not gather for tomorrow because yesterday’s word is sufficient for today, and tomorrow will carry its own word (Exodus 16:4). The anxiety that wants to stockpile — to hoard, to hedge every contingency — is actually a form of practical atheism: it behaves as though God’s provision expires. Faith drives on fumes. Not recklessly. Not without taking the next available exit. But without panic. The car has always made it. He has always heard. Let me return one more time to Ishmael, because I want to hold the typological weight of it carefully. Ishmael was not the covenant child. He was the child of a side story — of impatience, of a plan made in the flesh. And yet God made a promise over him too. *“I will make him a great nation”* (Genesis 21:18). The well appeared in the wilderness precisely when Hagar had given up and looked away from her dying son. *“God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water”* (Genesis 21:19). The well was already there. God did not create it in that moment — He opened her eyes to what was already present. This is the word I receive inside our current season: the provision is not absent. My eyes may need to be opened to what is already there. The koinonia, the brotherhood, the small daily work, the forty-year engine, the wife who speaks in borrowed accents — these are the wells already dug in the wilderness. I am learning to see them. I also want to name the phrase my brother offered almost under his breath: *“He who questions if he is poor is above the richest of them all.”* He said his thoughts had started sounding like Scripture. I do not think that is accidental. When a man begins to meditate on the Word, when he orients his morning around stillness before caffeine, when he chooses to let the Word form the grammar of his inner life — his thoughts begin to carry a different weight. This is the promise of Psalm 1: the man who meditates on the law day and night becomes like a tree planted by rivers of water (Psalm 1:2–3). The fruit comes later. The meditation comes first. The man who is questioning whether he is poor — who is pressing into the question rather than accepting the label — is already operating from a place the merely wealthy cannot access, because wealth without poverty of spirit (Matthew 5:3) produces only consumption, not the kind of questioning that opens into encounter. And all of this is happening inside what looked like a messy phone call between two men trying to figure out a crypto transfer. That, I think, is the entire testimony. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation puts before me — and before any who read it — is the challenge of **where we expect God to speak**. We have been formed, most of us, to expect the rhema word to arrive in formal containers: the sermon, the altar call, the prophecy line, the curated devotional. But the testimony of Scripture is relentlessly earthy. God spoke through a burning thornbush, a donkey’s complaint, a whispered still small voice after the great wind and fire, a pregnant woman’s involuntary utterance. The discipline this calls for is not to pursue more informal experiences at the expense of serious study — it is to cultivate ears that are always open, even when the speaker is your wife on a couch and the topic has already moved on. The second challenge is the one I named around **koinonia**: the Western church has largely traded neighborhood community for church community, and in doing so has preserved the vocabulary of fellowship while evacuating its content. Bearing one another’s burdens (Galatians 6:2) cannot happen across a parking lot on Sunday morning. It requires proximity, frequency, and the willingness to be seen in your ordinary mess — sputtering engines, overdrafted accounts, broken teeth, pregnancy nausea, and all. The corrective is not to attend more church events. It is to become genuinely available to the people who live within walking distance of your life. Third, there is a challenge around **the day of small things**. Much of Western Christian culture runs on scale: the large conference, the viral sermon clip, the million-subscriber ministry, the church that fills an arena. None of these things are evil. But they quietly train the heart to associate significance with size, and this is a form of worldliness that has dressed itself in Kingdom language. The Lord’s eyes are on the small work. The first green shoot of transformation in a brother who has two hundred bad habits but has quit one — that is worthy of celebration, not dismissal. We must recover the capacity to honor small faithfulness, because that is what almost all faithfulness actually looks like from the inside. Fourth, the **posture of lament** deserves to be rehabilitated in our communities. I spent two weeks getting up every morning and asking *why* — and some would call that a lack of faith. But the Psalms are saturated with lament. Job’s lament opens the door to the whirlwind of encounter. Hagar’s weeping in the wilderness is what draws the angel. The God who names Himself *Ishmael’s God* is a God who responds to the sound of honest grief. We have inadvertently built church cultures where performed positivity is mistaken for faith, and authentic lament is treated as spiritual immaturity. This is a pastoral failure with serious consequences: it sends the afflicted into silence rather than into the throne room. Fifth and finally, there is a challenge about **faith and practical action as co-laborers**, not competitors. The conversation held together in one breath both *“only God can make this happen”* and *“let’s figure it out.”* This is the right theological instinct. Faith does not make us passive. It makes us willing to act on inadequate resources because we have a theology of divine supply that precedes our action. We sort out the transfer. We drive on fumes. We pick up the trash on the walk. We do the small thing in front of us. And we do it with the settled conviction that the One whose name means *God hears* is not watching from a distance. He is at work in the sorting-out. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This calls me — and any who sit with it — toward a deeper cultivation of what I would call *covenantal perception*: the trained capacity to see the activity of God inside the texture of ordinary moments. Spiritual maturity is not measured by the grandeur of one’s spiritual experiences. It is measured by the depth of one’s recognition of God in the small and unremarkable. The mature believer does not need the earthquake to confirm that God is near. They have learned to hear the whisper. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The practical response is deceptively simple: go for the walk. Do the one thing. Extend the grace to the brother who is still on step one of two hundred. Stay in the neighborhood. Keep the engine running even when the gauge says empty, and let the testimony of provision build the faith for the next mile. Do not despise the small transfer, the small crop, the small word. Bring it as an offering and trust that the One who multiplied loaves does not require abundance as a precondition for miracle. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this calls us into is one of *attentive expectancy* — not the frantic seeking of signs, but the quiet readiness of someone who has learned that God speaks constantly and the shortage is never on His end. It is the posture of Elijah’s servant going back seven times to look at the horizon (1 Kings 18:43–44), or of Anna who served in the temple with fastings and prayers, and recognized the Redeemer when He arrived as a forty-day-old infant (Luke 2:36–38). The prophetic community does not manufacture the word. It prepares itself to receive it — and then does not call common what God has cleansed. ## A Closing Prayer Father, You are the God who hears — not as a statement of theological principle, but as a lived covenant reality pressing through the ordinary hours of ordinary days. You spoke into a casual evening conversation and produced an answer to weeks of morning lament, and You did it through the mouth of someone who barely knew the name You chose. That is exactly who You are, and I stand in wonder of it. I receive the name *Ishmael* as a personal covenant marker: I was heard. The lament reached You. And I know that the provision is not absent — my eyes simply need to be opened to the wells already present in this wilderness. I ask, on behalf of every brother and sister who will read this word, that You open their eyes too. Let them see the well. Let them hear the whisper. Let them not call small what You are calling sufficient. Build in us the koinonia that our neighborhoods are waiting for — the visible, daily, burden-bearing fellowship of people who have been so thoroughly loved that they cannot help but extend it. And for the practical things — the debts, the engines, the transfers, the overdrafts, the unborn children, the chipped teeth, the long commutes — we bring them all under Your name, the name that means You have already heard us before we finished saying it. Do what only You can do, Lord, and let us be faithful with what only we can do. To the One who fills all things, who governs the small and the great with equal precision, who neither slumbers nor sleeps — all glory, all honor, all gratitude, from now until the age to come. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Genesis 16:11 — “The LORD has heard your affliction” | יִשְׁמָעֵל (Yishma’el, yish-mah-EL) from שָׁמַע (shama, shah-MAH) + אֵל (El, EL) | The name Ishmael encodes a divine promise: God actively hears the afflicted, even those on the apparent margins of covenant promise | | Genesis 21:19 — “God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water” | וַיִּפְקַח (vayyifkach, vah-yif-KAKH) — “and He opened” | The verb implies a sovereign act of divine revelation;
