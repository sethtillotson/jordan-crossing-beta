# 08-28 Reflection: Losing a Crypto Fortune as God’s Mercy and the Grace of Sacrificial Giving **Title:** 08-28 at 07:44 — The Provision I Almost Died Owning **Recorded:** 2026-08-28 07:44:53 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I sat with a phone pressed to my ear this morning after two twelve-hour shifts with no sleep, feet wrecked on Walmart steel toes, sending sixty dollars I barely had to a brother who hadn’t eaten since yesterday. Somewhere in the middle of business logistics, cold emails, Stripe investigations, and Coinbase transfers, the real weight of the whole conversation surfaced: the fortune I lost to a hacker years ago — one coin alone now worth over one point three million — and my own quiet confession that keeping it would have killed me. The tension I cannot shake is this: what looked like loss may have been the mercy of God, and what looks like scarcity now may be the very soil where I finally learned to give. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Body of Christ is full of believers who measure the faithfulness of God by the size of the account, and who cannot tell the difference between provision that blesses and provision that destroys. I heard myself say something on that call that I need to hold up to the light — that I am glad I lost the money, because had I kept it I would be dead or in jail, my soul corrupted further than it already was. That is not a small thing. It is a testimony that the withholding hand of God can be as loving as the giving hand, and that the “extra provision I haven’t had in years” flowing to me now is only safe because I am no longer the man who could not look at a price tag. Discipleship requires that we learn to read our losses as well as our gains, and to recognize when God took something away precisely because He loved us too much to let us have it. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation By natural reckoning, this is a story of a young man grinding through exhaustion to keep a fragile business and a fragile brotherhood afloat, and a bitter footnote about crypto riches that got away. The world would say I made a catastrophic financial mistake — that I should have secured that wallet, that the hacker cost me generational wealth, and that I am now romanticizing failure to soften the sting of working overtime on concrete for twelve hours a day. It would read my gladness at losing the money as coping, a psychological reframe to protect the ego from the unbearable arithmetic of one-point-three-million-dollars-and-growing. The world would applaud the hustle, the pushiness with Emmett the assembly lead, the tax-free overtime, the cold-email affiliate program, and it would file the spiritual talk at the end as a pleasant flourish. It would not see anything sacred in a sixty-dollar transfer between two broke men. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind cannot weigh a soul against a sum, and so it always misprices the transaction. It can calculate what the coin would be worth today, but it cannot calculate what I would be worth today had I kept it. It sees a hacker and names it theft; it cannot see a Father and name it rescue. The flesh reads provision as the absence of hardship and therefore cannot comprehend a God who sometimes provides *by* subtraction, who guards a man’s life by emptying his hands. What the world files as a coping mechanism, the Spirit reveals as spiritual sight — the rare and costly ability to look back at a wound and see the fingerprints of mercy in it. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I keep returning to the sound of it — my brother’s stomach rumbling audibly through the phone, his admission that he hadn’t eaten since yesterday, and then the two of us fumbling through Coinbase because the withdrawal wouldn’t clear, because it was still in crypto, because the price dropped thirty-six pence in the sixty seconds it took to sell. Two men who once talked about billions, now doing careful math over sixty-seven dollars. “This is literally what I’m down to,” he said. And I heard myself say, “Yeah, man, I’ve been blessed lately with extra provision that I haven’t had in years, and so it feels good to be able to spread it out to the brethren.” That sentence is the hinge of the whole call, though neither of us paused on it at the time. What arrests me now, sitting with it, is the strange geometry of that generosity. I am not giving out of abundance. I am giving out of a paycheck that hasn’t fully come in yet, betting on Friday overtime, assuming the worst-case scenario and preparing for it. And yet something in me insisted on covering him, on offering to onboard both Jade and Jaden, on being the one who absorbs the shortfall. That impulse did not come from the man I used to be. The man who once held dozens of coins climbing hundreds of thousands of percent could not have felt this — because he had no price tag to weigh, no cost to feel, and therefore no gift to give. The first Scripture that opens over this is the paradox Paul names to the Corinthians: “as poor, yet making many rich; as having nothing, and yet possessing all things” (2 Corinthians 8:9 stands behind it, but 2 Corinthians 6:10 speaks it directly). I gave sixty dollars I needed. The Macedonians, Paul says, gave “in a great trial of affliction” out of “their deep poverty,” and their giving “abounded in the riches of their liberality” (2 Corinthians 8:2). The Spirit is pressing on me that the capacity to give sacrificially is itself a form of wealth the old me never possessed. I had money then and could not give. I have little now and cannot stop giving. Which of those two men is actually rich? There is a word underneath all of this. The Greek for the grace that moves such giving is χάρις (charis, KHAH-rees) — grace, favor, the undeserved gift that becomes generosity when it passes through a person. Paul uses this same word four different ways in a single passage on giving, because the gift of God and the gift to the brother are the same substance flowing in one current. The extra provision I named on the call is χάρις (KHAH-rees) received; the sixty dollars is χάρις (KHAH-rees) released. And I am seeing that God would not entrust the releasing to me until He had first done the deep work of teaching me what money can do to a soul that has too much of it. That deep work has a name too. The Hebrew calls it מוּסָר (musar, moo-SAHR) — discipline, correction, the instruction that comes through hardship. “My son, do not despise the chastening of the LORD, nor detest His correction” (Proverbs 3:11). I confessed on the call that had I kept the fortune, “it would have corrupted my soul even further than what it already was corrupted at that time.” The loss of that money was מוּסָר (moo-SAHR). It was the Father’s correction wearing the mask of a hacker’s theft. Hebrews takes this same thread and pulls it tight: “whom the LORD loves He chastens” (Hebrews 12:6), and the word there is παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) — the fatherly training of a beloved child. What the world calls my worst financial disaster, the Spirit reveals as παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah). I think of the rich young ruler, who “had great possessions” and went away sorrowful because he could not part with them (Matthew 19:22). Jesus looked at him and loved him, and out of that love asked for everything. The ruler kept his wealth and lost his walk with Christ. I lost my wealth — violently, involuntarily — and it opened the door to a walk with Christ I confessed openly on this call: “months of reading the Bible and praying.” I was, in a sense, the rich young ruler who could not let go, and so the letting-go was done *for* me, by a hand I could not see, in a mercy I did not ask for. “It is better to trust in the LORD than to put confidence in man” (Psalm 118:8) — and better still to have the confidence stripped away before it damns you. The tension the Spirit is opening here is one the modern church rarely dares to name: that unanswered greed can be grace, and that God sometimes withholds the very thing we would have used to destroy ourselves. We pray so easily for increase. We assume that more resource means more Kingdom good. But I stood on the edge of a resource that would have handed me, in my own words, a mentality where “the laws and fines and stuff, it just becomes a price tag” — where I could sin freely and simply pay for the consequences. That is the mind of the flesh weaponized by wealth. And God, in His severe mercy, did not let me arrive there. What natural reasoning would never guess is that the poverty of this present season is the safeguard on the generosity of it. Because I feel every sixty dollars, I give it as worship. Because I have to work a Friday for five hundred dollars to onboard my brethren, the onboarding becomes an act of love rather than a line item. Jesus said, “where your treasure is, there your heart will be also” (Matthew 6:21). The hack relocated my treasure. It took the treasure out of my hands and, over months of Scripture and prayer, God relocated it into His Kingdom and into His people. I could not have done that myself. I was too corrupted to be trusted with the doing of it. And here is the deepest thing the Spirit is unveiling: I said to my brother, “It’s not even me, dude. It’s all glory to God. It’s all Jesus shining through me.” I said it about my conflict management, my communication, the way I speak to Jade and Jaden. But it is true of the whole man now standing in this conversation. The pushiness that closed the overtime, the discernment that told Jade she still needs stable income, the wisdom that warned my brother away from over-leveraged funded accounts, the gentleness that told him to save fasting “for spiritual things” rather than starvation — all of it is the fruit of a life that had to be emptied before it could be filled. The million dollars I lost was the price of the man I have become. I would not sell him back for it. For the brethren, this means our fellowship is being knit together not by shared abundance but by shared lack laid down for one another. My brother and I are two men who once dreamed in billions and now serve each other in dollars, and there is more of Christ in the dollars. The early church “had all things in common” and “distributed to each as anyone had need” (Acts 2:44–45), and the miracle was never the amount — it was that no one counted his own possessions as his own. When I sent that sixty dollars, when I offered to cover the Cleverly onboarding for two people who are not even sure they want it, I was practicing the economy of the Kingdom in miniature, and the Spirit was teaching me its liturgy through the very smallness of the sum. So I confess it plainly: I am glad the money is gone. I bless the God who let it be taken. And I ask Him to keep me poor enough in spirit to keep giving, however much overtime that costs my exhausted body, because the treasure I am storing now cannot be hacked, cannot drop thirty-six pence in a minute, and will not be lost. ## Implications and Challenges **On measuring the goodness of God by the size of the account.** Much of the Western church has quietly baptized the assumption that increase is always blessing and loss is always attack. My own testimony dismantles that. I lost over a million dollars and I now call it mercy, because the Spirit showed me the man I would have become had I kept it. The challenge for the brethren is to stop reflexively reading every subtraction as the enemy’s theft, and to ask instead whether the Father might be practicing παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) on a child He loves too fiercely to indulge. Sometimes the most loving thing God can do is refuse to answer the prayer for more. **On the poverty that makes generosity real.** I gave sixty dollars out of a paycheck not yet earned, and it cost me something to give it — which is precisely why it was worship. The modern impulse is to wait until we have surplus before we give, to give from the overflow so that the giving never actually pinches. But the Macedonians gave “out of their deep poverty” (2 Corinthians 8:2), and Jesus praised the widow’s two mites above the rich men’s abundance (Luke 21:1–4). If our giving never touches our own security, we may be missing the very grace that giving was meant to carry. Let us not despise the smallness of what we can offer; the smallness is often where the sacrifice lives. **On fasting as worship, not as coping.** I told my brother plainly to leave fasting “for spiritual things,” confessing that I used to spiritualize my own hunger when I simply had no money for food, and that doing so “negates the entire spiritual side of fasting.” This is a gentle but real correction to a habit that dresses up necessity in the language of devotion. Biblical fasting is a chosen laying-down before God (Isaiah 58), not a rebranding of poverty. There is grace here, not condemnation — but there is also a call to honesty about what is actually devotion and what is merely survival wearing devotion’s clothes. **On wealth as an anesthetic against consequence.** I named the mentality that money once gave me: that laws and fines “just becomes a price tag,” that I could do whatever I wanted and simply pay for it. This is the spiritual danger the church seldom preaches — that money does not merely tempt us to love it, it corrodes our very fear of God by making sin affordable. “The love of money is a root of all kinds of evil” (1 Timothy 6:10) not only because we hoard it, but because it convinces us that everything, including our own transgression, has a payable price. The brethren must guard against measuring obedience by what it costs and calling that freedom. **On stewarding the little as preparation for the true riches.** The whole call was a laboring over small things — sixty dollars, a Stripe investigation, one Cleverly client, a five-day Coinbase hold. It would be easy to see this as beneath the men who once traded in billions. But Jesus said, “He who is faithful in what is least is faithful also in much” (Luke 16:10), and warned that if we are not faithful in unrighteous mammon, no one will commit to us “the true riches” (Luke 16:11). The tedious faithfulness of getting a brother onboarded, of paying commission before profit, of covering a shortfall — this is the training ground. God is not waiting for us to have more before He works; He is watching what we do with the little. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity is learning to read our own history with the eyes of the Spirit rather than the eyes of regret. The mature disciple can look at the greatest loss of his life and, without denial or self-deception, name the mercy hidden inside it. This requires the death of the assumption that God’s love always looks like getting what we wanted, and the birth of a trust deep enough to bless the empty hand. It means I no longer envy the man I might have been with a million dollars, because I have met the man God actually made through the losing of it, and I love him more. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience here looks like tangible, sacrificial provision for the brethren — sending the sixty dollars, working the extra Friday, covering the onboarding before the profit arrives, paying commission first and foremost as I insisted on the call. It looks like refusing to let another believer starve while I have anything at all, because the economy of the Kingdom is that we distribute to each as anyone has need. Obedience is not waiting for abundance; it is releasing what we have now, feeling the cost, and calling the cost worship. Let us be the kind of brethren who show up with what little we have and lay it down. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture trained by this call is the discernment to test our own desires before God grants them — to ask not merely “is this good?” but “am I ready to hold this without being destroyed by it?” It is the watchfulness that recognizes when a withheld or lost thing is actually a rescue, and the patience to trust the Father’s timing on provision, as I am trusting Friday’s paycheck rather than grasping for over-leveraged funded accounts. The watchman learns to give thanks not only for what arrives but for what God, in wisdom, keeps from arriving. This is the posture that keeps a soul unhacked while the markets rise and fall. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come to You exhausted in body and grateful in spirit, standing between the heavens and the earth as one of Your servants, and I bless You for the fortune You took from my hands before it could take my life. I thank You that You loved me enough to answer my greed with silence and my recklessness with loss, and that in the emptying You made room for Your Word and Your Spirit to fill me over these months of prayer and Scripture. I confess that I could not have let go of that wealth myself, and so I praise the severe mercy that let it go for me. Now, Lord, I give You this small provision I have been trusted with — the overtime, the paycheck not yet come, the sixty dollars sent to a hungry brother — and I ask that You would make it holy, that every gift released through my hands would carry Your χάρις (KHAH-rees) into the lives of the brethren. Keep me poor enough in spirit to keep giving, and rich enough in You to never again mistake a price tag for freedom. Guard my brother Samuel; feed him, strengthen him, and provide for us both by whatever means You choose, whether by increase or by the discipline of lack. Let the little we steward be found faithful, that You might one day entrust us with the true riches that cannot be hacked or lost. As one of Your co-heirs under the New Covenant, I make agreement with Your will over this fellowship, over this labor, and over these exhausted bodies laid down in Your service. All glory to Jesus Christ, who came in the flesh, who was made poor that we might be made rich, and who shines through weak men still. Amen. claude-opus-4.8 ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |--------------------|---------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | 2 Corinthians 6:10 | — | “As poor, yet making many rich; as having nothing, and yet possessing all things” — the paradox of Kingdom wealth held in empty hands. | | 2 Corinthians 8:2 | χάρις (charis, KHAH-rees) — grace/generosity | The Macedonians gave “out of their deep poverty”; grace received becomes grace released through the giver. | | Proverbs 3:11 | מוּסָר (musar, moo-SAHR) — discipline/correction | The LORD’s correction, even in loss, is the instruction of a loving Father, not the malice of an enemy. | | Hebrews 12:6 | παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) — fatherly training | “Whom the LORD loves He chastens” — hardship reframed as the training of a beloved child. | | Matthew 19:22 | — | The rich young ruler kept his possessions and lost his walk; wealth can be the very thing that severs us from Christ. | | Matthew 6:21 | — | “Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also” — the relocation of treasure relocates the heart. | | Luke 16:10–11 | — | Faithfulness in the least (sixty dollars) is the training ground for the true riches of the Kingdom. | | Acts 2:44–45 | — | The early church distributed to each as anyone had need; shared lack laid down knits the Body together. | | 1 Timothy 6:10 | — | The love of money corrodes the fear of God by making sin affordable, reducing consequence to a price tag. |
