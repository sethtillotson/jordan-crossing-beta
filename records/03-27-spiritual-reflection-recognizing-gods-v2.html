# 03-27 Spiritual Reflection: Recognizing God’s Work in Unlikely Transformation **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation (casual phone call between friends) with embedded personal reflection moments from Seth **Personal Meditation: High on Jesus — When God Moves in the Margins of Ordinary Life** ## Opening Observation Something in me has been sitting with this conversation longer than I expected. What began as a casual catch-up with my brother — talking schedules, business, tools, and trips — quietly became a window into something the Spirit has been pressing on me for some time. There is a tension I carry between the rawness of where I come from and the holiness of where God is taking me, and I find that tension does not dissolve when I pick up the phone and talk to an old friend — it shows up there too, perhaps most honestly. What I am seeing is that God does not wait for polished moments to reveal Himself; He moves in the margins, in the mess, in the laughter, and in the half-said holy things. **Why This Matters:** The life of faith is not lived exclusively in sanctuaries and study rooms. It is lived in ordinary exchanges — in conversations about vans and tools and travel plans — and the question the Spirit keeps asking me is whether I am attentive enough to recognize Him there. The danger for anyone walking deeper into the Word is developing a kind of spiritual double-life, where the sacred exists in one compartment and the relational, earthy, familiar world exists in another, never quite touching. But I do not believe that is the Kingdom Jesus described. He spoke of leaven hidden in meal, of seeds sown in dirt, of a Father who clothes the grass of the field and numbers the hairs on a man’s head. The Kingdom permeates. And so I find myself asking: am I carrying what God has been building in me — the testimony, the rebukes against shallow religion, the growing sobriety of mind — into every room I enter, including the informal ones? That question is not a comfortable one, and it is exactly the kind of question I believe the Spirit was asking me even while I was laughing and catching up. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, a phone call like this one reads as exactly what it appears to be: two old friends reconnecting after time apart, talking about the logistics of life — business growth, travel plans, shared memories, the grinding rhythm of building something from the ground up. The world would affirm the entrepreneurial hustle, celebrate the sixty-hour weeks, admire the licensing and the bond and the tools accumulated. It would locate meaning in progress — in the van, the dump trailer, the hundred and thirty houses. And it would frame the spiritual references in the conversation as harmless color, the kind of thing religious people say without necessarily meaning anything by it: “Praise the Lord,” “All glory to God,” passing phrases dropped into otherwise secular conversation. The natural mind would not press past the surface of those moments. It would not ask what it means that a man who is building a business and fighting to keep his life together is also, quietly, going to church more — and what that might signal. **The Limitation:** But there is something the natural mind simply cannot perceive here, and it is not hidden behind complexity — it is hidden behind familiarity. Familiarity is one of the great blinds of the flesh. When we have known someone for years, when we share a vocabulary of inside jokes and old stories and mutual chaos, we can miss the most significant thing happening in the room: the slow, sovereign movement of God in a human life. My brother mentioned almost in passing that he has been going to church a lot. He said it between conversations about payroll software and Florida trips, as though he were not quite sure what to do with it himself. The natural mind hears that and files it away as interesting background information. But something in me — something trained by the Spirit through weeks and months of exactly this kind of attentiveness — recognized it as the most important sentence in the entire conversation. God was there, in that breath. And the eyes of flesh are not built to catch it. ## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation As I listened to my brother move through that conversation — the jokes, the business updates, the logistics of schedules and distance — I kept returning to something he said almost under his breath: *“Yeah, I’ve been going to church a lot too.”* He said it like a man who does not yet have a theological framework for what is happening to him, but who knows something is happening. And I know that feeling. I know it in my bones because I lived it. There is a season the Spirit initiates that does not announce itself with trumpets or theological certainty — it announces itself with a quiet pull, a hunger you cannot quite name, a sense that the old roads are no longer sufficient. I believe my brother is in that season. And what I am learning is that my role in his life in this moment is not to preach at him — it is to be present, to be faithful, to offer what I have been given without forcing the door. That is why I shared my testimony. Not as a performance. Not as a presentation polished for a platform. I said it plainly: this is not a sermon. It is ninety-seven minutes of raw, real, ordered witness. And I structured it chiastically — a literary and theological form that traces the fingerprint of God across both Testaments. The chiasm, that ancient A-B-C-B-A structure found throughout Hebrew poetry and the architecture of the Gospels, converges everything at the center. Whatever is most essential to the testimony stands at the apex. That choice was not accidental. When I began to understand how Scripture itself is structured this way — how so much of the canon draws the reader’s eye to the center, to the hinge, to the moment of inversion and revelation — I could not build my testimony any other way. I wanted the form of the telling to match the truth being told: that God brings everything to a point of convergence, and that point is always the Cross. I am pressed by something when I think about the ninety-seven minutes and what my brother said when I mentioned it: *“What the fuck?”* And I laughed. But underneath the laughter I am asking a sincere question — what does it mean to give a full account? The Apostle Paul, in Acts 26, stood before Agrippa and gave the full arc of his testimony, holding nothing back — the zeal he once had for persecution, the encounter on the road, the light that blinded him, the commission that followed. He was not brief. He was complete. And Agrippa said, *“You almost persuade me to become a Christian”* (Acts 26:28, NKJV). Completeness is not vanity. There is something holy about a man who refuses to abbreviate what God has done in him simply because the length makes others uncomfortable. What arrests me further in this conversation is the tension around substances. I said something I knew was unresolved even as I said it — that my polysubstance use leads me back to prayer, that it functions as training grounds for returning to God. I believe I said this honestly rather than defensively, and yet I also hear the Spirit pressing me to hold that statement carefully rather than settling into it. Scripture does not condemn experience brutally, but it does call us toward something: νήφω (nēphō, NAY-foh) — to be sober-minded, watchful, self-restrained. The Apostle Peter uses this word in urgent succession: *“Be sober, be vigilant; because your adversary the devil walks about like a roaring lion, seeking whom he may devour”* (1 Peter 5:8, NKJV). The word νήφω (NAY-foh) does not primarily refer to the chemistry of intoxication — it refers to the posture of the mind and spirit, a readiness that cannot be dulled. I am not ready to issue a final verdict on the whole conversation I had with myself aloud in that moment, but I am sitting with the tension honestly. And I think that is the right posture — not rushing to self-justification, not rushing to condemnation, but remaining open to what the Spirit is actually asking of me in this season. What I said about modern Western Christianity — that I have rebukes but hold my tongue until I am sent — this is something the Spirit has been forming in me with increasing clarity. There is a difference between the rebuke that comes from irritation and the rebuke that comes from commission. Jeremiah knew this tension. He tried to hold the word of the Lord inside himself — *“His word was in my heart like a burning fire shut up in my bones; I was weary of holding it back, and I could not”* (Jeremiah 20:9, NKJV). But Jeremiah also spoke with precision and timing, not simply out of overflow. The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom (Proverbs 9:10), and wisdom knows when to speak and when to sit with the weight of what has been given. I am not ashamed of the rebukes I carry. I am only committed to waiting for the Spirit’s commission before I open my mouth in that capacity. That discipline, I am learning, is itself a form of worship. I think about my brother’s business — the van, the dump trailer, the lift, the sixty-hour weeks, the hundred and thirty houses — and I see the faithfulness of God working in the domain of provision. James 1:17 says every good gift and every perfect gift comes down from the Father of lights. That van is a gift. Those tools are a gift. The client who trusts him with her entire property portfolio is a gift. What the Spirit is showing me is that God does not limit His generosity to spiritual experience. He is generous in the material world too, and when a man begins quietly returning to God’s house while simultaneously watching his business expand and his livelihood stabilize, that is not coincidence — that is the Father drawing a prodigal with goodness. Romans 2:4 asks whether we have despised the riches of God’s goodness, forbearance, and longsuffering, not knowing that His goodness leads to repentance. I see that goodness at work in my brother’s life, and it moves me to gratitude and intercession rather than analysis. And yet I also hold the sadness of distance — of schedules that miss each other, of weekends that don’t align, of a trip to Florida that lands when I am heading to California. There is something bittersweet in the friendship between two men who are both in motion, building, growing, finding God again in their own strange and circuitous ways. The New Testament uses a word for the deep fellowship between brethren — κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-no-NEE-ah) — that means more than mere social proximity. It is a shared participation, a communion rooted not in convenience but in covenant. My friendship with my brother is not merely nostalgic. It is, I believe, κοινωνία (koy-no-NEE-ah) in formation — rough-edged, honest, often profane in its expression, but marked by something real beneath the surface. And I want to steward it well. Something in me keeps returning to the moment he said: *“We’re gonna see God.”* He said it as a joke — attached to a reference I will not elaborate — but I heard it differently. I have trained myself to listen for the accidental truths that fall out of people’s mouths before their filters catch them. *We’re gonna see God.* Yes. I believe that. Not necessarily in the way the joke intended, but in the way that every soul who has been placed in my life is being moved, however slowly, toward the One who made them. The Spirit is not limited by the frame of the joke. He hears the longing beneath the laughter. He always does. When I said *“High on Jesus lately”* — I meant it. I am not embarrassed by the idiom. There is a joy, a χαρά (chara, kha-RAH), that Paul describes as fruit of the Spirit in Galatians 5:22 — and that joy is not the thin, performed cheerfulness of institutional Christianity. It is a deep gladness rooted in encounter, in the certainty that the Living God is real and present and moving. I have been living in that gladness lately. It carries me through the welding, through the long hours, through the unresolved questions, through the conversations that go sideways and then right again. And I want my brother to taste it — not through a sermon, not through a lecture, but through the weight of what he hears when he presses play on my testimony and sits with ninety-seven minutes of honest witness. That is ultimately what this conversation is to me: a moment in the slow, patient economy of God’s pursuit of a man I love. Nothing dramatic happened. No altar call. No conversion. Just two old friends talking across the miles while something holy moved quietly in the background. I have come to trust those quiet movements. They are often the most lasting. The burning bush was, after all, a bush — ordinary, unremarkable to every eye except the one that turned aside to see. I want to be a man who turns aside. Even in a phone call. Even in the middle of talking about QuickBooks. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation surfaces is the impulse to compartmentalize — to treat holy things as belonging only to formal, sacred spaces and to leave relational, earthy conversations untouched by what the Spirit has been building. I want to name that impulse clearly: it is not humility. Humility carries the presence of God into every room. What compartmentalization actually protects is comfort — our own and the comfort of those around us who might find the fullness of our transformation inconvenient or strange. Holding the word of the Lord requires discernment about timing, not silence about its reality. ---
