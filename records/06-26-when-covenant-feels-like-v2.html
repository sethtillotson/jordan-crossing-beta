# 06-26 Call Reflection: Covenant Lament, Marriage, and Faith in a Hard Season **Title:** 06-26 at 07:16 — When the Covenant Feels Like a Weight You Cannot Carry **Recorded:** 2026-06-26 07:16:03 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I listened back to this call and something in me would not let it pass quietly into the archive of ordinary conversation. There is a rawness here that I do not want to dress up prematurely, because I think the Holy Spirit is doing something precise in the unpolished edges of it. I was not okay this morning — and more than that, I said so out loud, without a filter, to another man who loves God — and somehow that matters. What I am sitting with now is not the embarrassment of having vented, but the strange mercy that met me in the middle of my venting, through the voice of my brother. **Why This Matters:** The Western church has largely lost the category of the saint in agony. We have liturgies for celebration and formats for testimony, but we have very little room for the man who is covenant-bound, genuinely believing, and absolutely furious at the texture of his own life on a given morning. If we cannot hold that tension honestly — if we must perform okayness before we are allowed to be heard — then we have not built covenant community, we have built a social contract with religious branding. What happened in this call, imperfect and profane as it was on my end, is closer to the Psalms than most Sunday morning exchanges. And the Psalms, God kept. He put them in the canon. That tells me something about what He thinks of honest anguish offered in His direction. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the perspective of natural human reasoning, this conversation reads as a man under compounding stressors — relational friction at home, financial anxiety, physical exhaustion, unmet expectations, and the weight of an expanding household responsibility — venting to a friend who offers reasonable emotional support and practical encouragement. A counselor or therapist would likely identify the symptoms: sleep disruption, irritability, loss of motivation, a momentary ideation of escape or death, and the underlying frustration of feeling unseen and disrespected in his primary relationship. The worldly prescription would be self-care, better communication strategies with the spouse, perhaps a stress management routine, maybe a weekend away. The friend did well, the reasoning goes — he validated the feelings, reminded the man that hard seasons are normal, and offered presence. It is, on the surface, a relatable conversation between two tired young men navigating the real costs of adult life. **The Limitation:** What natural wisdom cannot perceive in this conversation is that the exhaustion I was expressing is not merely circumstantial — it is covenantal. My brother Samuel named it exactly, even through his own imperfect framing: *these are attacks*. The flesh reads that as metaphor, as a motivational reframe to help a discouraged friend feel like his suffering has a narrative. But the Spirit recognizes something the natural mind is structurally unable to process: that a man who has entered covenant with the living God becomes a specific target of resistance, and that the attacks are not random misfortune but directed pressure designed to produce exactly what I described — doubt, despair, isolation, the sense that nothing will smooth out, and the bitter wish to simply be done. The natural mind cannot distinguish between clinical burnout and the weight of a covenant being contested. Only the Spirit of God, illuminating the whole arc of Scripture, can hold both the reality of the pain and the meaning of it at the same time. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I keep returning to the Psalms because I think that is where the Spirit wants me to sit first. David wrote in Psalm 22:1, *“My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me? Why are You so far from helping Me, and from the words of My groaning?”* This is not a man who has lost faith. This is a man whose faith is so real, so anchored in a relational God, that the distance feels personal and devastating. What I said on that call — *I still bless His name, I just hate my life right now* — is not apostasy. It is, in a raw and unrefined form, exactly the kind of prayer the Psalter normalizes. The distinction matters enormously: I was not cursing God, I was groaning toward Him. There is a world of difference between a man who walks away and a man who wants to be taken — not because he has abandoned his covenant, but because the covenant itself has become the weight he cannot yet see the purpose of. That is lament. And lament is a form of prayer. The image that arrests me most in the transcript is when I said, *“Just take me.”* Spoken in frustration, yes — but I want to sit with what the Spirit does with that language rather than dismiss it as hyperbole. Elijah said something very close to this under the juniper tree in 1 Kings 19:4: *“It is enough! Lord, take my life, for I am no better than my fathers!”* What did God do with Elijah’s request to die? He did not rebuke him. He did not remind him of the pipeline revenue of the prophetic ministry. He sent an angel — twice — with bread and water, and said *“Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you.”* The God of Israel met a depleted prophet not with a theology lecture but with food, rest, and the simple acknowledgment that the road ahead was real and long and that the man needed physical care before he could hear anything else. I find enormous grace in that sequence. God is not embarrassed by my exhaustion. What Brother Samuel said — *you’ve got to have faith for them* — carries more weight than perhaps either of us registered in the moment. He was invoking a principle the New Testament makes surprisingly explicit: that intercession and faith can operate on behalf of another person’s breakthrough even when that person cannot sustain the believing in their own strength. The paralytic in Mark 2 was lowered through the roof by *their* faith, and Jesus, seeing *their* faith, spoke healing to *him*. There is a covenantal architecture to friendship in the Kingdom that goes beyond emotional support — it is priestly, it is substitutionary in a small and derivative sense, it is one member of the Body holding up what another member cannot hold in a given moment. My brother was doing that. He may not have had the theological vocabulary for it, but the Spirit was moving through him in exactly that pattern. I need to be honest about the marriage tension, because I believe the Spirit is not letting me pass over it. I described a dynamic in which my wife stepped into a provider and directive role during a season when I had not yet established the fruit she was waiting for — and that I responded to her directives with increasing resentment. I want to be careful here not to make her the villain of this meditation, because I do not believe that serves truth or serves her. What I am beginning to see, as I sit with this before the Lord, is that there are two real things operating simultaneously: there is a legitimate cry in me for the honor of headship that Scripture assigns to the husband — a desire to lead, to be trusted with that leadership, to not be managed — and there is also a wound in her, shaped by seasons of watching provision not arrive, that drove her toward control as a form of self-protection. Both of those things are real. Neither of them is simple. Ephesians 5:25 commands me to *love my wife as Christ loved the church* — and Christ did not lead the church by demanding its respect before He had demonstrated His sacrifice. He gave first. He laid Himself down first. I cannot begin with the authority and bypass the laying down. The Hebrew concept of headship — rooted in the word רֹאשׁ (rosh, ROHSH), meaning head, chief, beginning — carries a sense of initiation and source, not domination. The head is the place from which life flows downward. It is not the place from which commands issue to be enforced. When I frame the conflict primarily as *she tells me what to do and I hate it*, I am perceiving the symptom without yet receiving the surgical question the Spirit is pressing: *what has the head been initiating?* This is not condemnation. This is the Spirit doing precisely what He does — taking the anguish of a real problem and revealing the layer beneath the layer so that real healing can begin rather than a managed ceasefire. There is something significant in what I said about always giving advice that she does the opposite of. I want to name what the Spirit is surfacing there: it is possible that the pattern of advice-giving has itself become part of the relational friction rather than the solution to it. Proverbs 31 describes a wife who is trusted, who has latitude, whose husband *safely trusts* her (Proverbs 31:11). Trust in Scripture is not merely a feeling — it is a posture, a practice, a choice to believe before the evidence is complete. If every suggestion I offer carries underneath it an implicit audit of whether she complies, then I am not yet leading in love — I am testing for submission. And she can feel that, even if neither of us has named it. The Spirit is not excusing her responses. He is showing me the part of the architecture I am responsible for, because that is the only part I can actually work on. I return now to something my brother said near the end: *“We don’t walk to God because everything’s rosy. We walk to God because everything’s shit like this.”* Theologically imprecise in its language, but pastorally precise in its weight. The New Testament word for this is θλῖψις (thlipsis, THLIP-sis) — pressure, tribulation, the crushing weight that Paul describes in Romans 5:3 as the very thing that *produces* perseverance, which produces character, which produces hope. The sequence is not *wait until the θλῖψις (THLIP-sis) is over and then hope will arrive* — the sequence is that the pressure is the mechanism through which hope is developed. This is not a thought to say lightly. It is a hard word. But it is a true word, and it is what I sense the Spirit confirming through this conversation. There is also something I want to honor in my brother’s presence in this moment. He was playing pool. He picked up the phone. He did not try to fix everything. He did not immediately go to spiritual platitudes to close the discomfort down. He asked *why, what’s going on* — and then he listened long enough for me to actually say the hard things. Then, once he had listened, he spoke. That order — listen first, then speak — is the pattern of James 1:19: *“let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak.”* Friendship in the Kingdom is not manufactured in the moments when everything is going well. It is revealed in the moments when one of us calls from the middle of a hard morning and says the things that don’t sound very sanctified. That he stayed in that moment with me is a grace I do not want to take for granted. The mention of the baby on the way is significant, and I do not want it to disappear into the background noise of the conversation. A child is coming. A household is expanding. The financial pressure, the marital tension, the exhaustion — all of it is being compressed into the same season as an enormous act of creation and stewardship that God Himself has initiated. Psalm 127:3 declares that *“children are a heritage from the Lord, the fruit of the womb is a reward.”* The word translated *heritage* is נַחֲלָה (nachalah, nah-khah-LAH) — inheritance, portion, possession given by covenant. This child is not a complicating factor. This child is a covenant inheritance arriving in a contested season. The enemy does not intensify the pressure by accident in seasons of Kingdom fruitfulness. The attack is often a signal that what is coming is significant. What I most need to carry out of this conversation — beyond the theology, beyond the marital analysis — is the simple and irreducible truth my brother kept returning to: *God has me.* And when I cannot feel that, he said it for me. That is what the Body of Christ is for. Not to fix each other’s finances, not to solve each other’s marriages by phone call, but to hold the confession of faith over one another in the moments when the individual member cannot sustain it alone. That is what happened in this call, and I want to receive it fully rather than move past it quickly. ## Implications and Challenges The modern church often conflates emotional wellness with spiritual maturity, teaching implicitly that a growing believer should experience increasing peace and decreasing turbulence. But the canon does not support that template. Job, David, Jeremiah, Elijah, Paul, and even the Son of God Himself passed through seasons of profound anguish without those seasons signaling a deficiency of faith. The church must recover its theology of lament — not as a concession to weakness, but as a legitimate and biblical form of address to the living God. When we do not have room for lament, we produce silent sufferers who perform okayness in corporate worship while dying quietly at home. There is a real and dangerous tendency among covenant men to receive the language of headship and authority as permission to demand respect before demonstrating sacrifice. Ephesians 5 is unambiguous in its sequencing: the call to the husband precedes and exceeds the call to the wife in both length and weight. Husbands are compared not to kings issuing decrees but to Christ giving Himself. This does not diminish the legitimate longing to be honored in one’s home — it locates that longing within a larger calling that begins with giving rather than requiring. The Spirit is gracious in how He presses this, but He is not ambiguous. The prosperity instinct — the idea that numerical indicators of business progress are the same as the favor of God — requires ongoing discernment. My response in this call, insisting that nothing is real until the money is in the bank account, was more spiritually discerning than it may have sounded. Not because faith does not precede provision, but because the temptation to celebrate projections as though they were possessions is a form of premature praise that can short-circuit the very processes God uses to build character in the waiting. Let the faith be real. Let the work be real. And let the thanksgiving correspond to what has actually been given, not merely what has been projected. The impulse toward isolation — *leave me in the Amazon with no phone* — is real and understandable, but must be distinguished from the Spirit-led rhythm of withdrawal that Jesus modeled. Jesus withdrew to solitary places *to pray* (Luke 5:16), not merely to decompress. There is a difference between holy solitude and a flight from covenant responsibility. I can want the rest without making the escape the goal. The desire beneath the desire is the right one: to hear God’s voice again without the noise. That desire is holy. It just may not require the Amazon. Finally, the role of physical and material care in spiritual restoration should not be underestimated or spiritualized away. Elijah needed sleep and food before he could receive the word of the Lord again. I mentioned not having had good sleep, feeling stressed, not having things that helped me unwind. The temple of the Holy Spirit requires maintenance. Attending to basic physical rest and order in one’s personal environment is not a distraction from spiritual formation — it is part of it. The Spirit inhabits a body, and that body has needs that God Himself has taken seriously. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity does not mean we stop feeling the weight of hard seasons — it means we learn to bring the weight to God honestly rather than performing its absence. The Psalms teach us that God is not threatened by our fury or our grief. He is large enough to receive both the praise and the complaint. What He requires is that we stay in the conversation with Him rather than walking out of the room. Lament is not a failure of faith. It is faith refusing to go silent. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this meditation calls forth is threefold: to stay in the marriage and press toward the sacrifice that headship requires before demanding the honor it produces; to stay in covenant friendship and both give and receive the kind of honest, priestly presence that my brother demonstrated in this call; and to stay in the financial and vocational assignment even when the morning feels impossible, trusting that θλῖψις (THLIP-sis) is not the end of the sentence but the beginning of a longer word that ends in hope. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic saints must learn to discern the difference between a season of attack and a season of abandonment — because they feel identical from the inside but require entirely different responses. Abandonment calls for re-examination and repentance. Attack calls for standing firm, putting on the full armor, and refusing to let the pressure rewrite the covenant. This morning felt like abandonment. But the pattern of Scripture says it was attack. The posture from here is not surrender to the despair but a refusal to let the despair have the final word. We watch. We wait. We hold the Word. We let the Body carry us when we cannot carry ourselves. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who called out from a hard morning and found that Your presence had already arranged for a brother to answer the phone. I am not pretending this season feels light or that I have it together — You were there for that call, You heard every word, and You are not scandalized by any of it. I receive Your mercy over the rawness of it. I receive Your surgical love pressing into the marriage, into the ambition, into the exhaustion, and into the covenant that I sometimes feel pinned beneath rather than held within. Lord, You are the Author of that covenant, and I trust that the pressure of this season is not Your absence but Your hand, reshaping things at a level I cannot yet see. I speak agreement with Your Word over this household: that the nachalah You are sending is a sign of favor, not a compounding of burden. I speak agreement with Your Word over this marriage: that what You have joined, You are also sustaining, and that the love You have called me to give is not dependent on what I receive first. I intercede for my wife and for the child coming — that Your peace would be the atmosphere of this home even while we are still learning to build it. I thank You for Samuel, for his willingness to stay in the uncomfortable moment and speak truth into it. I bless Your name today not because the morning was easy but because You are still God in it, and because You kept Elijah alive under the juniper tree and fed him for the journey ahead. Do the same for me. The journey is too great for me in my own strength — I know that. But You are enough. You have always been enough. To You be the glory in this household, in this season, and in every hard morning that becomes testimony.
