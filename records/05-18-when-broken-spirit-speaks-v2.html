# 05-16 Call Reflection: Broken Spirit, Intercession, and God’s Accessibility **Title:** 05-16 at 17:33 — When the Broken Spirit Speaks Before God **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to this conversation unfold between myself and my brothers over a video call, something arrested me beneath the noise of chili peppers and shop runs and half-drunk negotiations — a single, unrehearsed theological moment that cut through everything else like a blade. The question was simple and slightly slurred at the edges: *What state of mind does God call upon? In which man does God call upon?* And yet beneath that question, something real and ancient was being pressed. I found myself answering from a place that felt less like my own cleverness and more like recalled revelation: a broken and contrite spirit is the one He will not despise. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the question of *access to God* — of what posture opens the heavens and what posture closes them — is not a peripheral theological curiosity. It is the hinge upon which intercession, repentance, and the entire economy of prayer swings. In a generation that has largely reduced prayer to emotional performance and spiritual access to personal merit or charismatic volume, the quiet, almost throwaway confession of a half-drunk young man — “Seth, I feel like my spirit’s always broken” — becomes something the Holy Spirit refuses to let pass unremarked. If we miss what God is doing in moments like that, we miss something essential about how the Kingdom operates. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the natural vantage point, this conversation is simply what it appears to be on the surface: a late-night video call between friends — one in North Dakota, one somewhere in the UK — involving alcohol, spicy chilis, a negotiated business arrangement, a detour through a corner shop, and scattered theological commentary woven between the chaos. The world would evaluate this by its most visible features: the drinking, the impulsive decision-making, the profanity, the distracted wandering through a store. At best, the natural mind might grant it the label of “entertaining fellowship.” At worst, it dismisses the theological moments entirely as the rationalizations of people using faith language to dress up ordinary behavior. The natural observer would see men who clearly care for one another, who are funny and real, but who are unlikely candidates for any serious encounter with the living God. **The Limitation:** But here is what the natural mind cannot perceive: God has never limited His speaking to pristine settings. The entire canon of Scripture is populated with broken vessels — a shepherd boy hiding from a father who forgot to call him, a prophet running from his own calling into the belly of a ship, a weeping woman accused of drunkenness in the very act of her most earnest prayer. What the flesh cannot compute is that the rawness of the moment, the unguarded nature of the exchange, the genuine question asked without performance or pretense, may actually constitute the very thin place where heaven descends. The Spirit is not scandalized by what the religious mind finds offensive. He is grieved by hardness, by pride dressed in piety, by the manufactured holiness that has no room for the genuinely contrite. The natural mind looks for God in the cathedral and misses Him in the corner shop. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sat with this conversation afterward — letting the Spirit press through what was noisy and what was signal — I kept returning to one exchange. My brother, drink in hand, mid-conversation about nothing particularly sacred, asked me what state of heart God calls upon. And before I could think to be theological, I answered from somewhere deeper than my study: the broken and contrite spirit. The Psalmist had already written it cleanly and without qualification: *“The LORD is near to those who have a broken heart, and saves such as have a contrite spirit”* (Psalm 34:18, NKJV). And then again: *“The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit, a broken and a contrite heart — these, O God, You will not despise”* (Psalm 51:17, NKJV). What I am seeing now, sitting with this exchange, is that the very setting of the conversation — unpolished, unscripted, loud with the world — did not disqualify the moment. It actually illuminated something about the nature of God’s accessibility. The Hebrew word at the center of Psalm 51:17 for “broken” is שָׁבוּר (*shavur*, shah-VOOR) — carrying the sense of something shattered, crushed, fractured by weight. And the word for “contrite” is דַּכָּא (*dakka*, dah-KAH) — meaning pulverized, brought low, ground down. David was not speaking poetically here. He was speaking from inside the worst season of his moral life, after Nathan the prophet had exposed adultery, orchestrated murder, and the death of a child. And what David understood — what I am pressing into again through this conversation — is that the broken posture is not merely emotionally useful. It is *covenantally positioned*. The crushed heart is not an obstacle to God’s presence. It is the altar upon which He chooses to descend. My brother’s simple declaration — *I feel like my spirit’s always broken* — was not a lament about spiritual failure. Or perhaps it was. But in either case, I recognized it as the language of someone who, however imperfectly, has not yet grown the callus of self-sufficiency over his soul. And that arrested me. Because that posture — that persistent sense of personal poverty before God — is precisely what Jesus names first in the Beatitudes: *“Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven”* (Matthew 5:3, NKJV). The Greek πτωχός (*ptōchos*, PTOH-khos) does not mean slightly lacking. It means utterly destitute, crouching, reduced to begging. The kingdom does not belong to the theologically sophisticated. It belongs to those who know they have nothing to offer but need. This brought me back to something I said in the conversation that I want to develop more carefully now. I spoke about intercession — about standing in the gap — and I said that a broken interior posture makes my prayers a sweeter fragrance before Him. This is not mere sentiment. The imagery of fragrant intercession runs through the entire priestly economy of Scripture. In Revelation 5:8, the four living creatures and the elders hold golden bowls full of incense, which are identified explicitly as *the prayers of the saints*. The smoke that rises is not the eloquence of the prayer or the theological precision of the petition. The fragrance is the surrender embedded in the posture. A broken spirit is incense before the throne. I also spoke about the nature of agreement between heaven and earth — what I described as God having made it this way, that He invites us into covenant agreement with His will. This is rooted in something far older than a conversational insight. The Hebrew concept of עֵדָה (*edah*, ay-DAH) — assembly, testimony, appointed meeting — carries the idea of standing at an appointed place at an appointed time for the purpose of bearing witness together with God. The intercessor is not merely petitioning. The intercessor is witnessing. Agreeing. Aligning the will of earth with the revealed will of heaven. And what I pressed in the conversation was the simplest form of this: childlike faith. *I don’t need to see the way. I pray with a faith that knows that God wants them to heal.* That sentence, which came out of me without calculation, is actually deeply rooted in the logic of Romans 8:26–27 — the Spirit Himself interceding for us with groanings that cannot be uttered, according to the will of God. The intercessor does not manufacture the prayer. The intercessor yields to the One who already knows the will. Then there was the moment — almost accidental in the flow of the conversation — where I quoted 1 Samuel 1:16 from memory. Hannah’s cry to Eli: *“Do not consider your maidservant a wicked woman, for out of the abundance of my complaint and grief I have spoken until now.”* What is remarkable is the context. Anna — Hannah — had been so deeply in prayer, so undone by her barrenness and her grief, that the high priest of Israel mistook her for a drunk woman. Eli saw her lips moving and her body swaying and concluded she was intoxicated. But she was not drunk with wine. She was drunk with the weight of her petition. This typological echo sitting inside a conversation where one of us had actually been drinking was not lost on me. The Spirit has a particular sense of irony that is not careless. The very accusation Eli leveled against Hannah — *you are drunk* — was present in this conversation in real time, and yet the same dynamic was also present: beneath the surface of what looked like something unspiritual, there was a genuine reaching toward God. The contrast between Hannah’s sobriety-in-appearance-of-drunkenness and what I said about the Holy Spirit sobering the drunk was worth developing further. I said that those who are indwelled by the Spirit receive clarity, and that the Spirit does the opposite of intoxication. This is precisely the contrast Paul draws in Ephesians 5:18: *“And do not be drunk with wine, in which is dissipation; but be filled with the Spirit.”* The Greek πληρόω (*plēroō*, play-ROH-oh) — to be filled, to be controlled by — stands in opposition to the wine that removes inhibition from the outside in. The Spirit fills from the inside out, producing not vacancy or confusion but clarity, sobriety of judgment, and alignment with divine purpose. The drunk man cannot intercede with precision. But the Spirit-filled man — even the one who carries grief, even the one whose spirit feels perpetually shattered — intercedes with an accuracy that exceeds natural intelligence. I then made a comment about David that I want to press more deeply now. I said that David, despite having multiple wives and despite committing adultery and murder, remained a man after God’s own heart because of his *metanoia* — his repentance. The Greek μετάνοια (*metanoia*, meh-TAH-noy-ah) — a turning of the mind, a complete reorientation of thought and direction — is what distinguished David not from men who sinned less, but from men like Saul who sinned and then managed their sin rather than mourning it. David’s cry in Psalm 51 was not damage control. It was devastation. *“Do not cast me away from Your presence, and do not take Your Holy Spirit from me”* (Psalm 51:11, NKJV). David understood that the worst possible consequence of his sin was not public shame, not military defeat, not even the death of his child — it was the withdrawal of God’s presence. That prioritization of the presence of God above every other loss is the very definition of the man after God’s own heart. And this brings me to the most quietly significant thing that happened in the conversation: the moment I told my brother that calling me while he was drinking — aware that he was making choices he had said he would not make — was itself the correct posture. He had called me earlier in the evening to tell me what he was about to do, knowing that I would remember what he had said when he was sober. I said, *that in itself is the correct posture.* I want to be careful not to moralize what was simply a real moment between brothers. But what I am seeing is that the act of inviting accountability — of calling someone who will remember your sober word and hold it next to your drunk word — is a pale but genuine image of what the broken spirit does before God. It comes into the presence of One who holds all our words, who remembers every covenant, who is neither fooled by our rationalizations nor finished with our transformation. The man who hides his sin preserves it. The man who brings it into the light — even imperfectly, even with a drink in his hand and excuses still forming on his lips — has begun the turning. Finally, what I cannot let pass without remark is the sovereign strangeness of the Spirit’s working in the details. I said it in the conversation and I will say it here again with more weight: I looked at my Bible out of nowhere, and the very first verse my eyes landed on was 1 Samuel 1:16 — Hannah’s defense of her broken and praying soul. In a conversation filled with noise and motion and digression, the Word arrived at exactly the right moment. That is not coincidence. The canon does not wander. The Spirit who inspired Scripture also directs its reading in real time. *“Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path”* (Psalm 119:105, NKJV) — not a floodlight that illuminates the whole road at once, but a lamp that gives enough light for the next step, the next moment, the next word that someone nearby needs to hear. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation places before us is the assumption that spiritual access requires spiritual performance. Much of contemporary Western Christianity — whether charismatic or conservative — operates with an unspoken belief that God is most available to those who have prepared adequately: who have prayed enough, fasted enough, studied enough, cleaned up enough. But the witness of Scripture is disruptive here. Hannah had done none of these things in the moment Eli found her. She was undone. She was weeping. She was so desperate she appeared intoxicated to the man who was supposed to be closest to God. And God answered her. The brokenness was not the barrier to the breakthrough. It was the door. A second challenge is the domestication of the concept of intercession. We have reduced intercession to a prayer list activity — a few minutes of organized petition before we get on with our day. But the broken-spirit intercession that Psalm 51 and the life of Hannah describe is something costlier and more continuous. It is a *posture*, not an event. It is the ongoing willingness to remain low enough that the weight of another person’s need actually reaches us, actually presses into us, actually costs us something. The intercessor who stands in the gap does not manage the gap from a comfortable distance. He stands in it, which means he feels the exposure on both sides. A third challenge sits inside my comment about David and metanoia. The modern church has largely replaced repentance with apology. Apology says *I am sorry I got caught, or sorry you were hurt.* Repentance — μετάνοια (meh-TAH-noy-ah) — says *my entire orientation toward this thing must change, and I am asking God to do in me what I cannot do in myself.* David’s Psalm 51 is not an apology. It is a surgical prayer for interior transformation: *“Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me”* (Psalm 51:10, NKJV). The Hebrew בָּרָא (*bara*, bah-RAH) — the same verb used in Genesis 1 for creation out of nothing — is the word David uses. He is not asking for a renovation. He is asking for a new creation. That level of self-awareness about the depth of his corruption is itself the mark of genuine repentance. A fourth challenge is the refusal of the Spirit to be contained by respectable settings. God spoke through a burning bush in the wilderness, through a donkey on a road, through dreams to pagan kings, through a net-full of fish on a tired morning after an empty night. If we wait for the sanctioned moment, the properly formatted prayer meeting, the doctrinally pure conversation before we expect the Spirit to speak — we will miss most of what He is doing. This does not license carelessness or excuse the pursuit of holiness. But it does insist that holiness is not the precondition of encounter. Encounter is often the beginning of holiness. Fifth, and finally: the theology of the childlike faith that I pressed in the conversation deserves to be rescued from sentimentality. Childlike faith is not naive faith. It is not faith that ignores the severity of the situation. It is faith that has computed the situation fully and then refused to let the situation define the ceiling of what God can do. When I said *I don’t need to see the way — I pray with a faith that knows that God wants them to heal*, I was not suggesting that healing is automatic or that God always heals in the way we expect. I was saying that the intercessor’s job is not to solve the problem. It is to agree with the known will of God and then leave the execution of that will entirely in His hands. That is not intellectual laziness. That is the highest form of theological trust. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity is not the elimination of the broken spirit. It is the preservation of it. The temptation of maturity is to replace brokenness with confidence, and confidence is good — but only when it is confidence in God, not confidence in our own spiritual condition. The mature intercessor has learned to stay low. The more we understand about God’s holiness, the less inclined we are to approach Him with our credentials. We come the way Hannah came — with no argument except our need and no offering except our grief. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Kingdom obedience in this season calls us to take seriously the ministry of standing in the gap. This is not the calling of a specialist class of Christians. Every believer who carries the indwelling Spirit is invited into intercession — into the agreement between heaven and earth that moves the hand of God in the affairs of people we love. What this requires practically is that we remain attentive to the Spirit’s promptings in unexpected moments, that we hold our sober words accountable against our weaker moments, and that we never mistake the absence of a formal setting for the absence of a divine invitation. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation demands is one of sustained attentiveness. The Spirit speaks in the moment when the Bible falls open. He speaks in the question that a half-drunk young man asks because he genuinely wants to know. He speaks through the canonical echo of Hannah’s prayer landing inside a conversation about drinking and chilis and business negotiations. We are not to manufacture the prophetic word. We are to remain so familiar with the canon, so attentive to the Spirit’s movement, and so positioned in genuine brokenness that when the kairos moment arrives — however unlikely its packaging — we are already there, ready to give the living word to the one who needs it. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who has been made a servant of the Word and a steward of the mystery You have entrusted to Your prophets — standing between the heavens and the earth, in agreement with what You have already declared, as one of the answers You have prepared before the foundations of the world. I thank You that You are not impressed by our settings or scandalized by our imperfection, that You hear the prayer offered from the corner of a garage, from the window of a windy street in North Dakota, from the mouth of a man who knows he has nothing to bring You but his need. I ask that You keep our spirits perpetually broken before You — not crushed by the enemy, but shattered by the weight of Your holiness and the depth of our dependence — so that our intercession would rise before You as fragrant incense from the golden bowls of the saints. Let the Word continue to fall open at exactly the right moment, and let us be faithful enough to read it aloud. Let the metanoia be real in us, not managed — not a sophisticated apology but a true turning, a true *bara*, a new creation where the old orientation simply ceases to exist. We declare with David that we do not want to live without Your Spirit, and we receive Your nearness not as something we earned but as something You promised to the crushed and the lowly. To You alone be glory in the church and in Christ Jesus to all generations, forever and ever. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Psalm 34:18 — “The LORD is near to those who have a broken heart, and saves such as have a contrite spirit.” | שָׁבוּר (shavur, shah-VOOR) — broken, shattered | God’s nearness is covenantally linked to interior fracture, not exterior performance. | | Psalm 51:17 — “The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit, a broken and a contrite heart — these, O God, You will not despise.” | דַּכָּא (dakka, dah-KAH) — crushed, pulverized, brought low | The contrite heart is not merely emotionally tender; it has been ground down under the weight of genuine self-knowledge before a holy God. | | Psalm 51:10 — “Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me.” | בָּרָא (bara, bah-RAH) — to create out of nothing (used in Genesis 1) | David’s prayer is not renovation language.
