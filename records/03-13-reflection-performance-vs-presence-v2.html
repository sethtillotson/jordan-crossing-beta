# 03-07 Reflection: Performance vs. Presence in Faith - The Cross Never Removed # **Reflection:** The Cross I Never Took Off — A Meditation on Performance, Presence, and the Fear That Draws Us Near --- ## ⚡ Opening Observation I find myself sitting with a conversation that arrested me — the image of someone who wore a cross for years, never removing it, yet never stepped foot inside a church building. Something about that strikes me as profoundly biblical in a way I didn't expect. There's a tension here between institutional performance and intimate presence, between the orchestrated liturgy and the unfiltered encounter, between knowing *about* God and knowing *God Himself*. **Why This Matters:** This reflection matters because it confronts the very heart of what discipleship actually is. It challenges the Western Christianity culture that has elevated Sunday attendance, theological precision, and social respectability above the radical, messy, unpolished reality of walking daily with the risen Christ. If we miss this distinction, we risk building a faith that looks impressive from the outside but remains hollow at the core — a performance without presence, a form of godliness that denies its power (2 Timothy 3:5, NKJV). --- ## 🌍 The Worldly Mind's Interpretation From a purely human perspective, this conversation would be analyzed through the lens of religious sociology and identity theory. The natural mind would see someone who maintained a symbolic token of faith (the cross necklace) as a form of cultural identity or psychological comfort — perhaps a talisman against existential anxiety, a marker of tribal belonging, or a vestige of childhood conditioning. The reluctance to attend institutional church would be framed as rebellion against authority, social anxiety, or perhaps justified critique of religious hypocrisy and performative ritual. The eventual return to communal worship would be understood as a psychological need for community, the resolution of cognitive dissonance, or the fulfillment of a developmental stage in spiritual maturity. **The Limitation:** This analysis, while not entirely wrong in its observations, fundamentally misses the *spirit* behind the phenomenon. It cannot account for the divine drawing, the work of the Holy Spirit across years of apparent absence, or the distinction between dead religion and living relationship. "The natural man does not receive the things of the Spirit of God, for they are foolishness to him; nor can he know them, because they are spiritually discerned" (1 Corinthians 2:14, NKJV). The worldly perspective can observe behavior but cannot perceive the heart — and God looks at the heart (1 Samuel 16:7, NKJV). --- ## ✝️ What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation I'm seeing something stunning here about the nature of true faith versus religious performance. This person wore a cross for years — from age sixteen or seventeen onward — never removing it, yet never attending church. At first glance, this seems contradictory. But as I sit with it, I realize this is actually the posture of someone who knew *who* they believed in, even when they didn't know *where* they belonged. There's a Greek word that keeps coming to mind: **πίστις** (pistis) — faith, trust, conviction. It's not mere intellectual assent to doctrine; it's a deep-seated trust in the character and reality of God. This person had pistis without the scaffolding of institutional religion, and that's profoundly biblical. The resistance to institutional church wasn't rebellion against God — it was discernment against religious theater. "It's so orchestrated; it's a performance," they said of Roman Catholic services. And isn't that exactly what Jesus condemned in Matthew 23? "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you are like whitewashed tombs which indeed appear beautiful outwardly, but inside are full of dead men's bones and all uncleanness" (Matthew 23:27, NKJV). The Greek word **ὑποκριτής** (hypokritēs) literally means "stage actor" — someone playing a part. When worship becomes theater, when the sanctuary becomes a stage, when congregants become an audience evaluating a performance, we've lost the plot entirely. What strikes me even more is the contrast drawn between two kinds of churches — the one where "everyone's dressed up to the teas" and judgment flows freely, versus the one where "if I walk in there in my pajamas, they will not judge me." This is the difference between seeing with fleshly eyes and seeing with spiritual eyes. In a Spirit-led community, "they don't see you as flesh; they see you as spirit." This echoes Paul's revolutionary statement: "Therefore, from now on, we regard no one according to the flesh" (2 Corinthians 5:16, NKJV). The Greek **κατὰ σάρκα** (kata sarka) — "according to flesh" — versus seeing people as Paul learned to see them: clothed in the righteousness of Christ, adopted children of the Most High, brothers and sisters in whom Christ dwells. I notice the Spirit drawing out a deeper truth about the fear of God. There's a critical distinction made in this conversation: "The fear of God is not terror in going to hell. It's the reverent awe of something so divine, so holy." The Hebrew word **יִרְאָה** (yirah) carries both meanings — terror and reverence — but the context determines which. The religious performance model trades in the wrong kind of fear: **פַּחַד** (pachad), dread and terror, the cowering fear of punishment. But the biblical fear of the Lord is yirah in its highest form — the breathtaking wonder that overtakes you when you encounter something infinitely greater than yourself, the trembling awe that combines love and reverence, intimacy and majesty. It's the fear that draws you *closer*, not the terror that drives you away. This conversation reveals something else: the difference between going to church and meeting God. "When you go into the church that I go to, when you go into the Catholic Church (at least for me), I felt like they just literally want to put the fear of God in you... It's almost like the message is there but you are doing it all wrong." This echoes exactly what God says through the prophet Amos: "I hate, I despise your feast days, and I do not savor your sacred assemblies... Take away from Me the noise of your songs, for I will not hear the melody of your stringed instruments. But let justice run down like water, and righteousness like a mighty stream" (Amos 5:21, 23-24, NKJV). God rejects worship that is theologically correct but relationally bankrupt, ritually precise but morally hollow. The progression described here — attending church, feeling unburdened, feeling good for a week, then forcing it, then stopping — is the trajectory of someone trying to maintain relationship through religious duty. "This time around, oh my soul, different. It's just this time I met Him." The Greek **συναντάω** (synantaō) means to meet, to encounter face-to-face. Not to learn *about* Him. Not to perform *for* Him. But to actually meet Him, person to Person, Spirit to spirit. That's the Kairos moment, the appointed time when chronological religion gives way to eternal encounter. I'm also arrested by the humility woven throughout this conversation about readiness. "The devil will try to hold you back. You know the doubt." And the response: "I realized I'd realized, like, okay this might lead to something here. But I am still in the middle. I am still sipping on milk at that point." The awareness that we are never "ready" in our own strength is actually the sign of true readiness. This is 2 Corinthians 12:9 lived out: "My grace is sufficient for you, for My strength is made perfect in weakness" (NKJV). The Greek **τελέω** (teleō) — "made perfect" — means brought to completion, finished, accomplished. God doesn't wait for us to be strong enough; He accomplishes His purposes precisely through our inadequacy, so that the glory goes to Him alone. The mention of Apollos is so fitting here. Here was a man "eloquent and mighty in the Scriptures" (Acts 18:24, NKJV), yet he only knew the baptism of John. He had theological precision but was missing the Holy Spirit. What did Priscilla and Aquila do? They "took him aside and explained to him the way of God more accurately" (Acts 18:26, NKJV). Not publicly. Not with condemnation. With grace and invitation. That's the model for correction in the Body — not to exalt ourselves, but to bring others closer to the fullness of Christ. The conversation circles back to this theme repeatedly: theology without Christ produces demons. "Demons have the correct theology. They know theology. That's how they've been able to manipulate the spiritual laws to deceive us." James 2:19 says it plainly: "You believe that there is one God. You do well. Even the demons believe—and tremble!" (NKJV). The Greek **φρίσσω** (phrissō) means to shudder with fear. Demons have perfect theology and zero love. They know truth and reject it. Western Christianity has sometimes produced the same outcome: perfectly orthodox doctrine devoid of transformative love. What I'm seeing here is that the Kingdom doesn't advance through performance or perfection — it advances through presence and surrender. "Come to Christ as you are." Not dressed up. Not theologically complete. Not emotionally stable. As you are. The parable of the prodigal son captures this: the father doesn't wait for the son to clean up before embracing him. He runs to him while he still stinks of pig slop, clothes him in the best robe, puts a ring on his finger, and throws a feast (Luke 15:20-24, NKJV). That's the posture of the Father toward every broken, doubting, inadequate child who turns toward home. There's a profound truth about personal relationship versus corporate religion that emerges here. "My personal relationship with God." "Whoa, whoa, whoa! We don't do that." But Scripture is saturated with the language of intimate, personal relationship. Jesus says, "My sheep hear My voice, and I know them, and they follow Me" (John 10:27, NKJV). The Greek **γινώσκω** (ginōskō) — "know" — is the same word used for Adam *knowing* Eve. It's intimate, personal, relational. Yes, we need the corporate Body. Yes, individualism without community becomes Gnosticism. But corporate religion without personal relationship becomes dead ritualism. We need both — the secret place of intimacy *and* the gathered assembly of the saints. The closing image that won't leave me is the cross worn continuously for years — never removed, even when church was absent. That's the posture of someone who knew, deep down, "I knew who had control." In a world obsessed with self-determination and personal agency, this is radical trust. The cross is the ultimate symbol of surrender — not our ability to perform, but Christ's finished work. "It is finished" (**τετέλεσται**, tetelestai) — paid in full, accomplished, completed (John 19:30, NKJV). Wearing that cross wasn't superstition. It was a daily, physical reminder: I am not my own. I was bought at a price. And the One who bought me is worthy of my trust. --- ## 🤔 Implications and Challenges **Brothers and sisters, this conversation lovingly confronts several deeply embedded patterns in modern Western Christianity, and I offer these not as condemnation but as an invitation into deeper freedom:** **1. The Performance Trap:** Many of our worship services have become theatrical productions where congregants are spectators rather than participants in the priesthood of all believers. If visitors feel they will be judged for wearing pajamas instead of their "Sunday best," we have created a culture that values external conformity over internal transformation. The early church met in homes, shared meals, and welcomed all comers without dress codes. When did we trade genuine community for religious theater? **2. Fear as Control Instead of Invitation:** Too often, Western Christianity has weaponized the fear of hell as a behavior modification tool rather than inviting people into the reverent awe of encountering a holy and loving God. Terrorizing people into compliance produces slaves, not sons. "For you did not receive the spirit of bondage again to fear, but you received the Spirit of adoption by whom we cry out, 'Abba, Father'" (Romans 8:15, NKJV). Are we introducing people to a Judge they must appease, or a Father who runs to embrace them? **3. Theology Without Transformation:** We have elevated doctrinal correctness to the point where some believers can recite systematic theology while walking past the hungry, the lonely, and the broken without stopping. James is blunt: "Faith without works is dead" (James 2:26, NKJV). Demons have perfect theology. The question is not "Do you know the truth?" but "Does the truth you know produce the love of Christ in you?" If our theology doesn't make us more like Jesus — who touched lepers, ate with sinners, and wept over cities — then we have knowledge without transformation, which profits nothing. **4. Discouraging Personal Relationship:** Any teaching that discourages believers from cultivating intimate, personal relationship with God — reducing faith to corporate participation alone — directly contradicts Scripture. Jesus Himself modeled withdrawal to solitary places to pray (Luke 5:16, NKJV). Paul speaks of revelations received in private encounter (2 Corinthians 12:1-4, NKJV). Yes, we desperately need the Body. But we also need the secret place. Churches that discourage "personal relationship" language may be, however unintentionally, keeping people from the very intimacy God desires with them. **5. Readiness as Pride:** The belief that one day we'll "arrive" and be complete Christians is subtle pride masquerading as ambition. Paul himself said, "Not that I have already attained, or am already perfected; but I press on" (Philippians 3:12, NKJV). The Christian life is not about reaching a plateau of self-sufficiency; it's about daily, lifelong dependence on Christ. Any teaching that implies we can become spiritually self-sufficient contradicts the Gospel of grace. **Grace upon grace, beloved. These corrections are offered in the spirit of Priscilla and Aquila — pulling us aside, not to shame, but to invite us more deeply into "the way of God more accurately." The goal is always love, always Jesus, always the transformation that comes from genuine encounter with the living God.** --- ## 🚀 What This Means For Us: **For Your Spiritual Maturity:** Come as you are — not as you think you should be. God is not impressed by your performance; He's captivated by your heart. Cultivate both the secret place of intimate prayer *and* the gathered assembly of the saints. Don't let anyone shame you for pursuing personal relationship with God, and don't let individualism rob you of the richness of corporate worship. Hold both in tension, as Scripture does. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Let reverent awe, not terror, shape your relationship with God. Fear Him in the biblical sense — stand in breathless wonder at His holiness and love, letting that awe draw you closer rather than drive you away. Examine your own heart: Is your faith built on performance (what you do for God) or presence (abiding in Christ)? Are you trying to earn approval, or resting in the finished work of the Cross? **For Prophetic Posture:** Wear your cross — literally or metaphorically — as a daily reminder that you are not your own, you were bought at a price, and the One who redeemed you holds all things. Resist the pressure to perform. Reject religious theater. Pursue genuine encounter. And when God calls you forward into something that feels too big, remember: your inadequacy is the very place where His strength is made perfect. Go boldly before the throne of grace, knowing you are clothed in the robes of righteousness, not because of what you've done, but because of what He has done. --- ## 🔥 A Closing Prayer Father, thank You for the cross we wear — not as superstition, but as surrender. Thank You that You don't wait for us to clean ourselves up before You embrace us. Thank You that Your grace extends beyond the measure of our performance, beyond the boundaries of our understanding, beyond the limits of our strength. Forgive us for the times we've traded relationship for ritual, encounter for entertainment, intimacy for institution. Teach us to fear You rightly — not cowering in terror, but trembling in awe at Your holiness and love. May our worship be genuine, our theology transformative, and our lives a living testimony that we have met the risen Christ. Clothe us in humility. Fill us with Your Spirit. And let every conversation, every encounter, every moment draw us and those around us closer to You. We surrender this understanding back to You, Lord. Let it bear fruit in our lives and in Your Kingdom. In Jesus' name, Amen. --- ## 📝 Scripture References & Original Language Notes | Passage (NKJV) | Greek / Hebrew / Aramaic | Theological Insight | |----------------|--------------------------|---------------------| | 1 Corinthians 2:14 | φυσικὸς ἄνθρωπος (physikos anthrōpos) - "natural man" | The unregenerate person cannot perceive spiritual truth; it requires the illumination of the Holy Spirit. | | Matthew 23:27 | ὑποκριτής (hypokritēs) - "hypocrite/stage actor" | Jesus condemns religious performance that prioritizes external appearance over internal reality. | | 2 Corinthians 5:16 | κατὰ σάρκα (kata sarka) - "according to flesh" | Paul shifts from fleshly evaluation to spiritual discernment in how we see others. | | Proverbs 1:7; 9:10 | יִרְאָה (yirah) - "fear" (reverent awe) | The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom — reverent awe that draws near, not terror that drives away. | | Amos 5:21, 23-24 | Multiple Hebrew terms for religious festivals and justice | God rejects worship that is ritualistic but devoid of justice and righteousness. | | 2 Corinthians 12:9 | τελέω (teleō) - "made perfect/complete" | God's strength is brought to completion specifically in our weakness, not our self-sufficiency. | | Acts 18:26 | ἀκριβέστερον (akribesteron) - "more accurately" | Correction in love brings greater precision in understanding God's way, not condemnation. | | James 2:19 | φρίσσω (phrissō) - "tremble/shudder" | Even demons have correct theology and tremble; faith without love and transformation is demonic. | | John 10:27 | γινώσκω (ginōskō) - "know" (intimate knowledge) | Jesus knows His sheep with intimate, personal knowledge, not distant acquaintance. | | John 19:30 | τετέλεσται (tetelestai) - "It is finished" | The finished work of Christ — paid in full, accomplished completely. | | Romans 8:15 | υἱοθεσία (hyiothesia) - "adoption" | We receive the Spirit of adoption, not bondage; we are sons, not slaves. |
