# 05-16 Conversation Reflection: Sacred Encounters in Ordinary Dialogue — Faith, Kenosis, and Kingdom Vision **Title:** 05-16 at 11:44 — When the Ordinary Conversation Becomes Holy Ground **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation (Live Capture) **Classification:** Conversation Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to the recording of this conversation between myself and my brother Samuel, what struck me most was not any single theological point but the terrain through which we traveled to arrive at them — the mundane logistics of email marketing campaigns, pricing tiers, delivery problems, and platform comparisons, until suddenly the conversation cracked open into something far deeper than either of us had planned. There is a particular grace in conversations that begin in the ordinary and end in the holy, and this one did exactly that. What began as a technical consultation became, without ceremony or announcement, a reflection on the nature of faith, the discipline of God, the beauty of Hannah’s vow, the kenosis of self-emptying trust, and the vision of a Bible study that might reach a hundred and fifty thousand souls through nothing more than a monthly email subscription and the willingness to show up before God with empty hands. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Church in every generation is tempted to separate the sacred from the secular — to believe that God is only present in the sanctuary, only active in the prayer meeting, only speaking through the sermon. But what this conversation reveals, almost accidentally, is that the Kingdom has no such separation. The same exchange that discussed MailChimp pricing tiers and SMTP delivery rates also produced a serious meditation on George Müller’s prayer life, on the kenosis of expectation in encountering God, on the interior Judas who professes with the mouth while doubting with the heart, and on the canonical arc from Hannah’s barrenness to Samuel the last seer. If we learn to pay attention — genuinely pay attention — to the conversations God places us in, we will find that He is threading revelation through the ordinary with extraordinary patience and precision. To miss this is not merely to miss a theological insight; it is to miss the very mode by which the Spirit most consistently teaches those who are willing to hear. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation Taken at face value through the lens of natural human reasoning, this conversation is simply two friends catching up — one helping the other solve a practical business problem, the two of them swapping humor, discussing music, and eventually making loose plans for a Bible study later that afternoon. The natural mind would observe two young men with entrepreneurial energy, a casual command of marketing concepts, some Christian cultural familiarity, and a warm friendship expressed in the informal language of everyday speech. It might appreciate the humor about Jesus reels, note the ambition in the email campaign idea, acknowledge the sincerity in the conversation about George Müller, and file it all away as a pleasant but unremarkable exchange. The secular analyst would perhaps nod at the communal warmth and leave it there — two friends talking, nothing more. **The Limitation:** What the natural mind cannot perceive is that beneath the texture of this casual conversation, the Holy Spirit was quietly assembling something. The ordinary mind processes information laterally — surface to surface — and therefore cannot sense the vertical dimension of a moment where two brothers, without premeditation, find themselves speaking the language of the Kingdom in the middle of a conversation about delivery rates. The one who has not been born from above cannot receive the things of the Spirit because they are spiritually discerned — not because Scripture is a locked book, but because the interior faculty required to perceive divine movement within human conversation has not yet been awakened. What appears to the natural mind as friendly theological small talk is, to the discerning ear, a map of discipleship in progress — faith growing through friendship, doctrine being worked out in real time, and the Spirit quietly pressing a vision of mass gospel distribution into two men who started the conversation talking about law firms and accountancy leads. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with this conversation and listen to it again through the ear of the Spirit, I am arrested first by the image that appears almost without warning near the middle of the exchange — the image of sending a hundred and fifty thousand emails not to sell business leads, but to invite strangers into a Bible study. I want to be careful not to inflate this beyond what it was: it was a spontaneous, half-joking, genuinely enthusiastic idea between two brothers who got caught up in the mathematics of open rates and Zoom capacity. And yet — the Spirit did not let it go. The conversation circled back to it, sharpened it, began to give it theology. My brother Samuel said it would have to be free, without conditions. I agreed and added that any donation would have to be compelled by the Spirit alone, and that every fund raised would go only back into the mission of spreading the gospel further. That is not marketing strategy. That is Kingdom economy taking shape in real time. I think of the way the Spirit has always worked through improbable instruments and impractical-sounding plans. Gideon reduced his army to three hundred men before God would give him the victory — not because three hundred was the logistically sound number, but because God required the outcome to be unmistakably His own. When my brother and I sit here calculating close rates and email delivery fees and Eventbrite capacity limits, there is something holy underneath the arithmetic. The question being asked — whether we know it or not — is whether we are willing to be the vessel, the willing instrument, the three hundred, while trusting that God will handle the outcome. George Müller’s name appeared in this conversation as it always does when this territory is entered, and rightly so. Müller never once asked a human being for funds during fifty-seven years of ministry. He cared for ten thousand orphans across multiple houses in Bristol. The equivalent of a hundred and ten to a hundred and fifty million dollars passed through his hands, and not once did he solicit it from people. He believed that God was perfectly capable of moving the hearts of givers without Müller’s manipulation, appeal, or pressure. That conviction was not naïve — it was theologically precise. It reflected a complete understanding of what the Kingdom economy actually is: not a charitable fundraising operation, but a living circulation of divine provision in which the Spirit compels the right person, at the right moment, to release the right resource in the right direction. My brother named it exactly right — it should never come to the host; it should come to the steward of Kingdom resources, and then go back out into more proclamation. The conversation then moved into a place I was not expecting: the moment my brother Samuel described how the closer he drew to God during that difficult season, the further certain things in his life — and certain people — seemed to move away. I have witnessed this before in the lives of those I love, and I recognize it as a spiritual reality that the natural mind will almost always misinterpret. When the רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — the Spirit, breath, and wind of God — begins to move powerfully into a person’s atmosphere, it does not produce only warmth and welcome. It also produces displacement. Darkness does not negotiate with light; it retreats from it. And what may look from the outside like personal instability or relational collapse is often, in the Spirit’s economy, the necessary clearing of ground for something new to be planted. Demons flee, as I said in that moment, and the statement is not hyperbole — it is Scriptural and experiential. What struck me next as I was listening back is the thread that runs from there into our discussion of Job. My brother asked a sincere question — why does wavering faith still produce constant God-consciousness? And the answer I gave was essentially the answer of Job’s journey, though I did not name it that way in the moment. Job’s problem was not that he stopped thinking about God. If anything, Job never stopped thinking about God — he thought about God obsessively, even angrily, even boldly. His problem was that the framework through which he was thinking about God had become contaminated by the pride of his own righteousness. He had constructed, over many years of obedient living, an unconscious theology that translated to: *because I am righteous, disaster cannot touch me; and if it has, then something has gone wrong in the system.* And God, with breathtaking patience and sovereign authority, dismantled that framework not through argument but through encounter — through the whirlwind, through Leviathan, through unanswerable questions about cloud formation and morning stars and the gates of death. The Greek word παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) — fatherly training, discipline, the kind of correction that only a Father who loves gives — sits underneath the entire Book of Job, though Paul names it explicitly in the New Covenant. Hebrews 12 tells us that no discipline seems pleasant at the time, but painful — yet afterward it yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness for those who have been trained by it. Job was trained by the whirlwind. He came out the other side saying something that no religious formula can produce: *I have heard of You by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees You.* The shift from secondhand theology — believing about God based on what others have told you — to firsthand encounter — actually seeing the face of God within the storm — is the whole arc of Job compressed into one verse. And I believe that is what my brother Samuel is in the middle of right now, and what I have been in the middle of for longer than I sometimes want to admit. The kenosis thread — the word κένωσις (kenōsis, keh-NO-sis), the self-emptying described in Philippians 2 where Christ emptied Himself of the independent exercise of His divine prerogatives to become fully incarnate — came up in our conversation in a slightly different form. I used the word directly in the transcript, and I want to expand what I meant by it. I said that the encounter with God does not happen because God is trying to prove He exists. The encounter happens because you already believe He exists. That is kenosis applied to the posture of faith: you must first empty yourself of the demand for proof, of the expectation of a spectacular sign, of the conditional posture that says *I will fully commit when I have seen enough to justify commitment* — and then, from that emptied and trusting place, you open the door. *Behold, I stand at the door and knock* (Revelation 3:20). He is already knocking. The question is always whether we will open it, not whether He is willing to enter. The interior Judas — the unbelieving believer, the one who professes with the mouth while privately holding out for something more convincing — is the one who stands at the door and hears the knocking and says, *prove to me first that it is really you, and then I will open.* But that is not faith. That is negotiation with the divine. The conversation about Hannah moved me deeply, and I want to honor it properly here. Samuel — my brother — noted something true and important: Hannah was among the first women in Scripture to make a vow to God not out of desperation alone, but with genuine intention to honor it. She promised that if God opened her womb, she would return the child to the house of the LORD for all the days of his life (1 Samuel 1:11). And she kept it. She brought Samuel to Eli and she gave him back. I said in the conversation — and I stand by this — that we should not miss the Spirit’s hand in the vow itself. Hannah did not generate that prayer from pure human maternal longing alone. The ruach (ROO-akh) of God came upon her to produce that very prayer, which means God had already determined to give Samuel to Israel before Hannah formed the words to ask for him. The vow was not Hannah purchasing her miracle through a spiritual transaction — it was God moving Hannah into alignment with what He had already purposed, so that she could participate in it with her whole self, including her willingness to give the child back. This connects directly to what I said about Jephthah — though I did not name him in the conversation — when I described the king who made the rash oath before battle, promising the first thing that came out of his house as a burnt offering if God gave him victory. That was Jephthah in Judges 11, and the first thing to emerge from his house upon his return was his daughter, dancing with tambourines. He tore his clothes. He said, *You have brought me very low* (Judges 11:35). The contrast between Hannah and Jephthah is the contrast between a vow born of Spirit-led alignment and a vow born of anxious, transactional, works-based bargaining with God. Jephthah could not simply trust that God was with him. He needed to purchase the victory with a promise — and the price was devastating precisely because it was unnecessary. God was already with him. The victory was already His to give. The oath added nothing except tragedy. And this, finally, is where the conversation’s theological river runs toward the sea. My brother and I spoke about works-based salvation — how even people who intellectually argue against it often use language that reveals they still secretly believe that God’s acceptance of them is conditional upon their behavioral performance. The whole Old Testament is, as I said, a mirror of the New Testament — shadow and substance, type and antitype, pattern and fulfillment. Jephthah bargaining with God before battle mirrors the modern believer secretly bargaining with God in prayer. The unwise oath mirrors the works-based psychology that says *if I do X, God will do Y* — which is a perfectly understandable human response to an overwhelming sense of divine transcendence, but which ultimately collapses under its own weight when tested by the kind of whirlwind God sent to Job. The only correction is the one Paul announces in Ephesians 2:8-9 and the one Jesus embodied in His whole life: **it is by grace, through faith, and not of works, so that no one can boast.** The only door is Christ. The only currency is trust. ## Implications and Challenges There is a pattern in Western Christian culture of compartmentalizing conversation — believing that a conversation is spiritual only if it begins with Scripture, ends with prayer, and maintains a certain measured tone throughout. This conversation does none of those things. It begins with email marketing and ends with plans for a Bible study over Hannah and Samuel. That the Spirit was present throughout the entire arc is either unbelievable or liberating, depending on what we have been taught about how God moves. The challenge here is to learn to listen for Him across the whole bandwidth of ordinary life, not just in its formal religious registers. The vision of sending the gospel to a hundred and fifty thousand people through an email list must be held loosely, soberly, and with genuine accountability. This is a vision worth stewarding. It is also a vision that, without integrity of character, financial transparency, and consistent theological grounding, could become precisely the thing it is trying to distinguish itself from. The challenge is not to avoid the vision but to build it on the foundation that cannot be shaken — genuine prayer, genuine accountability, and the George Müller-like refusal to ever manipulate or pressure people into giving. If God is in it, He will fund it. If He does not fund it, we have our answer. The theology of kenosis — self-emptying trust — confronts the modern church at a particularly tender point. We live in an era of spiritual consumerism where the encounter with God is often marketed as a product: come to this conference, attend this retreat, buy this book, and you will experience a breakthrough. But κένωσις (keh-NO-sis) moves in the opposite direction. It requires the emptying of expectation, the surrender of the demand for a spectacular experience, and the willingness to say *I choose You, I trust You* before the burning bush appears. The challenge for the contemporary believer is to resist the consumer model of faith and return to the posture of a servant who trusts the Master’s goodness before the evidence arrives. The concept of the interior Judas — the unbelieving believer — deserves more honest pastoral attention than it typically receives. Many people in evangelical churches have been told that as long as they prayed the sinner’s prayer, they are secure. And while salvation by grace through faith is not in question, the discipleship that follows that profession is absolutely in question. The person who professes Christ but privately holds God at arm’s length, waiting for irrefutable proof before fully surrendering, is not on a different road than the rest of us — they are on the same road, simply earlier in the journey. The pastoral challenge is not to condemn them but to name what is happening honestly and invite them further in. The story of Hannah and the contrast with Jephthah’s rash oath should cause every praying believer to examine the texture of their own petitions. Are we making vows to God that we cannot or will not keep, attempting to secure His favor through spiritual performance? Or are we praying with the confidence of one who already believes God’s heart is good — not bargaining with Him but aligning with Him, not purchasing His blessing but receiving it in the open hands of surrender? The challenge is to move from transactional prayer to covenantal conversation. Finally, the discipleship dynamic visible in this conversation — where one brother is further along in theological formation and the other represents, as Samuel himself put it, the honest confusion of ninety-nine percent of people — is a model worth recovering in the Church. We have professionalized spiritual formation to the point where many believers believe they can only learn from credentialed teachers behind pulpits. But Paul built the Church through networks of mentorship, through the traveling companionship of Barnabas and Silas and Timothy, through letters written to communities wrestling with real problems in real time. What this conversation demonstrates is that theology done in friendship — done honestly, imperfectly, with laughter and reverence in the same breath — is not an inferior form of discipleship. It may be the most durable form we have. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity is not the ability to speak only in refined theological register. It is the capacity to recognize the movement of the Spirit across the full terrain of life — in email marketing conversations, in the discovery that a friend came to Christ in the same week a marriage began to fracture, in the mathematics of email open rates that suddenly become the mathematics of Kingdom reach. Growing in maturity means developing the kind of attentiveness that does not require a church building or a formal study session to encounter God — it requires a listening heart in whatever room you happen to be in. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience called forth here is threefold: to steward vision with integrity, to pray with open hands rather than transactional fists, and to keep the door of relationship and mentorship open to those who are earlier in the journey. If God places a hundred-and-fifty-thousand-person email list in front of us as a potential tool for the gospel, the obedient response is not to dismiss it as grandiose or to rush forward without accountability — it is to bring it before God in prayer and let Him confirm, deny, or redirect it in His own time and manner. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this conversation cultivates is the posture of the servant who trusts the Master’s plan before seeing it unfold — the posture of Hannah before the womb was opened, of Müller before the food appeared on the orphans’ table, of Job after the whirlwind when he finally said *now my eye sees You.* We are called to abide in that kind of trust — not passively, not naively, but with our eyes open and our hands working, believing that the path has already been walked by the One who goes before us, and that our task is simply to follow it faithfully, one day at a time, one conversation at a time. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I thank You for the grace that speaks through ordinary conversations and turns them into holy ground. I thank You for the brother You have placed beside me on this road — for the questions he asks that carry the weight of ten thousand people behind them, for the faith that wavers and yet cannot stop thinking about You, for the season You have brought him through that mirrors the same sovereign love You have pressed through my own life. Lord, I bring before You the vision of a Bible study that reaches beyond what two men in a room could ever manufacture on their own, and I set it down at Your feet with open hands. If it is Your will, make a way — not because we asked the right people, or sent the right emails, or calculated the right close rate, but because You are the God who calls things that are not as though they are, and who funds what You ordain without requiring a human hand to compel the giving. I renounce every transactional prayer I have ever prayed, every Jephthah oath offered in anxiety, every moment I stood at the door of encounter and demanded proof before opening it. Teach me the kenosis of trust — the self-emptying that says *I choose You before I see You clearly* — and let that be the ground on which everything You build through me is planted. Let the word go out. Let the hundred and fifty thousand hear it. Let the door be opened wide, that many who have only heard of You by the hearing of the ear might, in this season, come to see You. To the God who speaks from the whirlwind, who opens barren wombs, who rebukes Leviathan and raises the dead — be all glory, all honor, and all praise, now and in every age to come.
