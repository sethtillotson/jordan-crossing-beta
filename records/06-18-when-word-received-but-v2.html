# 06-18 Call Reflection: Parable of the Soils, Unforgiveness, and Kingdom Obedience **Title:** 06-18 at 07:36 — When the Word Is Received but the Thorns Are Already There **Recorded:** 2026-06-18 07:36:09 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother Samuel walk through what happened — the correction offered in love, the block that followed, the vile response through a third party — something in me recognized immediately that this was never really about Chloe. The Spirit had already moved before either of us fully named it: this was a training ground, and the lesson embedded in it was far older and far wider than one relationship. What arrested me most was not the rejection itself, but the *pattern* the rejection revealed — how a heart hardened by unforgiveness cannot receive even the most graciously delivered word of life, and how the soil determines the fruit of the seed regardless of the quality of the sower. **Why This Matters:** The ability to discern what kind of ground a word has fallen on is not a luxury reserved for seasoned theologians — it is essential equipment for anyone who intends to speak life into broken people, serve in any form of reconciliation ministry, or simply walk in obedience to the command to love one another well. If we cannot read the soil, we will perpetually misread the outcome. We will take rejection personally when it is actually diagnostic. We will shrink back from future obedience when the very resistance we encounter is confirmation that we spoke truth. And we will neglect the intercession that remains our duty even after the door closes — because the one who cannot yet receive is the one who most needs someone standing in the gap for them before the throne. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural standpoint, what my brother Samuel experienced looks like a painful but familiar social outcome: he reached out to someone from a complicated past, attempted what appeared to be a sincere and thoughtful conversation, and was met with hostility and disconnection. The natural mind would frame this as either a relational failure — perhaps he misjudged the timing, perhaps the wording was too forward, perhaps the wounds were simply too fresh — or as evidence that the relationship had simply run its course. It would counsel emotional detachment, perhaps some self-reflection about communication style, and the general wisdom that you cannot force someone to hear what they are not ready to hear. The world has language for this. It calls it “boundaries,” “closure,” “moving on.” It treats the outcome as primarily psychological and the lesson as primarily personal. At most, it might recognize a growth opportunity in the rejection — resilience, emotional regulation, the ability to wish someone well from a distance. **The Limitation:** But that entire framework, however compassionate it sounds, operates entirely within a horizontal plane. It can describe the surface of what happened without ever touching the depth of what was actually occurring. It cannot see that a word spoken in love is a seed with its own sovereign trajectory. It cannot recognize that a woman’s explosion of anger the morning after a calm and gracious correction is not primarily a relational response — it is a spiritual one. It cannot perceive that unforgiveness is not merely an emotional wound or a personality pattern, but an established principality in a heart that has been given over to it long enough that even tender truth feels like assault. The natural mind looks at the blocked number and sees a dead end. The Spirit-trained eye looks at the same blocked number and sees a soil diagnosis — and then turns toward intercession rather than conclusion. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sat with everything Samuel shared, the first thing that came alive in me was the Parable of the Sower — not as a theological abstraction but as an actual interpretive key placed in my hands by the Spirit in real time. I asked Samuel if he remembered the Four Soils, and as I began walking through them I could feel the text becoming active, not merely illustrative. This is one of the things I am learning about the Word: it does not wait politely in the background while life unfolds. It interprets life. It is the lens, not the decoration. And what the lens revealed in this moment was specific and arresting: the ground Samuel had encountered was not hardened path, and it was not shallow rocky soil. It was thorny ground. The Lord Jesus describes this soil in Matthew 13:22 — “Now he who received seed among the thorns is he who hears the word, and the cares of this world and the deceitfulness of riches choke the word, and he becomes unfruitful.” But when I look at this situation through the full witness of what Samuel described, the thorns are not cares and riches — they are something more personal and more ancient: a spirit of unforgiveness that has become so deeply rooted it has structured the soil itself. The pattern is important. She did not reject the word in the moment. She *sat with it*. She stayed quiet on the phone. The seed went in. But overnight, what was already there — the thorns — began their work. By morning, what had been received was choked, and what emerged in its place was hostility. This is what I find so theologically precise about the thorny soil: it is the most deceptive of the four because it initially appears responsive. The hardened path never receives the seed at all — the enemy snatches it immediately. The rocky soil receives it with joy and then withers under pressure. But the thorny ground actually receives the word long enough that you think something is happening. The quietness on the phone — that was real. That was the seed landing. But the established patterns of bitterness and unforgiveness were already there, already deeply rooted, and they do not die overnight simply because good soil was briefly tilled. What this tells me about pastoral work — and Samuel is already entering it whether he has yet named it that way or not — is that we are not responsible for what the soil does with the seed. We are responsible for sowing faithfully and sowing *well*. What Samuel did on that phone call was exactly right: he named her genuine strengths before naming the shadow those strengths were casting. He did not flatter. He did not condemn. He spoke truth wrapped in the acknowledgment of real worth. That is the Pauline model. That is the pattern I have been watching take root in him, and seeing it exercised externally for the first time was one of the most genuinely remarkable things I have witnessed in this season of discipleship. The pedagogy of Jesus — received, internalized, and then expressed outward — is a living thing. And yet the outcome was a blocked number and a vile proxy message. This is where the natural mind stumbles and where the Spirit-formed mind must hold its ground. The enemy will use outcomes to discourage obedience. He will point to the rejection as evidence that the approach was wrong, that the timing was wrong, that Samuel should have stayed silent. But the fruit of an act of obedience is not measured by the immediate response of the recipient. It is measured by faithfulness to the impulse of love under the direction of the Spirit. Samuel was pressed to make that call. He made it. He framed it correctly. The soil rejected the seed. That is not a failure of the sower — that is a revelation of the soil, and revelation is never wasted. I think about what Samuel said next, and I have been turning it over since: *This is showing me so many things, but very few of them have to do with Chloe herself.* That discernment right there is the mark of a man whose eyes are being trained. He is learning to read circumstances not as endpoints but as signposts. The rejection became a confirmation. The blocked number became a released distraction. The vile message became a boundary the Lord Himself drew, not by Samuel’s hand alone. And the peace that followed — the conviction, the focus, the clarity about what this season requires — that is not stoic detachment. That is the fruit of a man who is learning to let the Father order his steps and trust the ordering even when the immediate result stings. This draws me directly to Joshua 3, which I had been sitting in just before our call. As the priests carrying the Ark of the Covenant approached the Jordan River, the Lord’s instruction was precise: *step into the water*. Not: wait until the water parts and then step. Not: find a shallow crossing and wade across carefully. Step into the Jordan — and *then* the waters will be held back. Joshua 3:15-16 records it exactly this way: “and as those who bore the ark came to the Jordan, and the feet of the priests who bore the ark dipped in the edge of the water… the waters which came down from upstream stood still, and rose in a heap very far away.” The miracle was contingent on the step. The step preceded the parting. I am seeing Samuel in that river right now. He has been hearing correctly about this season — about the business, the kingdom economy, the calling to build — but for a long time he was acting without the conviction settling into certainty. The doubt, the second-guessing, the wondering whether he should be doing what he felt called to do: that is the riverbank. But he stepped. He began posting, taking calls, making sales. And now things are being cleared. Distractions are being removed. The Lord is not being subtle about it. The waters are holding. The echo of Exodus deepens this further. Samuel himself named it: *It’s like crossing the Red Sea. He’s cleared a path. There was no food, and now bread rained down from the sky.* What I find remarkable about the Exodus typology is precisely what Samuel surfaced: the Lord led Israel by the Red Sea route *because* a direct path into battle would have caused them to retreat to Egypt. Exodus 13:17 says it plainly — “God did not lead them by way of the land of the Philistines, although that was near; for God said, ‘Lest perhaps the people change their minds when they see war, and return to Egypt.’” He knew the soil of their hearts before they did. He knew that the very people He was delivering still had Egypt in them — still had the capacity to prefer familiar bondage over uncertain freedom — and so He built the journey accordingly. This is the thing that modern Western Christianity so often fails to hold in tension: deliverance is not the end of the story, and the wilderness is not evidence that the deliverance was incomplete. It is the training ground that follows it. The generation delivered out of Egypt never entered the promised land. Their children did. And yet — and I pressed this point on our call because I needed to say it plainly — God was still faithful through every year of the wilderness. His faithfulness does not require that we arrive quickly. It requires that He remain with us, and He does. The manna did not stop. The pillar did not leave. His presence did not depart. What departed was their generation’s ability to trust Him across a sustained season of uncertainty. The parallel for Samuel — and for me, and for all of us in this kind of covenantal building season — is this: the wilderness is not punishment. It is refinement. It is the place where the Egypt in us gets exposed, processed, and released. Every complaint about the manna, every longing for the leeks of Egypt, every moment of doubting whether the Lord actually has a plan: those are not signs of weak faith deserving condemnation. They are the honest cry of people still being formed. But the ones who reach the promised land are the ones who, like Joshua and Caleb, maintain a different spirit — who believe the report of the Lord over the report of the giants, who take the next step even when the ground in front of them is river water. What I am also carrying from this conversation is the weight of the intercession we owe to those who cannot yet receive. Samuel did exactly what love required. He spoke the word. She rejected it. Now the pastoral duty shifts from speaking to praying. Ezekiel 22:30 has been echoing in me: “So I sought for a man among them who would make a wall, and stand in the gap before Me on behalf of the land, that I should not destroy it.” The one who stands in the gap does not stand there because the situation looks promising. He stands there because the Lord calls for someone to stand. Chloe does not know how blind she is to the way unforgiveness has become her soil. She does not know that what she called strength is actually a fortress that locks out the very love that could restore her. But we know. And knowing creates a responsibility not to condemn her, but to intercede — to pray that the Father breaks up that fallow ground, that the thorns are uprooted, that the hardness yields to tenderness in His timing and by His hand alone. There is a phrase in Samuel’s testimony that I have not been able to shake: *“I told you I can’t just forgive everything.”* She said this as though it were a mark of self-possession. And in the world’s framework, perhaps it is. But in the Kingdom, that statement is a confession of captivity. The one who cannot forgive is not free — they are bound to the very wound they are protecting themselves from. Matthew 18:35 closes the Parable of the Unforgiving Servant with one of the most sobering lines in the Gospels: “So My heavenly Father also will do to you if each of you, from his heart, does not forgive his brother his trespasses.” Unforgiveness is not just a relational deficit. It is a spiritual trap that encloses the one holding it. The thorns choke the one in whom they are rooted just as surely as they choke the seed that tries to take hold in that soil. This is the tragedy of it — and it is why we pray. ## Implications and Challenges To all who are being discipled in this season: the framing of Samuel’s correction — affirm genuine strength, then name the shadow that is covering it — is not a technique. It is a posture. It flows from actually believing that the person you are speaking to is made in the image of God, that there is something real and good in them that is worth naming before you name what is wrong. The moment it becomes a formula, it becomes manipulation. It must remain an act of genuine love or it is nothing. There is a quietly dangerous theology loose in Western Christianity that equates immediate positive response with confirmation that obedience was right. Samuel did everything correctly and was blocked and insulted. By that standard, he failed. By the standard of the Kingdom — where the Son Himself was rejected by the very ones He came to save — faithfulness is not measured by outcome. It is measured by obedience to the impulse of love. We must unlearn the metrics of effectiveness we absorbed from an output-driven culture and return to the biblical measure: *did I do what love required?* The Four Soils parable is not only an evangelistic diagnostic — it is a pastoral one. Every conversation we have with people who are hurting, hardened, or confused involves stepping toward a soil type we cannot always see in advance. The danger is presuming that emotional openness in the moment means receptive soil. Samuel’s experience is a real-world demonstration that a quiet, apparently receiving response can sit above thorns deep enough to strangle a word that actually landed. We need the Spirit’s discernment about what kind of ground we are working with, and we need the humility to trust His assessment over our own emotional read of the room. The Exodus typology must be recovered in its fullness, particularly the wilderness dimension. Many people are in wilderness seasons right now believing something has gone wrong — that their deliverance was incomplete, that God has forgotten them, that they made a wrong turn somewhere. What Samuel identified is prophetically accurate for a wider audience than he may realize: the Lord sometimes leads us the long way around because He knows the short path would destroy us. The journey through the wilderness is not detour. It is curriculum. On the matter of staying until released: I named on our call the reality that I remain at my job until the Lord says otherwise, and that even fleshly eagerness to jump into Kingdom work ahead of His timing is still flesh. This is a word that cuts against the romantic narrative in charismatic and prophetic circles that says once you have clarity about calling, you should immediately abandon stability and leap. Sometimes the leap is premature. Sometimes the Lord has you in a particular place because the training is not finished, the preparation is not complete, or the provision is not yet established. Obedience includes *staying* when He says stay, not only moving when He says move. Finally — and I say this with full grace — the vile message that came through Armani is worth examining not as a wound to nurse but as information to hold lightly. The accuser always works through accusation. *“You haven’t changed or grown at all”* — that statement is a lie designed to destabilize. The evidence of Samuel’s transformation is plain to any Spirit-formed eye. But the enemy does not need the accusation to be true for it to do damage if we hold onto it. We do not contend with it by arguing. We lay it down, return to the Father’s verdict, and keep walking. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** The parable of the soils calls us to develop eyes that read the ground — not to judge or abandon people, but to understand what kind of intercession, patience, and sustained love the situation requires. Spiritual maturity in relational and pastoral contexts means we stop being surprised by rejection and start being equipped by it. It means we allow outcomes to refine our discernment rather than discourage our obedience. And it means we do the work of grieving, if grief is present, without translating grief into bitterness — because we know better than anyone what bitterness does to the soil. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The Jordan crossing makes the principle unavoidable: the parting happens *after* the step. Kingdom obedience in this season for those in the middle of a building call means putting feet in the water — posting, calling, showing up, selling, being present — and then trusting the Lord to hold back what needs to be held back. The temptation is to wait for confirming conditions before we act. But the Ark-bearing priests demonstrate that the confirming condition *is* the act. Step. Then watch the waters stand. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this conversation calls us to is one of watching the Lord’s sovereign orchestration with alert eyes and a thankful heart. He is removing what needs to go. He is closing what needs to be closed. He is holding back what would drown us. The prophetic people do not run from the wilderness — they read it. They watch for the manna. They track the pillar. They remember that the generation that complained about the provision still received it every morning, and they choose instead to receive it with gratitude rather than accusation. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one of Your servants, standing in the gap and agreeing with what You are already doing. I declare with thanksgiving that what happened in this conversation is not coincidence — it is curriculum. You are training a man for reconciliation ministry through the very soil diagnosis his own obedience provoked, and I am grateful to have witnessed it. Lord, I pray for Chloe by name — not that her resistance be overcome by our strategies, but that You do what only You can do: break up the fallow ground, uproot the thorns of unforgiveness, and open eyes that cannot yet see and ears that cannot yet hear. Let her captivity to bitterness give way to the liberty of forgiveness in Your timing, by Your hand alone. For Samuel, Lord, I call over him the same anointing that rested on Joshua: a spirit of courage and holy momentum to keep stepping into the river, to post and call and build and move in this season of focus without apology and without retreat. And for all who are reading this — those in their own wilderness, those waiting for waters to part, those who have just been blocked and insulted for doing the most loving thing — let this testimony be a word of strength: You are faithful in the wilderness. You were faithful to a generation that never saw the land, and You were faithful to their children who walked into it. You are the same yesterday, today, and forever, and Your faithfulness is not contingent on our comfort or our speed. To You be all glory in the building of Your kingdom, in the naming of Your name over broken soil, and in every step of faith taken in the dark before the waters stand.
