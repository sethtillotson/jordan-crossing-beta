# 08-30 Sermon Reflection: Wisdom Before Power, the Renewed Mind, and Healing as Love **Title:** 08-30 at 23:58 — When Wisdom Ushers Power: The Loved Heir, the Renewed Mind, and the Word That Gives Witness to Itself **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** E — Recorded Sermon, Video, or Online Reel + Reflection **Classification:** Teaching Reflection --- ## ⚡ Opening Observation I sat under the words of Apostle Joshua Selman across the whole arc of one long ministry — from a teaching on the trustworthiness of God, through the mind of Christ as the missing battery, into the ordering of wisdom before power, on to the three keys for receiving as the beloved, the honoring of unhurried seasons, and finally into a flood of healing testimonies where the Word gave witness to itself. What arrests me across every movement is a single refrain he kept returning to in different keys: *God can be trusted*, and the whole architecture of receiving rests not on the crisis but on the character of the One being trusted. There is a tension threaded through it all between the God who gives speed and the God who will not rush a soul, between power hungered for and wisdom despised, between using Jesus as a courier and following Jesus as Lord. That tension is where the Spirit has been pressing me. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the whole Christian life rests on whether God can actually be believed, not merely admired, and because the Body of Christ has largely divorced the two things God joined together — the possession of new life and the renovation of the mind. We pray for breakthroughs while married to belief systems that guarantee our defeat, we chase manifestation while despising the wisdom that would make power safe to hold, and we hurry to leave the very seasons that are forming us. Faith that latches onto crisis rather than character will collapse the moment the crisis prolongs itself, and power pursued apart from wisdom makes us dangerous to ourselves and to the Body. This teaching confronts a generation trained to trust systems, credentials, and celebrity influence more than the uncreated Creator, and it exposes how easily we let pain become a mountain that eclipses His omnipotence. For the discipleship of the saints, this is not optional; the Scripture says it is the people who *know* their God who are strong and do exploits. To learn that God loves us *back*, that transformation is the price of dominion, that wisdom ushers power, and that healing is an expression of love before it is a display of might — these are the disciplines that carry a believer through the years when results have not yet arrived. This is why none of it can be flattened into a pep talk. --- ## 🌍 The Worldly Mind's Interpretation The natural mind hears a ministry like this and files it under religious optimism, motivational success philosophy, and crowd psychology. It notices the emotional cadence, the call-and-response, the promises of a "season of laughter," and concludes that such gatherings function as psychological release valves for people burdened by prolonged suffering. It translates "be transformed" into mindset coaching and habit-stacking, hears "by me kings reign" as a formula for advancement, and reads the healing segment as suggestibility, adrenaline masking pain for a night, the placebo effect dressed in religious language, and the confirmation bias of a room primed to report only successes. It notes that a charismatic figure names a symptom — toothache, back pain, sore throat, dental issues — precisely the ailments most responsive to belief, and among thousands someone will always feel something shift. It grants that trusting a doctor is rational because it rests on measurable track records, but it regards trusting an unseen God as a category error. This reading is not born of malice; much of it flows from a genuine desire to protect the vulnerable from deception, which in Jesus' name is a real and grievous thing. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind is working with instruments calibrated only for the seen, like a man trying to weigh light on a kitchen scale — the tool is real, but it was never built for the substance in question. It can measure a doctor's fifty percent probability but has no gauge for the One whose word does not experiment because it simply becomes. It can catalog crowd psychology but cannot weigh a soul being built through hidden years, nor account for a gastritis of fifteen years lifting in a moment while the speaker was still clearing his throat. Self-improvement can rearrange the furniture of the soul but cannot change the architecture, because the deepest belief systems are not merely intellectual errors but spiritual alliances — a mindset "in partnership with Satan to recycle pain." The flesh reduces wisdom to technique when Scripture reveals wisdom to be a Person, and it reduces a miracle service to an atmosphere to be optimized when it is in truth a designed threshold where the living God meets each person at the precise point of their need. Where the flesh sees a coping mechanism, the Spirit sees covenant; where it sees manipulation, the Spirit sees a shepherd teaching frightened people that they are loved *back* so they can dare to receive. --- ## ✝️ What the Spirit Reveals Through It I keep returning to that unguarded moment near the end of the first teaching, when the Apostle stepped out of the pulpit cadence and into something quieter — telling us how on his travels, in his little time to himself, he had been thinking about the destructive effect of faulty belief systems. He described meeting people who were nice, good, sincere, and still dangerous, not because they were evil but because they carried a virus in their mental space. He reached for COVID to make it flesh: the sincere person who never intended to infect you, yet whose mere proximity forced isolation because the virus was already present. Something in the honesty of that admission — a man of faith confessing he had been turning this over in the loneliness of travel — makes the whole teaching feel less like performance and more like testimony. What arrests me is that he placed this warning not under the heading of sin but under the heading of *illumination*. The danger he named is not primarily moral failure but corrupted belief — a software running beneath sincerity, shredding lives while the host hates that it is happening yet cannot stop, because programming runs deeper than intention. This is terrifying and merciful at once: terrifying, because good people can be conduits of destruction; merciful, because the remedy is not condemnation but light. He was telling us that what wounds the Body is often not the villain we can name but the false belief we have never examined. The first Scripture that opens to me here is the very one he leaned on: "The light shineth in darkness, and the darkness comprehended it not" (John 1:5). He insisted that light, not time, is what drives out darkness — that thirty-eight years at Bethesda did not heal the man, but one word from the Light did. If a faulty belief system is a kind of interior darkness, then no amount of time spent sincerely inside it will dispel it; only the arrival of light does that. The Greek beneath that verse deepens the point: the word rendered "comprehended" is κατέλαβεν (katelaben, kah-TEL-ah-ben) — to seize, overtake, or master. John is saying the darkness could not overpower the light. Darkness has no capacity to master light; the moment light enters, the contest is already decided. A sincere person carrying a destructive belief system is not condemned to it — the light is stronger than the software. He also defined light biblically as "whatsoever doth make manifest" (Ephesians 5:13), and the Hebrew imagination behind Isaiah's "Arise, shine; for thy light is come" (Isaiah 60:1) uses אוֹר (or, ohr) — light as the first-spoken creative act of God, the "let there be" that ordered the formless deep. So when God brings light to a faulty belief system, He is doing again what He did over the void: speaking order into chaos. This is why the Apostle's insistence that "God does not experiment" struck me so forcefully. The name behind the whole message is the God who is אֶהְיֶה (ehyeh, eh-YEH) — "I AM," the self-existent, uncreated One who cannot be threatened, surprised into revision, or made to try-and-see. He upholds all things by the word of His power (Hebrews 1:3), and the word there for "upholds," φέρων (pherōn, FEH-rone), means to carry or bear along, a present-tense sustaining. The universe is not a machine God wound up and left; it is a word He is continuously speaking. And this is exactly why the Apostle could show us that the life of Christ is a beautiful timepiece while the mind of Christ is the battery that makes it move. A brand new television, gleaming and complete, can sit silent on a shelf because no battery powers it — and I have been the silent television more times than I want to confess, owning the life and rejecting the reengineering, calling that condition faith. The Greek beneath "be transformed" in Romans 12:2 is μεταμορφόω (metamorphoō, met-ah-mor-FOH-oh) — to be changed in essential form, the same word used of Christ's transfiguration; not a rearranging but a metamorphosis so total the outer is altered by an inner change of substance. Its mechanism is named in the same verse — the "renewing of your mind," ἀνακαίνωσις (anakainōsis, an-ah-KY-no-sis), a making-new-again that renews the νοῦς (nous, noos) at the root so it perceives what it could not perceive before. When Israel came out of Egypt the sea fled and Jordan was driven back (Psalm 114), but the slave-understanding came out with them in their minds, and it was that darkened understanding, not Pharaoh's army, that exiled a generation from Canaan. This is why Galatians 4:1 cuts so deep — "the heir, as long as he is a child, differeth nothing from a servant, though he be lord of all." God is not withholding the estate; He refuses to hand a functioning enterprise to a mind that would burn it down. The theological tension this exposes is one the modern church has quietly buried: we have made grace an enemy of growth, preaching the finished work so that any call to be reengineered sounds like a slander against the cross. But Paul, writing to those who already have Christ's life, commands them to be transfigured. The tension is not grace versus effort; it is grace that saves versus grace that also sanctifies, and to accept the first while resisting the second is to leave the watch in the box. And here a Kingdom mystery uncovers itself: unbelievers who lack the life of Christ have laid hold of principles that are expressions of His mind, and by those principles they build what believers cannot. His wisdom is written into the very structure of reality, so that strangers eat the crumbs of that mind and grow strong while the children starve at the table because they refuse the Father's mind. This is why the Apostle pressed so hard the ordering — wisdom before power, understanding before might. He confessed that he cried out to God because this was the eighth miracle service of the year and he did not want the people to grow numb to the ritual, frightened not of empty seats but of full ones full of people gone numb. The danger he named is not irreverence but familiarity — the slow calcification of hunger into habit. Wisdom, in Proverbs, is רֵאשִׁית (reshit, ray-SHEET) — the beginning, the firstfruit, the foundational thing, the very word that opens Genesis. She is personified as אָמוֹן (amon, ah-MOHN), a master craftsman rejoicing before the LORD at creation (Proverbs 8:30), which is why "through wisdom a house is built" (Proverbs 24:3). And the New Testament unveils who this Wisdom finally is: "Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24). When Luke 2:52 says Jesus "increased in wisdom," using σοφία (sophia, so-FEE-ah), it means the incarnate Wisdom of God genuinely grew in wisdom before He was filled with power. The flesh wants Acts 10; the Spirit says first, Luke 2. And the astonishment of Ephesians 3:10 is that the powers of darkness are schooled not by the church's might but by the πολυποίκιλος (polupoikilos, poo-loo-POY-kee-los) — the many-colored, richly variegated — wisdom of God displayed through a redeemed people. Wisdom does not compete with power; wisdom prepares the room where power can be received without destroying the recipient. Then the ministry turned to the keys for receiving, and I cannot get past the order: love before power, power before faith. "I know you love Him, but are you aware He loves you back?" He named the quiet assumption I have carried — a parasitic love, "friends for food" — and turned it inside out: *He loves you more than you will ever love Him.* He did not begin with power, though power is real; he began with love, because a man who does not know he is loved cannot receive without suspicion. Psalm 36:7 makes the movement explicit: "How excellent is thy lovingkindness, O God! *Therefore* the children of men put their trust under the shadow of thy wings." Trust is the fruit of perceived lovingkindness. The Hebrew standing behind that word is חֶסֶד (chesed, KHEH-sed) — covenant loyalty, the love that binds itself by oath and will not let go, the love of Jeremiah 31:3 spoken to a broken, exiled people: "Yea, I have loved thee with an everlasting love." Paul takes this into his Ephesian prayer that we would γινώσκω (ginōskō, ghin-OCE-ko) — know intimately, experientially — the love of Christ that passes knowledge, and then bursts into ὑπερεκπερισσοῦ (hyperekperissou, hoop-er-ek-per-is-SOO), a triple-stacked superlative for the God "able to do exceeding abundantly above all that we ask or think" (Ephesians 3:20). Power without love terrifies; love without power disappoints; the two together are the gospel. The Kingdom mystery of receiving uncovers itself most vividly in blind Bartimaeus casting away his garment — his livelihood, his coin-cloth, his covering, possibly his very license to beg — before he was healed (Mark 10:50). To cast it away was to burn the ship, to make return to blindness unthinkable. This is the difference between hope and faith: hope keeps the garment folded nearby in case God does not come through; faith throws it into the crowd it can no longer find. And when Jesus said "Go thy way; thy faith hath made thee whole," the healed man did *not* go his way — "immediately he received his sight, and followed Jesus in the way." There are many who receive from Jesus and then go their own way, "the way that seemeth right unto a man, but the end thereof are the ways of death" (Proverbs 14:12). Bartimaeus was given permission to depart with his miracle and chose the Giver over the gift, releasing faith through words and actions of obedience — "with the heart man believeth unto righteousness; and with the mouth confession is made unto salvation" (Romans 10:10). And I keep hearing that other quiet interruption threaded through the healing service: "Don't rush seasons. You will miss the season you are hurrying to get out of." Between the ache of believers seated outside in the cold and the fervor of the meeting, a doctrine of process surfaced. The word עֵת (et, ayt) that saturates Ecclesiastes 3 is not merely chronological time but *appointed* time, weighted with divine purpose, and the New Testament knows both χρόνος (chronos, KROH-noss), sequential duration, and καιρός (kairos, kai-ROSS), the ripened, decisive moment that only arrives when the χρόνος has done its formative work. "God gives speed, but He does not rush people." Joseph's word "tested him" until its time came (Psalm 105:19); the waiting was the curriculum. Yet the same man who preaches patient process turns to heal with startling urgency, declaring that the Spirit who raised Christ will ζῳοποιήσει (zōopoiēsei, zoh-oh-poy-AY-say) — quicken, make alive — our mortal bodies (Romans 8:11). The God who takes years to build a Joseph raises Lazarus in one night, and both motions belong to the One who is כָּל־בָּשָׂר (kol-basar, kol-bah-SAR), the God of all flesh, for whom nothing is too hard (Jeremiah 32:27). Now the personal encounter arrives, and I cannot listen to any of this and remain a spectator. I have to ask where I have let time become my false savior, sincerely waiting inside a broken belief thinking endurance alone would dispel it. I confess I have doubted the *leaving* of things far more than I ever questioned their *coming* — granting decay an unquestioned right to arrive while treating restoration as suspicious, as though I never interrogate how the mass appeared and only doubt when it disappears. I confess I have owned the life and forbidden God to open the hood, wanting Him to bless the car without touching the engine. I confess I have treated the Lord as a courier and not a King, and that my instinct watching the healing service was to protect myself with suspicion, cataloguing flaws so I would not have to risk being moved. But suspicion is not discernment; suspicion loves its own safety enough to reject the good along with the questionable. The woman who had already scheduled her tooth extraction convicts me, because I too have a list of things I have decided to merely *manage* rather than bring to Christ. I repent of the low ceiling I have nailed over my own prayers. The prayer that rises in me is not merely "heal me" but "illuminate me" — and not merely "illuminate me" but "transfigure me, and let me follow You in the way." For the beloved brethren this is a corporate summons, not a private hobby. The Apostle grieved over "bright minds, beautiful people, sincere hearts, but poisonous belief systems," and this is the diagnosis of whole churches destroyed not by demons alone but by wrong belief held sincerely. If sincere brethren can carry destructive software, then love requires more than affirmation of one another's sincerity; it requires the courageous, gracious bringing of light — beginning with our own hearts, since we carry our own. We must stop discipling people into a consumer relationship with heaven, teaching them to extract and depart, and teach them instead that the healing is the beginning of following. We must become a people who can hold power and character together, who honor the overflow saints pressed to the edges as those being made, who neither swallow every ministry uncritically nor spit it out contemptuously, but test all things and hold fast what is good. And gratitude becomes the atmosphere of it — the intentional thank-you the Apostle called the seedbed of progress, the attentive eye watching for God's finger on every ordinary mercy, until the whole assembly falls on its face before the Lamb who alone was found worthy, to whom every genuine work of power must finally return or curdle into merchandise. --- ## 🤔 Implications and Challenges **On trusting systems over the Creator, and waiting out darkness instead of receiving light.** The Apostle's observation cuts against a deeply modern reflex: we entrust our bodies to doctors offering thirty-to-fifty-percent odds, yet stiffen before the One who upholds all things by His word. This is not an attack on medicine — he explicitly affirmed that trusting your doctor is all right — but a challenge to the inconsistency of our confidence, which reveals that our belief system, not our God, has set the ceiling on our expectation. Many of us have adopted a quiet theology of endurance-as-cure, believing that if we simply remain sincere long enough our brokenness will resolve, but the man at Bethesda dismantles this: thirty-eight years did not heal him; a word did. Time reveals what was planted, but only light drives out darkness. The invitation is to stop aging inside our conditions and to actively seek the manifesting light of God. **On the gospel that saves the soul but abandons the mind.** Much of modern Western Christianity celebrates conversion as the finish line rather than the starting gun, rejoicing over the new television and never asking why so many of the saved live silent, unpowered lives. Ephesians 4:18 warns of those "alienated from the life of God through the ignorance that is in them" — a condition, not only of the lost. Our recurring, unanswered prayers may be a diagnosis rather than an injustice, for the greatest way God answers prayer is often by causing the one praying to grow until certain requests dissolve. When I keep asking for the same breakthrough, the Spirit may be inviting me to inspect the vessel rather than accuse the Giver, exchanging my basket for a barn. And sincerity has become our excuse for stagnation: "bright minds, beautiful people, sincere hearts, but poisonous belief systems" refuses to let earnestness detoxify a poisoned mind, and love owes people the truth. **On the idolatry of manifestation over maturation, and the critical spirit that masquerades as maturity.** Much of the church is organized around the visible and immediate — the powerful service, the viral clip, the felt anointing — while treating the slow increase of wisdom as optional or even boring, so that even faithful ministry produces people addicted to the ritual of power. If we will not let Luke 2:52 govern our appetite for Acts 10:38, we will keep producing gifted people who are unformed and therefore unsafe. Yet the opposite ditch is just as deadly: many sober-minded brethren, especially in the wounded post-charismatic West, have made suspicion their reflexive posture and called it discernment. I felt this ditch in my own heart, and I name it as sin, not sophistication; the God who worked through Balaam's mouth is not constrained by my approval of the vessel. Genuine maturity risks being moved; only fear hides behind perpetual critique. Test all things, hold fast the good, and give the glory to Jesus alone rather than to any platform or personality. **On the courier gospel and the hunger to be seen.** There is a socially acceptable Christianity that treats Jesus precisely as DHL — a delivery service summoned in crisis and dismissed in comfort, the "going your way" that "seemeth right" but ends in death (Proverbs 14:12). It is marked not by scandalous sin but by polite indifference, receiving the gift and ignoring the Giver. Woven alongside it is the celebrity-shaped hunger to be the face behind everything, the extended prayers for "my season to be seen" and to be "recognized and rewarded," which "He must increase, but I must decrease" (John 3:30) directly confronts. There is a legitimate cry for fruitfulness and open doors, but the Gospel's deepest trajectory is downward — the seed that falls into the ground and dies. The correction is not the abolition of influence but its crucifixion, so that the true Light is made manifest through us rather than eclipsed by us. **On breaking generational patterns rightly and sealing what is restored.** The declaration over repetitive family curses — "beginning from you, it comes to an end now" — carries real biblical weight, for "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (2 Corinthians 5:17), and the woman resigned to removing her tooth as "a family thing" was met by the Lord in the middle of a declaration she was barely attending to. Yet this can curdle into a fatalistic obsession with bloodline curses that makes believers more afraid of their ancestors than confident in their Redeemer; the cross, not a platform formula, severs the old inheritance. The relentless refrain "it never returns to you again" touches a real concern, for Jesus warned of the spirit returning to the swept and empty house (Matthew 12:44). A body restored is a temple entrusted, and the empty house must be filled with the presence of Christ or the last state becomes worse than the first. We must steward what is restored rather than walking away with a testimony and no transformation. **On the ones who left unhealed, and the asymmetry of our faith.** Grace compels me to name what the platform naturally omits: not everyone in that room was healed, and cheap triumphalism wounds the tender by implying the still-suffering simply lacked faith. Scripture holds the mystery gently — Paul's thorn remained, Timothy's infirmities were not banished — without ever denying that God heals. This meets the deeper asymmetry the Apostle exposed, that we accept the arrival of sickness without interrogation but doubt its departure, granting decay an unquestioned right while treating healing as suspect. If God can raise an embalmed Lazarus whole, the correction of the living is the lesser wonder. The challenge to any church recovering healing is to build a community strong enough to carry both the celebrated miracle and the ongoing groan, refusing to shame the ones whose *today* has not yet come, while gratitude that is not ritual but power becomes the soil of all our progress. --- ## 🚀 What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity means moving our trust from crisis to character — from "God, do this for me" to "God, I know who You are" — letting a functional revelation of His uncreated, unthreatened, unable-to-lie nature become the floor beneath our faith so that prolonged pain no longer sets the ceiling on what we believe. It means holding two true things at once: that God still heals with explosive δύναμις (dunamis, DOO-nah-mis), and that the mere presence of power is never the final proof of a man's standing with God. It means welcoming the news that there is more to unlearn, holding every long-cherished belief loosely enough to place it on the table and ask whether it produces victory, advances destiny, and glorifies God — the fluidity of mind that lets the Holy Spirit perform His ongoing μεταμορφόω (met-ah-mor-FOH-oh) and ἀνακαίνωσις (an-ah-KY-no-sis). And it means learning to distinguish the God who gives speed from the god of hurry we have made in our own image, honoring the hidden work of the forge, becoming formidable through process or not at all, and receiving as those who are loved *back*, and first, and more, on the ground of His unchanging חֶסֶד (KHEH-sed). **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience calls us to welcome illumination rather than merely endure our darkness, and to extend that light to one another in love, letting it expose our own software before we bring it to another's. It means treating the renewal of the mind as warfare and the price of dominion, refusing to keep the life of Christ without the mind of Christ, and refusing the shortcut by embracing the sequence God Himself walked — wisdom before power, formation before manifestation. It takes the concrete shape of Bartimaeus casting his garment: identifying and releasing whatever backup plan, hedged unbelief, or comfortable exit we have kept folded nearby, and then, having received, refusing the permission to "go our way" and following Jesus in the way instead. It means receiving healing not as the specialized property of the anointed few but as the ordinary inheritance of the assembled Body — calling for the elders, laying on hands, praying the prayer of faith (James 5:14–15) — and stewarding whatever is restored by filling the swept house with worship and the Word. And it means the decrease of self, a deliberate refusal to make ministry a mirror, so that Christ alone increases and is seen. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture trained here keeps saying "God can be trusted" from revelation, not from results, watching for the arrival of light rather than the passage of time, and discerning between word-derived miracle and manipulative magic. It is a watchfulness against numbness — a determination to stay awake and yielded before the living God even when the shape of our worship is familiar — and a watchfulness over our own minds as the first battleground, refusing to host any mindset in partnership with the enemy to recycle pain. It learns to read the seasons the way a farmer watches the sky, discerning the עֵת (ayt), waiting without despair and moving without presumption, blessing the ones on the margins as those being made. It holds expectant faith in the God who still quickens mortal bodies together with sober discernment against every counterfeit, testing all things by the plumb line of Christ crucified and risen, tender toward the ones whose miracle tarries, never wielding testimony as a weapon of shame. And it forms a people whose very lives become manuals of His faithfulness, so that others learn who God is by studying how He held us, and every genuine wonder collapses us before the Lamb who sits on the throne, the only measure and the only glory. --- ## 🔥 A Closing Prayer Father, uncreated and unthreatened, God of all flesh before whom nothing is too hard, I come as Your servant and co-heir under the New Covenant, standing between the heavens and the earth to make agreement with Your will. I thank You that You are not a man that You should lie, that You searched all creation for one greater to swear by and found none, and so swore by Yourself — and that You loved me first, with an everlasting חֶסֶד (KHEH-sed) that bound itself to me before I could offer You anything. Forgive me for trusting the measurable while doubting the Maker, for granting sickness an unquestioned right to arrive while treating Your healing as suspect, for despising the overflow seasons and praying to escape the very forge where You are making me formidable. Forgive the reverent-sounding unbelief and the suspicion I have dressed as maturity; tear down the low ceiling I have built and open my house to heaven again. Arise over me, O Light, and drive out what the years could never dispel; work Your μεταμορφόω (met-ah-mor-FOH-oh) in me and Your ἀνακαίνωσις (an-ah-KY-no-sis) upon my mind, that the timepiece of my life would finally move by the battery of Your mind within me. I renounce every agreement with the enemy to recycle my losses, I cast away the garment of my hedged unbelief, and when You grant me sight and say "go thy way," I will refuse the permission and follow You in the way, for to whom else shall I go. Let Your manifold wisdom go before Your power in all Your house, that the very principalities would be schooled by the sight of a reconciled and holy people; and let every wonder You permit be a road to Your feet and not a destination in itself. Teach us gratitude that is not ritual but power, carry the ones whose healing tarries with the same tenderness as those made whole, and let our lives become manuals of Your faithfulness. To Him who sits on the throne, and unto the Lamb — not to any platform, any voice, any name but His — be all blessing and honor and power and glory forever. In the name of Jesus Christ, come in the flesh, my Lord and my life, amen. --- claude-opus-4.8 --- ## 📝 Scripture References & Original Language Notes | Passage (NKJV) | Original Language | Theological Insight | | --- | --- | --- | | John 1:5 | κατέλαβεν (katelaben, kah-TEL-ah-ben) — to seize/overtake/master | Darkness cannot overpower light;
