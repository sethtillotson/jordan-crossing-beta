# 05-06 Call Reflection: The Cross, Identity in Christ, and Samuel’s Vocational Discernment **Title:** 05-06 at 16:38 — The Cross Beneath the Weight of All Things **Recorded:** 2026-05-06 16:38:14 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation (Live Capture / Call Reflection) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother Samuel speak, something kept rising beneath the surface of what seemed like a casual catch-up call — the question of identity, not merely psychological or vocational, but covenantal. What began as news about an army application became, by the end of the conversation, something far weightier: a meditation on what it cost the Son of God to carry every weight of human sin so that we might carry none of it unredeemed. I am sitting with that now, and I am not ready to let it go quickly. **Why This Matters:** The Church in every generation faces the twin temptations of cheapening the cross and over-spiritualizing it — reducing the Passion either to a moral example or to an abstraction stripped of its terrible, glorious physicality. What I heard in this conversation, and what I have been pressing into through the teaching I referenced, is that the cross refuses both reductions. Jesus of Nazareth endured a specific, measurable, documented agony for six hours, and every breath he drew cost him the full weight of his body against iron driven through bone and sinew — and all of that physical endurance was the outward echo of an inward, spiritual transaction of unimaginable enormity. For disciples who are right now carrying grief, dissatisfaction, uncertainty about direction, weariness from long shifts, lingering illness, or the disorientation of returning home to find it no longer fits — which describes both Samuel and myself in this moment — the cross is not a doctrine to be agreed with. It is a weight to be reckoned with. And reckoning with it rightly changes everything. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, if it engages this conversation at all, will likely fixate on its most practically legible features: a young man taking initiative, assessing his situation with clear eyes, listing his deficits, identifying a solution, and acting. Samuel’s decision to apply to the British Army will read, to most, as a sensible pivot — a way to gain structure, income, community, and purpose in one move. Similarly, the conversation about the cross will register to a secular observer as an interesting intellectual exercise, perhaps even a compassionate one, but ultimately as mythology dressed in physiology. The secular frame is not without its own kind of insight; it can acknowledge the documented historical suffering of crucifixion, even grant that it was extreme. It may allow that identity shapes behavior, as the psychological literature well supports. It may recognize the importance of institutional accountability when a group records confessions without consent. But within that frame, everything remains horizontal — cause and effect, input and output, stimulus and response — and the deepest questions this conversation raises cannot be answered from within it. **The Limitation:** What the natural frame cannot perceive is that the events being discussed are not merely historical, psychological, or civic. There is a dimension running through this conversation that reason alone does not have eyes to see: that a man applying to the British Army, that a brother deciding to report spiritual abuse, that a recovering body lying still under illness, that two friends laughing about airplane maintenance and then suddenly speaking the name of Christ — all of it is held inside a story that was purchased at the cross. The worldly mind can map the surface. It cannot hear the frequency beneath it — the frequency at which identity, vocation, covenant, and the price paid to redeem them all resonate together into a single, still-reverberating note that the Lamb struck at Calvary and has not stopped sounding since. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I want to begin where this conversation arrived, even before it ended — at the cross. Because everything else that was spoken, however practical or circumstantial, was quietly orbiting this center of gravity the entire time, even when neither of us named it directly. The teaching I referenced from Apostle Michael — a man drawing from secular medical literature to document what actually happened to Jesus’ body over those six hours — confirmed something I had already been shown in prayer. What the medical reports describe is this: a man crucified in the Roman manner died, in large part, of asphyxiation. To exhale, the body relaxes. To inhale, the crucified man had to push upward against the nail through his feet, dragging torn flesh against rough wood, in order to expand his chest cavity enough to take a breath. Every single breath — for six hours — required this act of deliberate, agonizing exertion. Jesus did not simply hang and die. He chose, again and again and again, for approximately six hours, to press upward through agony and breathe. The question the Spirit pressed into me as I sat under that teaching was: *why?* Not why did he suffer — that much the text establishes clearly — but why that particular endurance? Why that sustained, active, voluntary six-hour prolonging of the agony? The answer I keep arriving at is this: the debt was not yet fully paid. The transaction was not yet complete. And Jesus, fully God and fully man, bearing within his body and spirit the weight of every sin and every sickness and every transgression of every human life that would ever be lived, could not simply stop. The theological weight of what Paul declares in 2 Corinthians 5:21 — “For He made Him who knew no sin to be sin for us, that we might become the righteousness of God in Him” — demands we reckon with what it actually means for the sinless One to *be made sin*. This was not a legal fiction. This was a real and terrible exchange. And it required completion. When Jesus finally breathed his last, the final thing he uttered before releasing his spirit was not a cry of defeat. It was a declaration of legal and cosmic finality: “It is finished” (John 19:30). The Greek word here is τετέλεσται (tetelestai, teh-TEH-les-tie) — a perfect passive indicative, meaning not merely “it is done” in the sense of cessation, but “it has been brought to its full and complete accomplishment, and that completion now stands permanently.” This was the word written across a paid debt in the ancient world. Stamped across an account fully settled. Jesus did not die because he ran out of strength. He died when the work was finished — and then, and only then, he gave up his spirit voluntarily. “Father, into Your hands I commit My spirit” (Luke 23:46). The order matters enormously: first, the completion. Then, the surrender. As my brother Samuel said — and I felt it land with real theological weight — it was not merely bodily pain that Jesus endured. If that were all, then crucifixion was uniquely terrible, yes, but it was not unique in kind among human suffering. What made Calvary categorically unlike any other event in human history is what Jesus was spiritually absorbing at the same time his body was doing its brutal, measurable work. He was receiving into himself every grief, every violence, every shame, every addiction, every betrayal, every disease, every perversion, every cruelty — not as a witness to them, not as a sympathetic observer, but as their *bearer*. Isaiah 53:4–5 says, “Surely He has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows… He was wounded for our transgressions, He was bruised for our iniquities.” The Hebrew word for “borne” here is נָשָׂא (nasa, nah-SAH) — to lift, to carry, to take upon oneself the full weight of another’s burden so that the one who originally bore it is released from it. This was the spiritual reality happening inside the physical event. The נָשָׂא (nah-SAH) of the cross was absolute and total. This is why, at a certain point during those six hours, something happened that had never happened before in all of eternity: the Son of God, in the fullness of the Trinitarian relationship that had existed before time, was forsaken. “My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?” (Matthew 27:46) — and this cry is a quotation of Psalm 22:1, which means Jesus, even in his dereliction, was praying Scripture. But the dereliction was real. To absorb sin — to become, as Paul says, the very thing he had never been — required a genuine separation from the Father. Because God cannot look upon sin (Habakkuk 1:13). Not as a stylistic preference, but as an ontological impossibility. And so the Son, having taken sin fully into himself, experienced the full consequence of sin’s deepest effect: separation from the Father. He died spiritually before he died physically. He was forsaken so that we would never have to be. As I sit with this, I am also sitting with something Samuel said that arrived almost casually in the middle of his update about the army application — and it is one of the most theologically precise things I have heard in a while. He said: “The only identity you should be living in is that of how Jesus Christ sees you.” And I responded with the phrase that has been living in me: Χριστὸς ἐν ἐμοί (Christos en emoi, khris-TOSS en eh-MOY) — Christ in you. This is Paul’s language in Colossians 1:27, where he calls this mystery “the hope of glory.” But glory, as the cross makes clear, is not arrived at cheaply. The one who is now *in* us went through Calvary so that his life could be legitimately placed within us. The identity we are called to inhabit — the identity of sons and daughters who are seen by the Father through the righteousness of the Son — was purchased by that six-hour active, deliberate, voluntary agony. We do not access this identity through positive thinking or religious performance. We access it through genuine union with the Crucified and Risen One. Samuel’s insight about being “a victim to his environment” struck me as a clear-eyed reading of a spiritual reality that Scripture names precisely. When he lived in a context that demanded the identity of a husband, he functioned as one. When he is submerged in an environment of passivity, he defaults toward passivity. This is not weakness unique to Samuel — it is the nature of the unredeemed soul’s relationship to identity: permeable, environmentally conditioned, unstable. But the invitation of the gospel is toward something that cannot be environmentally conditioned, because it does not originate in the environment. Christ in us does not change based on the neighborhood, the household, the season, or the mood. Χριστὸς ἐν ἐμοί (khris-TOSS en eh-MOY) is not a feeling. It is a fact anchored in what was τετέλεσται (teh-TEH-les-tie) — fully, permanently, completely accomplished — at the cross. I also want to name what happened with the group that Samuel rightly distanced himself from and then reported — because this, too, is a cross-shaped moment in miniature. The preaching, by Samuel’s own account, was everything it should have been: power, standing firm, the things of God. But it was soaked in deception. Confessions recorded without consent. Money sought by suspicious transfer. Truth weaponized into exploitation. This is not merely a legal or civic problem, though it is those things too, and Samuel was right to bring it to the appropriate authorities. It is a theological category: the corruption of the genuine by means of the counterfeit. The enemy has always worked this way — not by inventing new things, but by counterfeiting the things God has made holy. Recorded confessions that were meant to be sacred. Financial appeals that should have been transparent. The fury I felt — and that Samuel named — is the right response. It is what Scripture calls zeal for the house of God (Psalm 69:9, John 2:17). Holy anger in the presence of something that wears the name of Christ while serving a different master. And then — and this is where I see the hand of God most clearly in this conversation — when Samuel called the authorities to make his report, the women who received his calls were both believers. They were not formal contacts. They were not arranged by Samuel. They were placed there by the God who orchestrates all things and who, as Samuel himself said, knew exactly who he was putting in that situation. This is what Romans 8:28 means when it says that “all things work together for good to those who love God.” Not that all things are good. But that God, in his sovereignty, works *through* all things — including phone calls to government offices — for the sake of those who are his. Nothing is outside his jurisdiction. I want to return, finally, to the place where this conversation first took its deepest breath: the moment Samuel said, “I know if I pick up my cross and continue this journey with Christ, everything should be okay.” He said it quietly, almost as an aside. But I am not letting it pass quietly in this reflection. To pick up the cross is not a metaphor for difficulty. It is the specific language Jesus used in Luke 9:23: “If anyone desires to come after Me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily, and follow Me.” The cross was an instrument of execution. To take it up is to consent to the death of self-directed living. But here is what makes it bearable, and more than bearable — glorious: the one who calls us to carry the cross already carried the weight of all the sin that the cross was meant to punish. He did not ask us to do what he was unwilling to do. He did it first, completely, to τετέλεσται (teh-TEH-les-tie). And now the cross we carry is not one of condemnation but of consecration — dying to the old identity so that Χριστὸς ἐν ἐμοί (khris-TOSS en eh-MOY) has room to be lived out in us fully, visibly, credibly, and with power. ## Implications and Challenges The conversation Samuel and I shared challenges several assumptions that have quietly settled into the modern Western church’s relationship with both the cross and the question of identity. First, there is the temptation to sentimentalize the cross without reckoning with it. Many Christians speak of Jesus’ sacrifice with genuine emotion, and that emotion is not wrong. But sentiment that bypasses the actual weight of what happened at Calvary — the full absorption of every sin, disease, shame, and spiritual death that the human race has accumulated — will eventually thin out under pressure. A cross that is merely touching cannot sustain the life of discipleship when the real cost of following Christ becomes apparent. Samuel’s willingness to sit in this theology — to ask what Jesus actually felt when he bore the weight of every murdered son and grieving mother all at once — is the kind of reckoning that produces durable faith rather than fragile emotionalism. Second, the modern church often treats identity as a project of self-discovery or self-improvement rather than the outcome of a covenantal exchange. Samuel named this clearly when he observed that his behavior follows his perceived identity — that when he believed himself to be a husband, he functioned as one. But he also named the correction: the only stable identity is the one grounded in how Christ sees us, purchased by the cross, sealed by the Spirit, and expressed through genuine daily union with Christ. This is not a therapeutic category. It is a theological one, and the church does its members no favors by defaulting to self-help language when Scripture provides something far more robust. Third, what happened with the deceptive group Samuel encountered is a warning that theological content, on its own, does not authenticate a ministry. Samuel noted that the preaching sounded right — power, standing firm, the things of God — but the structure in which it operated was exploitative and deceptive. The fruit test of Matthew 7:15–20 is not a test of doctrinal correctness alone. It is a test of what the ministry produces in those who receive it: freedom or bondage, transparency or secrecy, generosity or extraction. The willingness to report what he found, even when it was difficult and even when the preaching itself had moments of genuine truth, demonstrates the kind of holy discernment that the church desperately needs. Fourth, the sovereignty of God over seemingly incidental details — including which official answers a phone call — is not a minor point to file away under “nice things that happen sometimes.” It is the active governance of a Father who is intimately involved in the specific circumstances of his children’s lives. To receive moments like that as coincidence is to reduce the God of the Bible to a deist abstraction. To receive them as providence is to live in the kind of trust that Samuel articulated at the end of the call: “All we know is we can trust Christ.” Fifth, and perhaps most personally pressing: the physical recovery and the stillness of illness that I referenced during this call are not merely inconvenient interruptions to ministry. Scripture repeatedly shows that seasons of bodily stillness and limitation are often seasons of spiritual deepening — the wilderness before the public ministry, the prison before the dream interpretation, the sickness that becomes the occasion for the manifestation of God’s glory. The stillness is not empty. It is a kind of listening room. Sixth, let us be clear that picking up the cross daily, as Jesus commanded, is not a call to manufacture suffering. It is a call to live inside the finished work with enough sobriety to recognize that the self-life — the environment-conditioned, externally-determined, performance-based sense of self — must genuinely die, repeatedly, so that the life of Christ can be what actually animates us. This is not morbid. It is the most alive thing there is. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Reckoning with the cross as a real, specific, documented, spiritually weighted event — not a symbol but a transaction — shifts the entire posture of faith. When I know that every sin I have ever committed was borne by a man who had to push upward against iron nails just to breathe while he was carrying it, gratitude is no longer occasional. It becomes structural. And when I know that the identity he purchased for me through that agony is permanent, stable, and independent of my environment, I stop building my sense of self from circumstances and start receiving it as a gift already given. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Samuel’s next season — whether the army receives his application or not — is an opportunity to live as a man whose identity is settled before the outcome is known. The obedience being called forth here is not the obedience of the man who knows how things will turn out. It is the obedience of the man who picks up his cross anyway, trusting the one who went ahead of him and said: *tetelestai* — it is finished, completely and permanently, and I have gone to prepare a place for you. Kingdom obedience means stepping into the next thing — the application, the report to the authorities, the recovery from illness, the daily dying to the lazy environment — while resting in an identity that does not depend on any of those things going well. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this conversation cultivates is the posture of one who knows that the future cannot be predicted but can be trusted. Samuel said it clearly: “We can never predict where we are going.” That is not pessimism. That is the freedom of a man whose hope is not in a particular outcome but in the God who governs all outcomes. The prophetic saint is not one who has mapped the future. The prophetic saint is one who has planted his flag in the finished work of Christ and refuses to be moved by the uncertainty of what comes next — holding instead to the word that has already been spoken: *it is finished*. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one of Your servants — a co-heir of the Kingdom, standing on this side of the cross where the debt has been fully and permanently settled, where τετέλεσται (teh-TEH-les-tie) is not a historical memory but a living, present reality that governs everything I am and everything I carry. I thank You for this conversation, for my brother Samuel’s honesty and courage and theological hunger, for the women You placed on those phone calls, for the teaching that confirmed what You had already shown in prayer, and for the still, recovering body that has had to stop and listen more than it has spoken this week. I ask that You establish Samuel’s identity in Christ — not in the British Army, not in his marksmanship scores, not in any role or uniform, but in the immovable reality of Χριστὸς ἐν ἐμοί (khris-TOSS en eh-MOY), which was sealed at Calvary and ratified by resurrection. Let every false structure that recorded confessions for exploitation be exposed and dismantled by the authorities You have already placed, and let no one else be drawn into its net. For those in this conversation who are sick, tired, dissatisfied, or uncertain about direction — let the cross be the answer that outlasts every question. We pick up our crosses. We follow You. We trust that what You finished, You finished completely. And we agree with Your will between the heavens and the earth: let Your Kingdom come, let Your will be done, in Samuel’s application, in my recovery, in every soldier’s barracks and every chaplain’s tent and every government office and every phone call and every breath — until the name that is above every name is the only name that matters.
