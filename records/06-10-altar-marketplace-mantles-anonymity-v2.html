# 06-10 Call Reflection: Faith, Marketplace, and the Nicodemus Transition **Title:** 06-10 at 17:24 — The Altar and the Marketplace: On Mantles, Anonymity, and the Nicodemus Transition **Recorded:** 2026-06-10 17:24:21 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture / Call Reflection) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother Samuel unfold a business vision with remarkable clarity and energy, something in me recognized the hand of God moving through what could easily be mistaken for an entrepreneurial brainstorm session between two friends. The conversation moved through marketplace strategy, prophetic posture, instant obedience with five dollars, physical exhaustion, and the weight of public witness — all in a single call. What arrests me most, sitting with this now, is not the business model itself, but the deeper question that kept surfacing beneath every layer: *Who is building this, and for whom?* That question is not a skeptic’s challenge; it is the very question the Spirit was pressing throughout the entire exchange, and it is one I want to sit with carefully. **Why This Matters:** The saints of God are not insulated from commerce, economics, ambition, or the very real need to generate income and build sustainable structures. The Kingdom of God is not opposed to the marketplace — it was never meant to be. But the terrifying and beautiful truth of covenant discipleship is that the Lord does not merely bless what we consecrate after the fact. He builds what He has already commissioned, and He calls His servants to discern the difference between a gift of an idea and a directive from the throne. If the Body of Christ is going to walk in genuine Kingdom economics rather than spiritually branded entrepreneurialism, we must learn — together — how to steward ambition under submission, how to carry the stripes of our formation publicly when the Father requires it, and how to remain at the altar even while the building is happening in the streets. This matters for every saint standing at the intersection of calling and commerce right now. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, what I witnessed in this conversation was two men doing what driven, resourceful people do: problem-solving, strategizing, and encouraging one another toward financial momentum. The business model Samuel described — a commission-based, ambassador-driven membership community — is recognized by the market mind as a legitimate affiliate structure, not fundamentally different from what countless successful platforms have built. The natural mind would celebrate the clarity of vision, the timing of available capital, and the infectious enthusiasm of a young builder who has clearly developed through real-world failure and iteration. It would affirm the psychological value of community and belonging as a monetizable product, and it would nod approvingly at the social proof element of influencer-driven distribution. Even the frankness about its resemblance to multi-level marketing would be viewed not as a red flag but as simple market realism. From this vantage point, the theological portions of the conversation would be interpreted as personal motivation — the kind of spiritual language that helps certain personality types maintain confidence and emotional resilience under pressure. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind, for all its competence at mapping terrain and calculating margins, cannot perceive the covenantal current running beneath this entire conversation. It cannot hear the difference between a man who says *I’ll find a way* from personal determination and a man who says *it will be done* because he is positioned under an open heaven. It cannot weigh the significance of six dollars and nineteen cents arriving at the precise moment it was needed, timed to the movement of a wife’s provision and a Spirit-pressed prompting in the chest, as anything more than fortunate coincidence. The capacity to discern whether an idea is a gift *to* a person or a directive *from* God requires an inner orientation that no market analysis, no psychological framework, and no motivational philosophy can cultivate. That discernment is born in the secret place — in the accumulated weight of seasons where a man has been brought to the absolute end of himself and found, again and again, that the Lord was already there. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sat in this conversation with my brother Samuel, the thing I kept noticing was how naturally and unselfconsciously the Spirit was threading Himself through what sounded, on the surface, like a business call. I was not orchestrating a teaching moment. I was tired — genuinely tired — and I had already told the Lord that morning that my body and my soul were simply exhausted. And yet even from that posture of admitted depletion, the Spirit was not absent. He was pressing, guiding, and occasionally correcting me in real time, even as I tried to encourage Samuel. That is itself a revelation worth sitting with: the Spirit does not wait for us to feel adequate before He begins to move through us. The moment I find most theologically charged in this entire exchange is the small financial transaction — the five dollars sent to Mercury, the twenty-five cent fee, the six dollars and nineteen cents in the account, the wife’s provision arriving at precisely the right instant. What I said in the conversation was true: it was not me who orchestrated that. I was the one *in position* to hear what to do, exactly when it needed to be done. This is the posture of the servant who has learned through long discipline that provision does not originate from his ingenuity but flows through his availability. It calls to mind the word of Jesus in Luke 16:10 — *“He who is faithful in what is least is also faithful in much.”* The Lord was not testing Samuel’s bank account in that moment. He was demonstrating to both of us the principle of faithful stewardship in the small: instant obedience, exact timing, no room for hesitation. Five dollars and the precision of six-nineteen — these are not trivial. They are a living parable, enacted in real time, on an otherwise ordinary Tuesday. I am also sitting with the correction I received earlier that same morning, before the call even began. I had woken exhausted and lamented to the Lord — genuinely, not performatively. And His response was not sympathy. It was more work, and a manager pressing a rush order, and staying late. When I looked up at the ceiling in that moment, I said *fine, I’ll stop complaining.* And I mean that in the most reverent sense — not resigned resignation, but genuine reorientation. The Lord was reframing my awareness of my own capacity. He was, in essence, asking me the question He asked the disciples in the storm: *Why are you so fearful? Have you still no faith?* He was not dismissing my exhaustion; He was placing it within a larger frame. The weariness I feel is real. But He was reminding me that I am not as overextended as I perceive myself to be when my eyes are fixed on Him rather than on the accumulating weight of my circumstances. This brings me to what I shared with Samuel about the season I believe the Lord has me in — and what I believe He is doing in Samuel as well. I spoke of a *mantle passing season*, and I want to be careful and precise about what I mean by that. I am not claiming prophetic certainty over Samuel’s calling. What I am saying is that I have watched this young man build — through divorce, through international relocation, through locked accounts and failed ventures and family pressure — and there is a faithfulness in that building that is not merely entrepreneurial. It is covenantal. The Greek word παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) — the fatherly training, the discipline administered by a father who loves his son — is not abstract theology for Samuel. It is the texture of the last several years of his life. And the Scripture is clear in Hebrews 12:11 that *“no chastening seems to be joyful for the present, but painful; nevertheless, afterward it yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it.”* The afterward is arriving. The harvest of that painful formation is beginning to show. The word that most arrested me in this conversation was Samuel’s own language when I cautioned him about whether the Christian network idea was a gift *of* an idea or a directive *from* God. He said, almost involuntarily: *It feels like a gift of an idea.* I want to honor that instinct without overinterpreting it. The Spirit does sometimes drop vision into the spirit of a man as a gift — a seed that requires cultivation, testing, and discernment before it becomes a directive. What I pressed back with was not skepticism but the posture that has kept me from many costly mistakes: return to the secret place, dwell there, ask again, and see if the same impression remains under sustained intercession. Not every sudden great idea is from the Spirit. But neither does the Spirit withhold creative vision from His servants. The discernment is in the returning. This is where the Nicodemus thread I raised became important, and I want to develop it more fully now than I was able to in the moment. In John 3, Nicodemus came to Jesus νυκτός (nyktos, NOOK-tohs) — by night. Under cover. Privately. His hunger for the truth was genuine, but his public identity remained protected. The cost of his initial encounter with Christ was entirely internal. Nothing changed in his visible life. He remained a Pharisee, a ruler of the Jews, a man of social standing — and his encounter with the Messiah existed only in the secret room of his own awareness. But this is not where Nicodemus ends in the canon. In John 7:50–51, he raises his voice — carefully, legally, but publicly — in the Sanhedrin: *“Does our law judge a man before it hears him and knows what he is doing?”* It was not yet a full confession. It was the first crack of daylight in a man who had lived in the dark regarding his faith. And then in John 19:39, after the crucifixion, Nicodemus comes openly, bringing *a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about a hundred pounds,* to prepare the body of Jesus. The anonymity is entirely gone. The cost is now total and public. The journey from the night visit to the public anointing is the journey every disciple who hides their faith must eventually make. And it is not optional. It is the shape of genuine discipleship. I spoke this to Samuel not as condemnation but as preparation. His instinct for privacy is understandable — it is even, in some seasons, wisdom. But there is a Nicodemus posture that the Lord will not permit His servants to maintain indefinitely, especially those He is positioning in public-facing Kingdom work. The question is not *if* that moment of public declaration will come. The question is whether we will be ready when it does, and whether we will have cultivated enough of a secret-place relationship with the Father that when He requires our face and our name and our testimony in public, we are not caught unprepared. The παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) of the hidden season is what makes the public declaration possible without it producing pride. There is also something deeply significant in the way the conversation moved from Samuel’s business strategy into the possibility of Christian business networks, and then into church distribution and corporate Body life. What began as a purely commercial conversation kept reaching upward toward something more covenantal. This is not incidental. It is the Spirit pressing on Samuel’s entrepreneurial gift and asking: *What if this is not just for profit? What if the network itself is the ministry?* I want to be careful not to prophecy beyond what the transcript supports, but I will say this: the fact that Samuel himself was the one who raised the idea of Christian networks, of faith-based communities, of pushing good missions through the structure — that movement toward consecration was not prompted by me. It arose from within him. And that is worth paying attention to. The Petrine arc I referenced is also worth unpacking. In Luke 22, Peter swears he will never deny Jesus. And then three times, under the pressure of public scrutiny, he does. And then in John 21, after the resurrection, the restoration comes not through a rebuke but through a question: *“Simon, son of Jonah, do you love Me?”* Three times, matching the three denials, Jesus restores Peter’s commission — *feed My lambs, tend My sheep, feed My sheep.* The point is not that Peter was uniquely weak. The point is that the public restoration was as necessary as the private failure. Discipleship is not merely interior. It is enacted before the watching world, and the testimony of a restored man carries a weight that an untested man simply cannot carry. Samuel is still young. His formation is ongoing. But the arc is already visible in him. I want to close this main body of reflection on a note of personal weight. When I told Samuel that my walk with the Lord has been one of being brought to the very end of myself — to the place where my mind, my flesh, and my soul simply cannot handle it — I was not performing spiritual humility. That is the actual geography of my discipleship. And what I have discovered in that geography is that the end of self is not the end of the journey. It is the beginning of real communion. When there is nothing left of your own cleverness, your own resources, your own stamina, and your own plan — what remains is the Lord Himself. And from that place, language changes. Posture changes. When I said *it will be done* about those five dollars, I was not mustering faith. I was simply reporting from a place of prior experience with a God who provides. That is what the long school of the Spirit produces: not louder declarations, but quieter certainty. ## Implications and Challenges The conversation raises a gentle but necessary challenge to the way many believers in the modern Western church relate to entrepreneurial vision and so-called God-ideas. There is a subtle but dangerous pattern of *consecrating after the fact* — of building something according to our own vision and then asking the Lord to bless it once it is already in motion. The corrective is not passivity; it is the discipline of returning to the secret place *before* the launch, *before* the ads, *before* the hire — and waiting long enough to distinguish between genuine Spirit-direction and the enthusiasm of a gifted mind encountering a great idea. Both are real. Only one is guaranteed not to eventually run on empty. The Nicodemus pattern is far more common in the contemporary church than we acknowledge. Believers who maintain genuine, private faith while carefully protecting their public identity from any association with the gospel are not spiritually dishonest — they may simply be in a Nicodemus season. The challenge is refusing to make that season permanent. Jesus told His disciples plainly in Matthew 10:32–33: *“Whoever confesses Me before men, him I will also confess before My Father who is in heaven. But whoever denies Me before men, him I will also deny before My Father who is in heaven.”* This is not a peripheral verse. It is covenantal language. The Body of Christ must be a community where the anonymous believer is gently, graciously, and persistently invited into the light — not shamed out of their hiddenness, but drawn by love toward the cost of visibility. There is also a challenge embedded in the discussion of multi-level and ambassador-driven structures. Not because such models are inherently wrong — history shows they can work when framed ethically and transparently — but because the Kingdom of God must not simply rebrand commercial systems and call the rebranding consecration. If Samuel builds a Christian business network, the question is not whether it carries Christian aesthetics but whether it carries Christian ethics: genuine service, transparent economics, dignity for every participant, and a refusal to exploit the language of faith to generate commercial trust. The model must be as holy as the mission. The conversation also surfaces a challenge for those who, like me, are in what I called an *altar season* — a season of intercession, rest, and watching rather than active public building. There is a temptation in this posture to either over-spiritualize it as a permanent calling or to become passive and disengaged from the real lives of the people we are called to serve. Staying at the altar while others build is not an excuse for disconnection. It is a priestly commission. The priest does not go to battle, but the army does not advance without the priest at the altar. Finally, the moment of instant obedience — the six dollars and nineteen cents, the prompting in the chest, the precise timing — carries a direct challenge to the modern Western Christian’s relationship with hearing. We have largely accepted a low-grade theology of divine communication, in which God speaks primarily through circumstances and general wisdom rather than through specific, real-time, Spirit-to-spirit direction. But what I experienced that morning is not exotic or rare in the New Covenant. It is the promised birthright of every son and daughter who walks in step with the רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — the Spirit, the very breath of God. If we do not cultivate the inner quiet necessary to hear those promptings, we will regularly miss the exact moment and the exact amount and the exact provision the Father has already prepared. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity in the Spirit is not marked by the size of what we are building but by the quality of our listening before we build. It means developing the capacity to hold a great idea loosely enough to bring it back to the Father and genuinely ask: *Is this Yours, or is this mine?* It means being willing to stay in the secret place even when the market timing feels urgent. And it means learning to receive correction — like the correction I received through a manager adding boxes and pressure on an already exhausting day — as the Lord’s loving reorientation of our gaze, not as punishment. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Kingdom obedience in the marketplace looks like building with the *Imago Dei* as the governing principle — treating every customer, every ambassador, every competitor, every church contact as a child of God whose dignity is non-negotiable. It looks like committing to transparent business ethics that do not require the Lord’s name to be invoked in order to justify a model that could not otherwise bear scrutiny. And it looks like launching — actually launching, in faith, with the money available — while remaining genuinely submitted to the Father’s right to redirect, reframe, or consecrate the work as it unfolds. **For Prophetic Posture:** Those of us who are currently in altar seasons must not mistake stillness for absence from the mission. Our intercession is not a consolation prize for those too tired to build. It is the invisible infrastructure that holds the builder upright in the spiritual atmosphere. And those who are building must cultivate the habit of returning to the secret place — not once, but repeatedly — to ensure that the structure being raised is one the Lord Himself is willing to inhabit. The prophetic posture for this season is one of collaborative faithfulness: the intercessor and the builder, the priest and the marketplace servant, together — neither dismissing the other’s assignment. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You now as a son who has been brought to the end of himself enough times to know that the end of self is the beginning of You — and I am grateful for every one of those moments, even the ones that nearly undid me. I thank You for the conversation today, for the five dollars and the six dollars and nineteen cents, for the precision and the humor and the grace with which You make Yourself known in the smallest of things. I ask that You continue to build Samuel from the inside out — that the παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) of his hidden seasons would bear the peaceable fruit of righteousness in public ways that glorify Your name and not his own. I ask that every idea that is genuinely Yours would be made unmistakably clear through sustained time in the secret place, and that every idea that is merely good would be set aside without grief. Keep us both from the Nicodemus posture that becomes a permanent residence. Bring us, in Your timing and by Your grace, into the full light of public testimony — faces uncovered, names spoken, testimonies unashamed. I stand at this altar, Lord, not because I am done but because You have placed me here, and I trust that the altar and the marketplace are not in conflict in Your economy — they are two movements of the same Kingdom. Let everything built in this season be built on You, under You, and for You. Let the רוּחַ (ROO-akh) guide every step we cannot yet see. And let the fruit remain. In the name of Jesus Christ, the One who came in the flesh, who rose from the dead, and who is coming again — Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Luke 16:10 — “He who is faithful in what is least is also faithful in much” | πιστός (pistos, PISS-tohs) — faithful, trustworthy | Faithfulness is not scaled to the size of the task; it is a character quality tested in the small and demonstrated in the large.
