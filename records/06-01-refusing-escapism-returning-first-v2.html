# 06-01 Call Reflection: Refusing Escapism and Returning to Christ **Title:** 06-01 at 19:09 — Refusing Escapism and Returning to the First Works **Recorded:** 2026-06-01 19:09:38 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection — ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother describe saying no to edibles while grieving, something in me recognized the holy violence of that moment. It was not glamorous. It was not clean. It was not wrapped in church language. But beneath the frustration, the profanity, the ache, and the temptation, I heard the sound of a man refusing to be ruled by escape. **Why This Matters:** This matters because discipleship is often tested at the exact point where pain begs for anesthesia. Many of us do not first fall because we hate God; we fall because we are weary, grieving, restless, lonely, ashamed, or afraid, and we want something to quiet the ache before the Lord has finished speaking into it. The call was full of ordinary things—cars, work, dating, jokes, accents, family interruptions—but the spiritual thread underneath was unmistakable: the Lord was exposing the difference between being comforted by Him and being numbed by created things. That distinction is not small. It touches holiness, maturity, endurance, worship, identity, and the way the Body of Christ learns to carry pain without bowing to idols. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The worldly mind would likely interpret the conversation as two friends processing stress, addiction patterns, grief, ambition, distraction, and everyday life. It would see the refusal of edibles as willpower, perhaps helped by accountability and competition. It would frame the talk about success after divorce as emotional motivation, the discussion about substances as behavioral change, the comments about provocative dress and danger as cultural frustration, and the car talk as ordinary friendship and masculine bonding. In that reading, everything is horizontal: pain management, self-improvement, social commentary, personal discipline, and practical survival. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind cannot perceive the altar hidden inside an ordinary phone call. It can identify coping mechanisms, but it cannot discern when the Holy Spirit is teaching a son to stop calling bondage “relief.” It can applaud discipline, but it cannot recognize repentance unless repentance looks polished and religious. It can hear a brother say, “I said no,” and reduce it to self-control, while missing the deeper issue: a throne is being contested. Something is asking for lordship over the wounded places of the heart, and the Spirit of Christ is calling those places back under the reign of God. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I am seeing that saying no to escapism is not merely abstinence; it is worship. When my brother said no while grieving, the visible act was simple, but the invisible act was priestly. He was refusing to present his pain on the altar of numbness. Paul says, “present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable to God, which is your reasonable service” (Romans 12:1, NKJV). That presentation includes the body when it craves relief, the mind when it wants to flee, and the heart when grief demands a substitute savior. Something arrests me in the way he said he hated himself afterward. That detail matters. Obedience does not always feel immediately victorious. Sometimes the flesh throws a tantrum after the spirit obeys. Hebrews says, “Now no chastening seems to be joyful for the present, but painful; nevertheless, afterward it yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness” (Hebrews 12:11, NKJV). The “afterward” is crucial. There are moments when obedience feels like loss before it becomes liberty. When he named the real issue as escapism, I felt the Spirit putting language to a deeper bondage than weed itself. Substances may be the instrument, but escape is the principality of the pattern. A man can escape through weed, alcohol, food, lust, fantasy, entertainment, work, theology, ministry, ambition, or even constant crisis. The object changes, but the movement is the same: I do not want to remain present before God in the place where I hurt. This is where the Scripture cuts deeper than behavior management. Jesus does not merely ask, “Did you use the substance?” He asks, “Where did you run when your soul was pierced?” David says, “Trust in Him at all times, you people; pour out your heart before Him; God is a refuge for us” (Psalm 62:8, NKJV). The enemy offers escape by suppressing the heart; God offers refuge by receiving the heart. Escape buries pain alive. Refuge brings pain into the presence of the Living God. I notice also that competition entered the conversation as motivation. My brother wanted to win, to outlast, to prove something. There can be a natural usefulness in that, but the Spirit keeps purifying motive. Paul uses athletic language without shame: “Run in such a way that you may obtain it” (1 Corinthians 9:24, NKJV). Yet the prize is not ego. The prize is freedom unto Christ. The danger is when victory over a substance becomes another form of self-exaltation. The grace is when even immature motives become training wheels until love becomes the stronger engine. The conversation about vengeance after betrayal and divorce carried that same tension. There is a kind of wounded ambition that can either harden into bitterness or be transformed into holy endurance. Joseph is the great witness here. His brothers meant evil against him, but he later said, “God meant it for good, in order to bring it about as it is this day, to save many people alive” (Genesis 50:20, NKJV). Joseph did not deny the evil. He did not pretend betrayal was harmless. But he refused to let betrayal become the interpreter of his destiny. That is the difference between revenge and resurrection. Revenge says, “I will become great so they regret losing me.” Resurrection says, “God will raise what was buried, and the fruit will feed even those who wounded me.” Joseph’s authority in Egypt was not merely promotion; it was preservation. If God gives us increase after grief, it is never merely so we can stand above our enemies. It is so we can become bread in a famine. The thread about kingship also stood out to me. In the call, we moved from earthly royalty to the claim that there is no true King but God. Even when the details of extra-biblical stories or retellings need careful testing, the instinct itself touched something deeply biblical. Israel’s tragedy was not simply that they wanted a king; it was that they wanted a visible order that could replace dependence on the invisible God. The Lord told Samuel, “they have not rejected you, but they have rejected Me, that I should not reign over them” (1 Samuel 8:7, NKJV). That exposes the heart of idolatry. Idolatry is not only worshiping statues; it is asking something created to carry the weight of divine rule. Substances can become kings. Grief can become king. Success can become king. Romance can become king. Governments, celebrities, institutions, and even religious leaders can become kings. But Revelation calls Jesus “King of kings and Lord of lords” (Revelation 19:16, NKJV). Every lesser throne must bow. When we mentioned the leaven of the Pharisees and Herod, I felt the Spirit joining two corruptions: religious appearance and political power. Jesus warned, “Take heed, beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and the leaven of Herod” (Mark 8:15, NKJV). Leaven works quietly. It spreads through the whole lump. The Pharisee wants to look righteous before men; Herod wants to preserve power before men. Together, they produce a religion that honors God with lips while enthroning self in practice. That is why the question that came up later matters so much: not merely “Is this sinful?” but “Does this glorify God?” That question is sharper because it moves from minimum legality to maximum love. Paul writes, “Therefore, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do all to the glory of God” (1 Corinthians 10:31, NKJV). The immature heart asks, “How close can I get to darkness without technically disobeying?” The maturing heart asks, “How fully can this life become a window for the worth of Christ?” This applies to clothing, speech, substances, ambition, relationships, humor, money, and even how we steward old cars and ordinary possessions. The issue is not becoming anxious, performative, or religiously paranoid. The issue is consecration. The Hebrew idea קָדוֹשׁ (qadosh, kah-DOHSH) — holy / set apart — does not mean fragile or sterile. It means belonging to God. Holiness is not the absence of personality; it is personality reclaimed from corruption and offered back to the One who made it. Even the car conversation began to speak to me as a parable. There was an old vehicle, loud and imperfect, but still moving. It needed attention, patience, diagnosis, and care. It had parts that needed lubrication, systems that needed testing, and sounds that needed discernment. I am seeing how often the Lord deals with us that way. He does not discard us because we rattle. He listens under the hood. He knows whether the sound is surface-level exhaust or something deeper in the connection between power and movement. There was also something tender in the ordinary friendship itself. Two brothers talking about God, addiction, Genesis, business meetings, cars, pen pal letters, jokes, and family interruptions may not look like ministry to religious eyes. But fellowship is often less polished than we imagine. The Greek word κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-noh-NEE-ah) — fellowship / shared participation — is not merely sitting in rows at a service. It is shared life under Christ. Sometimes κοινωνία (koy-noh-NEE-ah) sounds like prayer. Sometimes it sounds like warning a brother not to go pick up weed. Sometimes it sounds like laughing while still telling the truth. The moment where I snapped at a reckless driver and then immediately recognized I could have prayed was also revealing. I said something like, “Get your life together,” and then I realized that sometimes that is exactly how correction feels in the secret place. Not cruel, not rejecting, but direct. Jesus says in Revelation, “As many as I love, I rebuke and chasten. Therefore be zealous and repent” (Revelation 3:19, NKJV). The word παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) — fatherly training / discipline — helps me here. God’s correction is not the punishment of an enemy; it is the training of sons. That means the rebuke itself can be love. In a culture that often treats correction as rejection, the Lord teaches us to receive His discipline as evidence of belonging. Hebrews says, “For whom the LORD loves He chastens, and scourges every son whom He receives” (Hebrews 12:6, NKJV). The point is not that God is harsh. The point is that He is Father. He refuses to let sons remain enslaved to impulses that are beneath their inheritance. As I sit with the whole call, I am struck by how the Spirit weaves sacred conviction through scattered human conversation. The call did not stay on one topic. It moved like real life moves. But underneath it all, the Lord kept pressing the same question: Who is king here? Is grief king? Is escape king? Is lust king? Is ambition king? Is fear king? Is public opinion king? Or is Jesus truly Lord over the hidden places, the aching places, the ordinary places, and the still-unfinished places? And this is where I feel the invitation becoming communal. We are not merely trying to stop bad habits. We are learning to become a people who can remain present to God without needing false refuge. We are learning to tell each other the truth without contempt. We are learning to celebrate small obediences because small obediences often mark the beginning of great deliverance. We are learning that the Kingdom of God comes not only in dramatic altar moments, but also when a grieving brother says no, when another brother receives correction, when Scripture becomes alive, and when ordinary friendship becomes a battleground for freedom. ## Implications and Challenges Grace meets us before it corrects us, but grace does correct us. One challenge here is that modern Western Christianity can sometimes treat escapism lightly if the escape is culturally acceptable. We may condemn certain addictions while ignoring the more respectable ways we avoid God: endless scrolling, emotional dependency, constant entertainment, overwork, fantasy, or spiritual busyness. The Spirit is not merely asking what we use; He is asking why we flee. A second challenge is that we often want holiness to feel instantly rewarding. But this call reminded me that obedience can feel awful in the moment. A person may say no and still feel angry, deprived, irritated, or sorrowful afterward. That does not mean the obedience failed. It may mean the flesh is losing its assumed right to rule. A third challenge is that motivation must be purified without being prematurely dismissed. Competition, ambition, and even the desire to prove that one’s life will not be destroyed by betrayal can become dangerous if they harden into bitterness. But submitted to Christ, those same energies can be redirected into endurance, discipline, provision, and testimony. The Lord does not merely erase intensity; He sanctifies it. A fourth challenge is that we need to recover the higher question: “Does this glorify God?” Many believers live at the level of technical permission rather than priestly offering. But New Covenant freedom is not freedom to remain self-centered without consequence. It is freedom to belong wholly to Christ. A fifth challenge is that fellowship must become more honest. If brothers and sisters cannot say, “Do not do it; join me in this journey God has put us on,” then we have settled for politeness instead of love. Real love does not control, manipulate, or shame, but it does contend. It stands at the door with a brother and says, “You are more capable in Christ than this bondage has told you.” And still, all of this must end in mercy. None of us are free because we are strong in ourselves. We are free because Jesus Christ breaks yokes, receives the brokenhearted, rebukes those He loves, and gives His Spirit to train us into sonship. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This teaches us to discern the difference between comfort and numbness. Maturity means learning to bring grief, craving, anger, fear, and loneliness into the presence of God instead of handing them to false refuges. It also means receiving correction as love, not as condemnation. **For Kingdom Obedience:** This calls us to obey in the immediate moment, even before our emotions agree. Saying no once matters. Turning away once matters. Calling a brother instead of disappearing matters. Returning to the first works matters. The Kingdom advances in these hidden obediences. **For Prophetic Posture:** This trains us to watch the throne of the heart. The prophetic life is not only about seeing external events; it is about discerning what seeks lordship within us. We abide, wait, testify, and yield as servants who know there is no true King but God, and every counterfeit throne must fall before Jesus Christ. ## A Closing Prayer Father, in the name of Jesus Christ, I thank You for the mercy hidden in ordinary moments and for the Spirit who teaches us even through imperfect conversations. I come into agreement with Your will between the heavens and the earth, as one made a co-heir in Christ, a servant under New Covenant authority, and one who desires to be faithful with the secrets You give to Your servants. Teach us to refuse escapism, not by pride, but by deeper surrender. Train us through Your loving rebuke. Make us a people who run to You with grief instead of bowing to idols. Strengthen my brothers and sisters in the hour of craving, cleanse our motives, purify our ambitions, and enthrone Jesus over every hidden place. Let our lives glorify You in what we refuse, what we receive, what we endure, and what we offer. To the King of kings and Lord of lords be honor, dominion, praise, and thanksgiving forever.
