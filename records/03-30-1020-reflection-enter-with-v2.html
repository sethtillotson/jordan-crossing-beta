# 03-30 @ 10:20 Reflection: Enter With Nothing — Striving, Abiding, and the Secret Place That Undoes Us **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation (live exchange between Seth and Samuel) with brief post-conversational reflection woven throughout **Personal Meditation: Enter With Nothing — The Striving Soul, the Secret Place, and the Terrifying Grace of Abiding** ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother walk through his plans — the business, the moral justifications, the sense of spiritual momentum — something in me recognized a tension I have sat with many times myself. There is a particular kind of deception that does not come dressed in darkness; it comes dressed in good intentions, Kingdom language, and even genuine compassion for hurting people. What arrested me most in that conversation was not the question of whether the work itself was wrong, but the deeper question underneath it: *whose initiative is this, really?* The distance between striving and abiding is razor thin from the outside, but from the inside, it is the difference between building on sand and building on the Rock. **Why This Matters:** The saints of God are not primarily threatened by obvious sin. Most mature believers have learned to recognize and resist the blatant works of darkness. The real threat — the Trojan horse threat — is the subtle substitution of our own well-intentioned striving for the quiet, costly, daily obedience of abiding in the Vine. When a believer cannot distinguish between the warmth of self-initiated purpose and the fire of God-breathed calling, they will spend their most spiritually productive seasons building what will ultimately need to be torn down. This matters because the Body of Christ is full of sincere, gifted, morally serious people who are exhausted — not because they have been idle, but because they have been working without hearing. The invitation to the secret place is not a passive retreat from responsibility; it is the very source of every truly fruitful work. If we miss this, we risk producing a Christianity that looks extraordinarily busy and feels profoundly right, while being quietly severed from the Living Vine who alone bears lasting fruit. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind listening to that conversation would almost certainly side with Samuel. Here is a man who has identified a real problem — financial fraud destroying real families — and who possesses the skills, the methodology, and even a redemptive personal motivation to address it. He is not chasing money carelessly; he is thinking morally, structuring intentionally, and even framing the entire enterprise within a faith commitment. From a purely human perspective, this is precisely what personal growth and social responsibility look like. Psychology would call it post-traumatic integration — transforming past harm into present healing. Culture would applaud the entrepreneurial pivot toward purpose-driven work. Even a casual Christian observer might see this and say, “Well, he’s helping people and giving God the credit — what more do you want?” The urgency to act, the good feelings surrounding the work, the genuine compassion for victims — all of it registers as virtue by every measurable standard the natural mind has access to. **The Limitation:** And yet the natural mind, however sincere and however morally serious, cannot perceive what lies beneath the surface of sincere motivation. It has no instrument calibrated for the difference between the soul’s warmth toward a good idea and the Spirit’s commission toward an appointed work. It cannot see the Trojan horse concealed inside genuine compassion — the slow, almost imperceptible shift from “God through me” to “me, sanctified by God.” The natural mind is also unable to account for what *does not get done* because of what *does* — specifically, the hours in the secret place that get quietly consumed by hours of striving. The limitation is not that the natural mind is wicked; it is that it genuinely cannot access the frequency on which the Father speaks, and it will always, always mistake the glow of personal righteousness for the light of Heaven. ## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation As I reflect on what I heard in that conversation — as I trace the contours of what my brother was working through and what the Spirit pressed me to say in response — I find myself standing before a passage I cannot escape. Jesus said in John 15:5, “I am the vine, you are the branches. He who abides in Me, and I in him, bears much fruit; for without Me you can do nothing.” The word translated “abides” is μένω (menō, MEH-no) — to remain, to dwell, to stay. It is not an occasional visit. It is not a spiritual check-in before launching a plan. It is a posture of permanent residence. And the terrifying corollary is what Jesus says next: “without Me you can do nothing.” Not *less*. Not *less effectively*. Nothing. The Greek word is οὐδέν (ouden, OO-den) — nothing at all, not even a little, zero. This is not hyperbole. This is the architecture of the Kingdom. What I was pressing my brother toward is not inactivity. I want to be clear about that, because I feel it is easy to hear the call to the secret place as a call to passivity. It is not. Elijah was one of the most active prophets in Israel’s history — fire called from Heaven, false prophets confronted, kings rebuked, drought declared. And yet the moment he ran from Jezebel in exhaustion and fear, the angel of the Lord did not give him a strategy. He gave him bread and water and rest. Twice. “Arise and eat,” the angel said, “for the journey is too great for you” (1 Kings 19:7). The journey ahead required nourishment that human striving could not manufacture. Elijah had to be fed from Heaven before he could walk forty days to Horeb — to the mountain of God — where the still small voice was waiting. The secret place is not where nothing happens. It is where everything essential begins. What troubled me in listening to my brother was something I recognize in myself — the impulse to take something true about who God made me to be and run with it before I have heard the word “go.” My brother has a genuinely sharp analytical mind. He has lived through a season of sin. He has emerged with real skill and real compassion. All of that is true. But truthfulness is not the same as timeliness. Not everything that is genuinely from God is genuinely from God *right now*. Abraham had been promised a son. Sarah was genuinely his wife. But when they tried to produce the promised heir through Hagar, they were working with true materials in the wrong season — and the consequences echoed for millennia. What I was saying to my brother, in essence, was: the materials may well be from God; the question is whether the timing and the manner of their use have been *heard* or merely *reasoned*. There is a phrase I find myself returning to: the difference between sanctified striving and surrendered abiding. Sanctified striving says, “Lord, I am doing this — please bless it and correct anything wrong with it.” Surrendered abiding says, “Lord, I am not doing anything until I hear You.” Both postures can sound humble. Both can feel spiritual. But they operate from entirely different foundations. The first positions the self as the initiating agent and God as the sanctifying agent. The second positions God as the initiating agent and the self as the responsive instrument. Jesus never modeled the first. He modeled the second absolutely. “The Son can do nothing of Himself, but what He sees the Father do; for whatever He does, the Son also does in like manner” (John 5:19). The Greek verb here for “sees” is βλέπω (blepō, BLEH-po) — active, present-tense, ongoing sight. Not a remembered vision. A continuous watching. This is precisely where the Job analogy becomes so important — and I felt it necessary to press my brother here because I knew it would sting slightly. Job was described by God Himself as “a blameless and upright man, one who fears God and shuns evil” (Job 1:8). That is not a small commendation. And yet buried in the narrative — so subtly that readers miss it through thirty-seven chapters — is a root of self-righteousness that Job himself could not see. He was not trying to be self-righteous. He was defending his genuine integrity. But defending genuine integrity before God has a name, and that name is self-righteousness — because it implicitly argues that the self’s record is a valid basis for standing before the Holy One. When God finally speaks from the whirlwind in Job 38, He does not address Job’s suffering. He addresses Job’s assumption that Job’s righteousness entitled him to an explanation. The correction is not condemnation — but it is thorough. And it produces something Job’s striving never could: a sight of God that undoes and restores simultaneously. “I have heard of You by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees You” (Job 42:5). I think about the Trojan horse image I used in that conversation, and the Spirit keeps pressing me deeper into it. The enemy rarely attacks the mature believer at the point of obvious sin. That battle, for a sincere disciple, is often largely won. The real assault comes at the point of genuine strength — gifting, compassion, vision, moral seriousness. Because those are the things we are least likely to test. We test our fears. We test our doubts. We rarely test the things that feel righteous and motivated and good. But Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 11:14 that “Satan himself transforms himself into an angel of light.” The word “transforms” is μετασχηματίζεται (metaschēmatízetai, meh-tah-skay-mah-TI-zeh-tie) — a deliberate change of outward form, a costume change. The enemy does not announce himself as darkness when he comes as light. He comes as a business idea bathed in compassion. He comes as a calling draped in moral language. And the only defense is not cleverness — it is continuous residence in the secret place where the Father’s voice can be heard above the noise of our own enthusiasm. When I said to my brother, “start in the secret place, and always return to it,” I was thinking of Matthew 6:6 where Jesus says, “But you, when you pray, go into your room, and when you have shut your door, pray to your Father who is in the secret place; and your Father who sees in secret will reward you openly.” The word translated “secret place” here is κρυπτός (kryptos, KRIP-tos) — hidden, concealed, the unseen. The Father inhabits the unseen. His voice is heard in the hidden place. The reward — the fruit, the confirmation, the word — comes openly, visibly, verifiably. But it always originates in the hidden. What my brother was doing — reasoning through his business model, feeling the warmth of compassion, sensing the moral rightness of using his skills for good — was all happening in the *visible*. The kingdom-confirming word he needed was available only in the κρυπτός (KRIP-tos). And then something extraordinary happened in that conversation. My brother — entirely on his own, with no prompting from me — arrived at the mountain. He began describing a plan to take himself into the wilderness, fast for several days, bring nothing but his Bible and a change of clothes, and simply sit with God. And in that moment, I felt something shift. Not because it was a perfect spiritual plan, but because it was genuinely responsive. He had moved from striving to something that at least had the shape of surrender. This is the movement I care about. This is what Elijah’s walk to Horeb prefigures. This is what Moses’ forty days on Sinai prefigures. This is what Jesus’ forty days in the wilderness prefigures. The wilderness is not a punishment — it is a preparation. It is where false dependencies are starved and true dependence on the Father is established. רָעַב (ra’av, RAH-av) — hunger — has always been one of God’s most effective teachers. When you are truly empty, you discover who your Provider actually is. You meet יְהוָה יִרְאֶה (Yahweh Yireh, yah-WEH yir-EH) — the LORD who sees and provides — not as a theological concept, but as a personal, present, daily reality. I think about what Jesus said when He was hungry in the wilderness and the enemy offered Him bread: “It is written, ‘Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God’” (Matthew 4:4). The word translated “proceeds” is ἐκπορευομένῳ (ekporeuomenō, ek-po-REV-oh-meh-no) — present participle, continuous action. Not “every word that *once* proceeded.” Every word that *is right now proceeding*. The mouth of God is not closed. The Father is still speaking. And my brother, in planning that mountain excursion with nothing but himself and his Bible and his fast, is positioning himself to hear what is presently proceeding — not to confirm a business plan, not to get divine sanction for what he has already decided, but simply to *be there*, empty, available, and waiting. That is the right posture. That is the beginning of real fruitfulness. My final word to him was this: “Enter with nothing, or you’ll leave with nothing.” I want to unpack that because it sounds paradoxical and I mean it with full weight. If you enter the secret place with an agenda — even a spiritually framed agenda, even a morally serious one — you will find yourself filtering whatever you hear through that agenda. You will emerge having found confirmation, or warning, or reassurance, but all of it passing through the lens of what you already wanted to resolve. That is not the secret place; that is a prayer meeting with yourself as the primary participant. The secret place that Jesus describes in Matthew 6 is a *shutting of the door* — a deliberate closure of the self’s noise so that only One voice remains. My brother himself said it best at the end of our conversation: “I’m not going to ask ‘is this right or wrong?’ I’m just going to shut up and listen.” That is wisdom. That is the beginning of μένω (MEH-no) — of remaining, of abiding, of dwelling where the Father dwells. Whatever comes out of that mountain time, it will be real. And real is what my brother needs more than anything his own gifted mind can produce. ## Implications and Challenges The conversation I witnessed and participated in surfaces a challenge that I believe is one of the most pressing and least addressed in the contemporary Western church: we have produced a culture of spiritually-framed self-initiation and called it calling. We celebrate the believer who acts boldly, launches courageously, and credits God faithfully — without ever seriously examining whether the action originated in the throne room of Heaven or in the ambition of a sincere human heart. The result is a church that is extraordinarily busy and frequently exhausted, producing fruit that often does not survive the first serious storm. James 1:17 tells us that “every good gift and every perfect gift is from above.” But we have quietly reversed the direction — bringing good gifts upward to God for blessing, rather than receiving perfect gifts downward from God for deployment. This inversion is subtle, it is sincere, and it is devastating in the long run. There is also a challenge here to the way we handle moral motivation as spiritual justification. My brother’s compassion for scam victims is genuine — and that genuineness is precisely what makes it dangerous as a standalone guide. Genuine compassion is not the same as divine commission. The history of the church is full of sincere, compassionate believers who did real harm in the name of God precisely because they trusted their own moral clarity over the still small voice. The Crusades surfaced naturally in our conversation as a case study — men who were genuinely defending persecuted Christians, who genuinely loved God, who genuinely believed their cause righteous, and who built a legacy of violence, trauma, and spiritual corruption that took centuries to partially undo. “The road to hell is paved with good intentions” is a cliché because it is relentlessly true. The corrective is not cynicism about compassion — it is the discipline of holding every compassionate impulse before the Father long enough to distinguish between what *we* feel called to and what *He* has commissioned. The conversation also challenges a particular kind of modern discipleship that is long on doing and short on waiting. We have largely lost the theology and the practice of holy waiting — the kind that Daniel practiced three times daily with his windows open toward Jerusalem (Daniel 6:10), the kind that Anna practiced for eighty-four years in the temple “with fastings and prayers night and day” (Luke 2:37), the kind that Jesus modeled in rising “a long while before daylight” to go to a “solitary place” to pray (Mark 1:35). We have replaced these rhythms with podcasts about purpose, devotionals about destiny, and conferences about calling — all of which can be genuinely helpful, but none of which substitute for the actual practice of daily residence in the secret place. The discipline my brother is planning — several days in a wilderness setting, fasting, silent, without agenda — is not extreme. By the standards of the biblical pattern, it is simply normal. It is the modern Christian who has drifted, not the one returning to the mountain. The self-righteousness thread that I raised in the conversation deserves its own honest examination. Self-righteousness is rarely experienced as pride. It is almost always experienced as *integrity*. Job did not feel proud — he felt wrongly accused. My brother did not feel self-righteous — he felt morally serious. This is what makes it so treacherous. The spiritual discipline needed here is not more rigorous self-examination — that can actually deepen self-focus. It is the practice of bringing every thought, every plan, every conviction into the light of God’s presence and letting Him be the one to affirm, redirect, or dismantle. Paul says in 2 Corinthians 10:5 that we take “every thought into captivity to the obedience of Christ.” The Greek word for “captivity” is αἰχμαλωτίζοντες (aichmalōtizontes, ike-mah-lo-TI-zon-tez) — to take as a prisoner of war, to lead captive by force. This is not gentle suggestion. It is the aggressive spiritual act of refusing to allow any thought — however righteous-feeling — to operate independently of Christ’s authority. That is the real battle. That is the warfare worth waging. Finally, I want to say something tender: the fact that my brother almost got offended — and *caught* himself getting offended — is a sign of genuine spiritual growth. The capacity to recognize the rising of self-defense as a signal that something true has touched something proud is a rare and valuable discernment. Where that discernment leads — to quietness, to the secret place, to honest surrender — is the narrow road. And the narrow road, Jesus promises, “leads to life” (Matthew 7:14). Not to less fruit. To life. To the kind of fruit that remains. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This meditation calls me — and those who walk alongside me — to submit every genuine gift, every sincere compassion, and every morally serious impulse to the discipline of the secret place before it becomes a plan. Spiritual maturity is not the accumulation of correct theology or impressive Kingdom activity. It is the deepening capacity to distinguish between the warmth of self-initiated purpose and the fire of Heaven-initiated commission. It grows through consistent, agenda-free residence in God’s presence — not through more sophisticated spiritual reasoning. Maturity looks like a willingness to lay down a good plan in obedience to a God who has not yet said “go.” **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this meditation calls forth is the hardest kind — not the obedience of doing something difficult, but the obedience of *not doing* something that feels right until the Father speaks. It is the obedience of entering the wilderness with nothing. It is the obedience of sitting in a “shitty factory job” for a season because the Father has not yet said otherwise. It is the obedience of μένω (MEH-no) — of remaining — when everything in us wants to move. Kingdom obedience begins not at the point of action but at the point of hearing, and hearing requires the consistent practice of holy, expectant silence. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic saints are not those who speak the most — they are those who have heard most deeply. Prophetic posture is built in the wilderness, on the mountain, in the secret place before dawn, in the hours of fasting that feel empty until they don’t. My brother’s planned wilderness excursion is, whether he fully realizes it yet or not, a prophetic act — a declaration that he will not feed himself from his own understanding but will wait for angels to minister, for the still small voice to come, for the Father to speak what no amount of striving could ever produce. That posture, sustained and returned to again and again, is what forms the kind of vessel through which God genuinely moves — not the vessel that impresses, but the vessel that carries. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I thank You that You are patient with us — patient with our striving, patient with our good intentions, patient with the Trojan horses we welcome with both hands while sincerely loving You. I stand in agreement with Your will over my brother Samuel, over myself, and over every one of Your beloved children who is right now confusing the warmth of their own compassion for the fire of Your commissioning. I declare as a co-heir with Christ, under the authority of the New Covenant sealed in His blood, that the secret place is *accessible* — that the veil has been torn, that the way into Your presence is permanently, gloriously open. I ask, Father, that You meet Samuel on that mountain with unmistakable presence. Let him hear not the echo of his own planning but Your voice — clear, specific, tender, and true. Starve out of him everything that does not come from You, and feed him with the bread that comes from Heaven, just as You fed Elijah in the wilderness and Your own Son in the desert. As for me, let this meditation be a returning to the altar — a fresh laying down of the self-initiated and a fresh taking up of only what You have breathed. May we all enter the secret place with nothing, and leave with everything You always intended us to carry. To the Father, through the Son, by the Spirit — all glory, now and forever.
