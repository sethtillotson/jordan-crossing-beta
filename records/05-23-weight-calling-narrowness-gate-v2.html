# 05-23 Conversation Reflection: Prophetic Calling, Kenosis, and the Secret Place **Title:** 05-23 at 13:24 — The Weight of Calling, the Narrowness of the Gate, and the Light That Guides Only One Step at a Time **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Conversation Reflection ## Opening Observation As I sat with this conversation, what struck me most was not the practical talk about cars and commutes and career moves — though all of that was real and present — but the moments when the conversation thinned out into something deeper, something that the Spirit seemed to be pressing through the ordinary exchange. There were two or three places in this conversation where the flow suddenly shifted, where the mundane gave way to the eternal, and something holy moved through what might otherwise have been just a casual catch-up between friends. What I heard — in those moments particularly — was a meditation on smallness before God, on the grief that accompanies true calling, and on the narrow gate that so few find not because it is hidden, but because most are unwilling to let go of everything they have carried to reach it. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ in this generation is in desperate need of conversations that do not stay merely horizontal. We talk about plans, jobs, relationships, ambitions, travels, and practical logistics — and none of that is wrong. But the saints who are being formed for the age to come are those who, even in the middle of life’s sprawling ordinary details, find themselves suddenly arrested by the weight of revelation, suddenly pulled back to the question of who God is and who they are in Him. What I heard in this conversation is a picture of that — two brothers moving back and forth between the earthly and the heavenly, and in those moments of theological clarity, speaking things that the Church has largely forgotten: that greatness before God begins with the recognition of our own smallness, that every gift comes wrapped in grief, and that the secret place is the only room large enough to hold the full weight of a human life. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, this conversation looks like what it partially is: two young men catching up, processing life transitions, discussing career pivots, complaining about gas prices, debating pricing strategies, and occasionally dipping into spiritual reflection before returning to practical matters. The natural mind would organize all of this under the heading of “life planning” — two people in the midst of navigating uncertainty, using friendship as a sounding board for decisions about work, location, finances, and identity. It would observe the theological moments as interesting diversions, perhaps even admirable evidence of their faith background, but ultimately secondary to the real substance of the conversation, which it would identify as forward momentum: where are we going, what are we building, how do we get there? The natural mind respects ambition, admires clarity of purpose, and values the kind of practical wisdom that turns raw talent into income. It would look at the conversation and say: two motivated young men with faith and drive, figuring out their next steps. **The Limitation:** But there is a register of understanding that ordinary human perception simply cannot access without the illuminating work of the Spirit — not because the natural mind lacks intelligence, but because it lacks the faculty of spiritual discernment that alone can hear what is underneath the surface of a conversation. The natural mind hears two men discussing pricing tiers and commission structures; the Spirit hears the same men, moments earlier, touching something ancient and vast: the knowledge that the human self, in its full unreconstructed weight, cannot be used by God as it is. The natural mind would process the comment about daily dying as a philosophical observation; the Spirit recognizes it as Paul’s cry from the interior of genuine cruciformity. The natural mind would hear the exchange about grief and calling as a relatable emotional observation; the Spirit hears the echo of every prophet who ever received a word too large for his current life to contain. To perceive what is actually moving through a conversation like this requires the mind of Christ — and that is not a faculty we develop; it is one we receive, in the secret place, through surrender. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I am sitting with something that my brother said near the middle of this conversation, something that stopped me even as I heard it live. He spoke about the discipline of deliberately placing yourself before the grandeur of Christ — not as a spiritual technique, but as a posture of recalibration. He described the experience of looking at the vastness of what God has done, at the sheer scale of the universe held in sovereign hands, and watching your own problems shrink in real time — not because they are dismissed, but because they are properly proportioned. This is not the same thing as toxic positivity or minimizing suffering. This is something older and far more costly. This is the posture of Isaiah in the temple, overwhelmed not by comfort but by the holy otherness of the One upon the throne, and simultaneously devastated by the recognition of his own smallness: *“Woe is me, for I am undone!”* (Isaiah 6:5, NKJV). The Hebrew word that underlies “undone” carries the sense of being silenced, cut off, dissolved at the seams. That is the beginning of prophetic formation — not a feeling of empowerment, but a collapse before greatness. What my brother was describing, perhaps without fully knowing it theologically, was the essential movement of κένωσις (kenōsis, keh-NO-sis) — the self-emptying that precedes genuine filling. Paul uses the verbal form of this in Philippians 2:7, where he writes that Christ *“made Himself of no reputation”* — the Greek literally reading that He emptied Himself. The invitation for every disciple is to participate in that same downward movement. Not self-destruction, not annihilation of personhood, but the radical divestiture of the constructed self — the ego-architecture we have built from achievement, wound, relationship, and reputation — so that the life of Christ might have room to expand within us. You cannot receive what cannot fit through the door, and the door that leads to genuine Kingdom identity is the narrow gate of Matthew 7:13-14, which Jesus described not as obscure but as small — deliberately and structurally small, so that nothing can be carried through it that belongs to the old self. I find myself pressing further into what I said during this conversation about prophetic calling — that there is a grief that comes with every genuine gift, proportional to the level of dependence on God that the gift will ultimately require. I want to sit with this more carefully, because it is one of the most misunderstood dynamics in charismatic and prophetic Christianity today. The Western church has largely taught that the gifts of the Spirit arrive as upgrades — as additions to an already functioning self. You were living your life, and now you also have the prophetic. You were who you were, and now you also speak in tongues. But this is not the pattern of Scripture. The pattern of Scripture is rupture before restoration, dismantling before commissioning, wilderness before ministry. Every major prophetic figure in the canon undergoes a stripping before a sending. Elijah runs from Jezebel into the wilderness of depression and exhaustion before the still small voice arrives (1 Kings 19). Jeremiah laments the day of his birth (Jeremiah 20:14-18) even as the word of the Lord burns in his bones like fire shut up in his very framework (Jeremiah 20:9). The grief that accompanies calling is not incidental. It is structural. It is the mercy of God creating within the vessel the exact internal space that the assignment will require. God does not waste pain. Every affliction in the life of a called vessel is a precision instrument — not punishment, not abandonment, but formation. Paul understood this from the inside: *“And He said to me, ‘My grace is sufficient for you, for My strength is made perfect in weakness’”* (2 Corinthians 12:9, NKJV). The word translated “sufficient” is ἀρκεῖ (arkei, ar-KAY), from ἀρκέω (arkeō) — to be enough, to ward off, to repel want. Paul is being told that the grace of God is not merely available; it is actively sufficient, actively repelling the deficit that weakness would otherwise create. But notice the sequence: the thorn comes first, the pleading comes second, and only then does the divine sufficiency become visible. You cannot witness the sufficiency of grace without first experiencing the inadequacy of self. The grief teaches you what God’s grace is actually for. I also find myself returning to what I said about the secret place — that it is the only room large enough to hold the weight of what we carry. This was said specifically in the context of my brother processing his grief about a place and a season he cannot quite let go of, and the temptation to either suppress it or to indulge it in environments that cannot actually help him. The language of the “secret place” comes from Psalm 91:1 — *“He who dwells in the secret place of the Most High shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty”* (NKJV). The Hebrew word translated “secret place” is סֵתֶר (seter, SEH-ter) — a hiding place, a shelter, a place of concealment and covering. It is the place where you are invisible to the storm while being entirely visible to God. What I was pressing upon is that human counsel, however well-intentioned, is structurally limited by the finitude and woundedness of the counselor. The bar will numb but not heal. The crowd will perform empathy but not penetrate to the root. Even the wisest friend — even a prophetic friend — gives you a word from a vessel that is itself in process. But in the סֵתֶר (SEH-ter), you bring your weight to the only One who carries His own weight effortlessly and yours simultaneously. The shadow of the Almighty is not a metaphor for emotional comfort; it is the literal posture of divine covering — the Shekinah glory that does not merely sympathize but shields. There was a moment in the conversation where I spoke about Psalm 119:105 — *“Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path”* (NKJV) — specifically making the observation that the lamp does not illuminate the entire road. It lights only the next step. Everywhere else remains dark, and you are required to trust, not to see. I want to develop this further because I believe it is a word that every believer in a season of prophetic formation needs to receive. The נֵר (ner, NARE) — the lamp — in the ancient world was not a floodlight. It was a small clay vessel holding oil, producing just enough glow for the foot to find safe ground on the immediate terrain. This is the image the psalmist reaches for in describing the word of God. God does not give you the full architectural blueprint of your life. He gives you enough light for the next faithful step, and He asks you to trust that His sovereignty extends through all the dark that you cannot currently see. This is not a deficiency in divine communication; it is a deliberate design for dependence. If you could see the entire road, you would not need Him at each step. The darkness ahead of the lamp is not the absence of God; it is the invitation to walk with Him rather than merely follow a map. Something I noticed in myself as I was speaking with my brother was the tension I described — that my gifting seems to have outpaced my personal formation in certain visible ways. I said that my daily life has not yet caught up to what God has already built on the inside of me. I want to be careful here, because this is a genuine theological and experiential reality, not a boast. Every truly called vessel lives in this tension for a season — the tension between what the Spirit has placed within and what the character has yet to fully express. David was anointed king in 1 Samuel 16 but did not sit on the throne until 2 Samuel 2 — a gap of years filled with wilderness, warfare, betrayal, temptation, caves, and psalms. The anointing preceded the throne by design, because the throne required a man forged by the years between. The Spirit deposits the gift in full; the character is developed in stages; and the weight of that gap is real, holy, and purposeful. This brought me back to the exchange about false teaching — about the prosperity gospel, the prayer transaction machine, the worship industrial complex. I had named these in the conversation as examples of the Pied Piper dynamic Jesus warned about in Matthew 7:21-23: *“Not everyone who says to Me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ shall enter the kingdom of heaven, but he who does the will of My Father in heaven.”* The Greek word translated “will” here is θέλημα (thelēma, THEL-ay-mah) — not merely desire or preference, but the settled, purposeful, covenantal intention of God. The false systems I named share a common error: they have detached the gifts and the blessings from the person of the Giver, reframing the Kingdom of God as a system of spiritual mechanics rather than a covenantal relationship with a holy Person. You can learn to operate machinery without knowing the engineer. But you cannot abide in Christ without knowing Christ. The narrow gate is not merely a theological position; it is a relational passage. And you can only pass through it by releasing the machinery you have confused with the Man. What I was reaching for when I spoke about the Kingdom being “in your midst” or “within you” (Luke 17:21, NKJV) was the recovery of a biblical concept the Western church has largely sentimentalized: Emmanuel, God with us — not as a holiday slogan, but as the operative reality of the New Covenant. When Jesus walked with His disciples, the Kingdom of God was literally present in space and time. When He breathed upon them in John 20:22, they received the down payment of that same presence as indwelling reality. The promise of John 14:23 is staggering in its density: *“If anyone loves Me, he will keep My word; and My Father will love him, and We will come to him and make Our home with him.”* The word “home” is μονή (monē, moh-NAY) — a dwelling, an abode, a place of permanent residence. The same root appears in John 14:2 when Jesus speaks of the many “mansions” in the Father’s house. The symmetry is not accidental: He goes to prepare a place for us in His Father’s house, and in the meantime, He and the Father come to take up permanent residence within us. This is not metaphor. This is the Garden of Eden restored invisibly, inwardly, in anticipation of its full physical restoration at the consummation of all things. The conversation ended practically — back to logistics, to recording schedules, to email marketing timelines and pricing structures. And I do not say that with any condescension. The sacred does not make the ordinary disappear; it transfigures it. The fact that two brothers can move fluidly between the grandeur of God and the price of gas, between the grief of calling and the mechanics of a sales funnel, is not spiritual inconsistency. It is a picture of what integrated Kingdom life actually looks like — not monasticism that withdraws from the world, but incarnational presence that carries the Kingdom into the world without being swallowed by it. The goal is not to stay in the theological register always; it is to live from it always, even when the conversation is about transmission solenoids and moped rides in Athens. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge I want to name gently but clearly is the Church’s persistent confusion between spiritual activity and spiritual formation. My brother said something worth hearing again: you cannot accept your identity through God until you shrink yourself down to the size of your actual creaturely reality before Him. Much of what passes for discipleship in Western Christianity produces spiritually busy people who have never experienced genuine smallness before God. We train people to serve, to give, to attend, to perform, and even to speak — without first training them to be undone. Isaiah’s commission came after his collapse, not before it. We must recover the sequence. The second challenge is the tendency to treat the secret place as a retreat option rather than as the irreducible center of a disciple’s life. My counsel to my brother — to bring the weight of his unresolved grief specifically to the secret place — was not a deflection. It was a theological conviction: that the human soul in genuine pain is most dangerous when it seeks resolution from environments that are structurally incapable of providing it. The bar, the crowd, the endless processing of grief with well-meaning but finite friends — these may offer temporary relief or even genuine companionship, but they cannot perform the deep surgery that only God’s word and presence can accomplish in the interior life. The Church must recover the classical discipline of solitary prayer not as spiritual elitism, but as basic soul hygiene. A third challenge emerges from the discussion of false teaching. It is not enough to name the prosperity gospel as error from a safe theological distance. The deeper issue is that many sincere believers have been so formed by transactional frameworks — prayer as leverage, worship as currency, giving as investment — that they genuinely cannot conceive of a relationship with God that does not produce immediate, measurable, and primarily material outcomes. The corrective is not merely doctrinal instruction but the kind of formation that comes only through extended seasons of unresolved waiting upon God — seasons that feel like failure but are, in fact, the deepest kind of preparation. Fourth, the conversation touched on something that rarely gets named in Christian community: the grief of calling is not a sign that something has gone wrong. It is a sign that something has gone right — that the gift is real, that the assignment is genuine, and that God is creating in the vessel the exact interior capacity the assignment will require. The Church that teaches believers to expect only triumphant, pain-free spiritual ascent will produce believers who are spiritually fragile precisely in the moments when they need to be most resilient. The thorn and the grace are not in opposition; they are partners in formation. A fifth implication: the conversation between my brother and me about AI bias — about Google Gemini refusing to return a Bible verse — is a small but revealing window into the broader cultural pressure that the saints will face with increasing intensity. The suppression of Scripture is not always dramatic. Sometimes it is algorithmic. Sometimes it is dressed in the language of copyright protection or content safety. The saints must cultivate an internal reservoir of the Word that does not depend on digital accessibility — a Word hidden in the heart, as the psalmist says (Psalm 119:11), that no guardrail can reach and no algorithm can suppress. Finally, and perhaps most tenderly: the exhaustion I named — thirty-three days without releasing an episode, weeks of sickness, a prophetic dream, a sense of imminent confrontation with what does not belong in the house of God — is not spiritual failure. It is the pattern of sabbath and preparation that recurs throughout Scripture before significant prophetic acts. Elijah slept and ate before the journey to Horeb. Jesus withdrew before and after the most demanding moments of His ministry. The extended pause before the word is often what makes the word worth giving. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity is not the accumulation of theological vocabulary or the multiplication of spiritual experiences. It is the deepening of genuine smallness before a genuinely great God — the kind of smallness that does not produce passivity but actually generates the courage to act, because it has stopped depending on its own size. Growing up in Christ means becoming increasingly comfortable with the lamp that only lights one step, increasingly trusting of the darkness that surrounds it, and increasingly confident not in the ability to see but in the faithfulness of the One who sees for us. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The call to obedience in this season is, above all, a call to return to and remain in the secret place — not as a spiritual activity among others, but as the source from which every other activity flows. For those carrying unresolved grief, unfinished assignments, or the weight of gifts that have not yet found their full expression: bring it there. Not to the bar. Not to the crowd. Not even to the most prophetically gifted friend. Bring it to the One who is the סֵתֶר (SEH-ter), the hiding place, and let Him do in the interior what nothing external can reach. **For Prophetic Posture:** Those who are being formed for prophetic function in this generation must learn to live in the tension of the anointing that has outpaced the current expression — and to regard that tension not as failure but as the very context in which character is forged. The lamp lights one step. Take it. The next step will be lit when you arrive at it. The prophetic posture is not certainty about the full road; it is fidelity to the step currently illuminated, offered in the full trust that the One who lit it knows where the path ends. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You in the name that is above every name — Jesus the Christ, the narrow gate, the lamp, the living Word, the One in whom all the shadows of Scripture find their full substance. I thank You that You are not offended by our ordinary conversations, by our practical concerns, by the sprawling and sometimes chaotic texture of our actual lives. You came and dwelt among us precisely in the ordinariness of human flesh — eating fish on the beach, working with wood in a carpenter’s shop, talking at the sides of wells — and so You are not absent from our conversations about gas prices and transmission fluid and sales funnels and career transitions. But I ask You today to deepen in us, and in all who receive this word, the capacity to hear You moving beneath the surface of everything — to perceive the eternal weight pressing through the temporal moment, to recognize the call of the secret place even in the middle of the busy ordinary. Let the grief that comes with calling produce in us not bitterness but depth. Let the lamp that lights only one step build in us not anxiety but trust. Let the smallness that we discover before Your grandeur not diminish us but free us — free us from the exhausting labor of maintaining a self that You never asked us to be, and free us into the breathtaking lightness of being exactly who You wrote us to be before the foundations of the earth. We are co-heirs, servants, watchers, prophets, and beloved children — and we make our agreement with Your will between the heavens and the earth, confident that what You have spoken of us, You will perform in us and through us, to the glory of Your name alone.
