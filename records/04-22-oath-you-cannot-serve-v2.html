# 04-22 Call Reflection: Samuel’s Oath, Spiritual Allegiance, and the Fear of the Lord **Title:** 04-22 at 09:51 — The Oath You Cannot Serve Two Masters With **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother Samuel recount what had unfolded that evening — the drinking, the oath, the Brazilian mafia, the voice notes to an ex — something in me grew very still. What presented itself on the surface as an absurd, almost comedic chain of events was, underneath it, the cry of a man genuinely coming apart at the seams, reaching for belonging in every direction except the one he already knew. The laughter in his voice did not fool me, and I told him so. Behind every joke was a brother calling out for help, and I heard him. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the story of a man stumbling into dangerous covenants while drunk is not as exceptional as it sounds — it is a parable of a condition that afflicts far more of the Body of Christ than anyone cares to name. When a believer is not abiding, when the Holy Spirit’s counsel has been dulled by carelessness, by excess, by unresolved emotional wounds and aimless evenings, the soul does not simply sit still and wait — it reaches. It reaches for brotherhood wherever brotherhood is on offer. It signs oaths. It sends messages it should not send. It looks for a table, for loyalty, for a name. And the grief in this is not that Samuel is uniquely weak; the grief is that the Church has so often failed to be the thing he was looking for in a bar that night, so that when real communion appeared — even in the form of a Brazilian crime organization apparently more serious about their bonds than most congregations — it pulled him in. The question this conversation pressed upon me was not simply *what did Samuel do wrong?* It was *what does it mean that this keeps happening?* And more urgently: what does the fear of the Lord look like for a man who knows the truth but is not yet walking in it? ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the outside looking in — through the lens of psychology, social dynamics, and human developmental theory — Samuel’s evening reads as a relatively straightforward case of a young man with unmet attachment needs, an emotionally volatile home environment, and poor impulse regulation when alcohol lowers his executive function. The “mafia oath” registers as peer pressure compounded by intoxication and novelty-seeking. The voice notes to Emily read as emotional regression under the influence. The laughter throughout the call reads as a defense mechanism — humor deployed to soften the shame of a string of self-destructive choices. And the solution, by this framework, is accountability, sobriety, perhaps therapy, a stronger support structure, clearer boundaries. These are not bad things. But they remain entirely on the surface of what was actually happening in that phone call. **The Limitation:** The natural mind can diagnose the symptoms without ever perceiving the spiritual architecture underneath them. It can see a young man making poor decisions; it cannot see a soul suspended between two covenants, unsure which one it truly belongs to. It can identify anxiety and attachment disruption; it cannot see that the oath taken in a Brazilian bar was spiritually significant not because of the organization behind it, but because it reveals the precise danger Jesus warned against when He said that a man cannot serve two masters. The flesh can observe consequences; only the Spirit can perceive the *kairos* — the appointed weight of a season — and recognize that what looks like comedic chaos is actually the beginning of a reckoning. The natural mind hears laughter and is reassured. The Spirit hears laughter and listens past it, the way I told Samuel I was doing, because laughter is sometimes the last garment a man wraps around genuine desperation. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I want to begin here, at the oath — because the moment Samuel told me he had sworn into something, I knew immediately what the Spirit was pressing on me to say, and I said it plainly: oaths are anti-biblical. Jesus addressed this directly in the Sermon on the Mount, in Matthew 5:33–37, where He said, *“But I say to you, do not swear at all: neither by heaven, for it is God’s throne; nor by the earth, for it is His footstool… But let your ‘Yes’ be ‘Yes,’ and your ‘No,’ ‘No.’ For whatever is more than these is from the evil one.”* This is not a minor etiquette preference. It is a declaration about the architecture of integrity — that a person whose inner life is transparent before God has no need of oaths, because their word already carries the weight of their character. The moment an oath becomes necessary, a division has already been exposed between what a person is and what they are promising to be. And here is the weight of it: Samuel’s oath, even if its words were Christian-adjacent, placed him in a position of divided allegiance. This is precisely the danger I tried to name in the conversation — that an oath does not simply bind you to a promise; it creates a moment in the future where you may be called upon to honor that oath in a way that conflicts with your higher allegiance to Christ. The patriarch Abraham understood this when he refused to take even a thread from the king of Sodom, lest anyone say that Sodom had made Abraham rich. Allegiance creates obligation, and obligation shapes identity. Samuel had already declared that his role was Christ-led, that he belonged to God and not to them — and this was the Holy Spirit moving even through the fog of the evening. But the oath still stood, and that tension is real. What struck me just as deeply, though, was not only the oath but the *condition* that made it possible. Samuel was drinking. And I said it plainly because it needed to be said plainly: you cannot feel the leading of the Holy Spirit much when you are drinking. I say this not as moral condemnation but as spiritual physics. The רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — the Spirit / breath / wind of God — moves in a man through the faculty of the inner man, through the renewed mind, through the sensitivity of the spirit to the things of the Spirit. Paul in Ephesians 5:18 contrasts being filled with wine with being filled with the Spirit — *“Do not be drunk with wine, in which is dissipation; but be filled with the Spirit”* — and the contrast is not merely behavioral but navigational. When the Spirit’s compass is dulled, a man navigates by whatever is loudest around him. That night, what was loudest was warmth, camaraderie, drinks, and a group of Brazilians who apparently took their brotherhood seriously. And I will say this, because I said something like it to Samuel and I believe it is genuinely true: the fact that these men appeared to be more serious about their covenant with one another than most church members are with theirs is not a rebuke of the mafia — it is an indictment of what the modern church has become. I have seen this pattern in what the Lord has been showing me about my own calling. It is often the people whom the institutional church looks past, looks down on, or has never reached that carry the deepest instinct for the things the church is supposed to embody — loyalty, shared life, real sacrifice for one another, a table that means something. The problem is not that these men valued communion. The problem, as I named it, is the *enemies*. Any fellowship that defines itself by who it excludes, who it destroys, who it stands against — that fellowship has not yet arrived at the table Jesus sets. The table Jesus prepares *in the presence of your enemies* in Psalm 23:5 is not a victory banquet over defeated foes. It is an invitation. The enemies are present at the table — they are being invited to it. That is the scandal of the Kingdom, and no criminal organization, however Christian its oath, has fully grasped that yet. And yet I found myself thinking, even as I spoke with Samuel, about the nature of true loyalty and brotherhood. He was drawn to something real. The longing underneath his evening was not pathetic — it was prophetic. He was hungry for the thing the Church of Jesus Christ is supposed to be: a people who take their covenant seriously, who do not scatter when things get serious, who dap each other up with genuine weight. The grief is that he was looking for it in a bar in Brazil. And the question the Spirit was pressing through this conversation — pressing on *me*, as the one on the other end of the line — was whether I was being that for him, and whether the community around him was being that for him. Brotherhood that is real enough to compete with a criminal oath is the thing Jesus commissioned. I also felt the weight of Samuel’s words when he said he did not know how to walk away from this group without risking his life. And I answered him the way the Spirit pressed me to answer: *It’s not your life, is it?* And he received it — he said, *“So even if I got killed on this, doesn’t mean I’m dead.”* This is a man who knows the truth. Death is a lie. Separation is the great lie — the lie that anything can happen to a believer that would finally and permanently sever him from God. Paul dismantled this lie exhaustively in Romans 8:38–39, listing every conceivable force — death, life, angels, principalities, height, depth, things present, things to come — and declaring that none of them can separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus. Samuel knows this. He said it back to me. But knowing it and *living from it* are two entirely different things, and it was the gap between those two that the whole evening revealed. This is where the fear of the Lord enters the meditation, and it enters with gravity. I said to Samuel: *Now is the time for you to learn the fear of the Lord.* The יִרְאַת יְהוָה (yir’at YHWH, yeer-AHT yeh-HOH-vah) — the fear of the LORD — is not the fear of an abusive tyrant. It is the trembling recognition that you are held by Someone whose wisdom exceeds your ability to manage your own life, and whose mercy is itself a form of severity when necessary. The minor prophet Joel — and I was drawing on his language in the conversation — wrote: *“Turn to Me with all your heart… Who knows if He will turn and relent?”* (Joel 2:12–14). There is a holy uncertainty in those words. God’s mercy is not automatic. It is not a vending machine activated by the right combination of contrition and Christian vocabulary. He is sovereign. He may choose grace abounding, or He may choose severe mercy — the kind of mercy that lets a man sit with the full weight of his consequences until he learns what he cannot learn in comfort. And I want to name what I mean by severe mercy, because it is not punishment in the punitive sense — it is the fatherly discipline of παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) — fatherly training / discipline that Hebrews 12:6–11 describes as the mark of sonship, not abandonment. *“For whom the LORD loves He chastens, and scourges every son whom He receives.”* The man who is left entirely to himself, who never faces real consequence, who is always rescued before the lesson settles — that man is not being loved; he is being managed. Samuel’s string of decisions — the PMC the day before, the mafia oath the next day, the voice notes to his ex — these are not random chaos. They are the escalating language of a man the Spirit is pressing, pressing, pressing: *You know what you’re supposed to do. Now do it.* The paradox I shared with Samuel near the end of our conversation was one the Spirit had been unfolding in me as well: we do not need to know much. We need to know enough. And what is enough? Enough is this: we do not know, of ourselves, what we are doing — but the One who commissioned us knows, and He has promised that everything genuinely required for what He has actually commissioned will come in abundance. Not the things we have added to the commission. Not the side missions. Not the oaths we swore in Brazilian bars at midnight while drunk. But the things that are genuinely His commission — those things are fully resourced from before the foundation of the world. The issue is trust. The issue is whether we are willing to abide in Him fully enough that we know what is actually commissioned and what is our own restless reaching masquerading as purpose. Samuel ended the conversation still laughing, still a little overwhelmed, asking what he had gotten himself into. And I ended it trying to point him back to the meditations I had sent — to the written words that the Spirit had already been speaking. Because that is often how it works: the word has already been given. The question is whether we will sit with it long enough for it to root. The call dropped before I could confirm he had received it. But I believe the Spirit was still speaking even in the silence at the end of the line. ## Implications and Challenges What this conversation challenges first is the comfortable fiction that discipleship can be sustained without community. Samuel was not wandering because he is uniquely rebellious — he was wandering because the hunger for real brotherhood is real and legitimate, and when the Church fails to provide it with the seriousness the soul requires, the soul will find it elsewhere. The challenge for every community of believers is not merely to offer fellowship as a program or a scheduled event, but to be a covenant people whose table actually means something — whose loyalty is costly enough to compete with an oath sworn in a Brazilian bar. Second, this meditation challenges our relationship with oaths and casual covenants. The Western church has so domesticated the Sermon on the Mount that most believers have no theology whatsoever around oath-taking. We swear on Bibles in courtrooms, sign agreements without prayer, and join organizations without asking what allegiance is being claimed. Jesus’ prohibition of oaths in Matthew 5 is not legalism — it is wisdom about the architecture of a life. Every additional oath, every secondary loyalty, every brotherhood that demands a piece of us is a thread of the garment that should belong entirely to Christ. This is not about isolation; it is about singularity of allegiance. Third, this conversation confronts the church’s silence about alcohol and spiritual sensitivity. Paul’s Ephesians 5 contrast between wine and the Spirit is not prudish — it is navigational truth. A man making significant life decisions under the influence of alcohol has handed his discernment over to his environment. This is not a call to legalistic abstinence as a badge of righteousness; it is a call to honest self-examination about when and why we reach for the thing that lowers our ability to hear the Spirit clearly. Fourth, the theology of severe mercy deserves reclamation. The modern church has largely collapsed the sovereignty of God into a vending machine of grace — insert sufficient repentance, receive immediate rescue. But Joel, Jonah, Amos, and Hebrews all bear witness to a God whose love is sometimes expressed in allowing the full weight of consequence to rest upon us until we have learned what comfort was preventing us from learning. This is not cruelty. It is the most serious form of fatherhood. And it should produce in us not fatalism but reverent, alert cooperation with the Spirit — abiding closely enough that we know what is commissioned and walk accordingly. Fifth, the table set in the presence of enemies is a prophetic image the Western church has almost entirely misread. We have turned it into a triumph narrative — God defeats our enemies, then throws us a party in front of their humiliated faces. But the deeper reading is missional: the enemies are present because they are being invited in. The table is prepared in their presence so that they might come and eat. This reframes the entire way we think about those outside the covenant — not as threats to be managed or defeated, but as guests not yet seated. Finally, this conversation is an invitation to every believer who carries a brother or sister in genuine intercession: hear past the laughter. The person laughing the hardest is sometimes the one who most needs you to stay on the line and say, *I know the degree of the situation.* Do not be fooled by the performance of composure. Love listens through it. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Real maturity means we can receive a brother’s chaos without flinching and without dismissing him — and that we speak plainly when the Spirit prompts us to speak, even if the truth is uncomfortable. *You cannot feel the Holy Spirit much when you are drinking.* That sentence could have landed as a rebuke; it landed as a lifeline because it was true and it was spoken with care. Maturity also means we have enough theology of divine sovereignty to hold the paradox: God is always with us, and God sometimes chooses severe mercy. Holding both without collapsing into either presumption or despair is the work of a lifetime. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this calls forth is simple and costly: abide. Do not fill the silence with alcohol and strangers and oaths. Do not reach for brotherhood in every room except the one where the Spirit is waiting. Walk in what you know you are called to — and trust that everything genuinely commissioned will be abundantly provided, while everything you have manufactured out of restlessness will eventually demand a price you did not budget for. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture here is one of holy sobriety — not merely in the physical sense, though that is included, but in the posture of the inner man. To prophesy, to discern, to speak kairos words into someone’s life in the middle of their chaos, requires that our own spirit be still enough to hear. We are called to be the kind of people that others can call at the end of a very strange night and find genuinely present, genuinely listening, genuinely without condemnation, and genuinely unafraid of the degree of the situation. That is a prophetic posture. That is what it looks like to prepare the table. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I bring this evening before You — the laughter and the fear underneath it, the oath sworn in a bar, the voice notes, the searching, the wild careening of a man who knows You but is not yet fully resting in You. Lord, You are not surprised. You were on that call before I was. I ask that Your severe mercy — if that is what this season requires — would be swift and purposeful, and that Samuel would find in the weight of it not condemnation but a Father’s hands. I thank You that death is a lie, that separation is a lie, that nothing in all of creation can finally and permanently cut off Your own from You. But I also ask, Father, that You would bring him to the end of his own reaching — that You would make the oaths and the organizations and the late nights hollow in his mouth until the only thing that satisfies is abiding in You. And for myself, I ask that I would be found faithful as a brother — not a distant voice quoting Scripture, but a man close enough to say *I hear past your laughter, I know the degree of this, and I am not going anywhere.* Let this conversation bear fruit that remains. Let the word that has already been sent take root in him now. And Lord, let the table You are setting — in the presence of every enemy, every gang, every lost brotherhood, every man who took an oath in the wrong room — let that table be wide enough that they all find a seat. To You be all glory, dominion, and honor, now and into the age of ages. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Matthew 5:33–37 — “But I say to you, do not swear at all…” | — | Jesus’ prohibition of oaths targets the divided inner life — the man who needs an oath is the man whose plain word is not yet trustworthy.
