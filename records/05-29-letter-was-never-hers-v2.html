# 05-29 Call Reflection: Active Waiting, Prophetic Ministry, and Covenant Brotherhood **Title:** 05-29 at 13:32 — The Letter Was Never Hers: A Mirror, A Covenant, and the Active Waiting of a Spirit-Led Life **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation (Live Call Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother Samuel pour out his heart across an ocean of grief, exhaustion, and raw honesty, something began to stir in me that was not merely sympathy — it was recognition. The Spirit was moving through the ordinary texture of that conversation: through the laughter about drive-throughs and moonshine, through the ache of a broken marriage, through a letter that was written for one person and belonged entirely to another. What arrested me most was not the weight of what my brother was carrying, but the precision with which the Spirit cut through it — quietly, without announcement, landing like a scalpel on the exact nerve that needed it. I did not plan what I said in that moment. It came from somewhere deeper than my own reasoning, and when it landed, we both felt it. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Body of Christ is full of brothers and sisters carrying mirrors they think are windows. They are writing letters to people who were never meant to receive them, looking outward at the source of their pain when the Spirit is pressing the reflection inward toward the source of their healing. The grace of active waiting — what my brother named precisely and beautifully in that call — is not passivity dressed in spiritual language. It is the posture of one who has learned that the Spirit moves through the ordinary, through the honest, through the unscripted moment of a conversation between two broken men trying to walk with God. If the Church recovered this — the sacred ordinariness of two people holding each other accountable in the Spirit, listening together until something breaks open — we would need far fewer programs and far more porches. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a natural vantage point, what happened in that conversation is easily explained: two friends venting, one processing a divorce, the other helping him process it. The letter Samuel wrote would be read by a therapist as a healthy grief exercise — emotionally intelligent, self-aware, a good step toward closure. The observations we made together about American convenience culture would register as sociological commentary, the kind that gets nodded at on podcasts. My remark about waiting for Christ’s return would be filed under religious coping mechanism, the way wounded people sometimes retreat into spirituality when the present feels unmanageable. My wife’s concern — that I had given up on life — would be understood as a reasonable relational alarm bell. All of it makes sense through the lens of psychology, sociology, and emotional self-management. The world has categories for everything we said. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind cannot perceive the architecture beneath the conversation, because it has no category for the Spirit moving through laughter about moonshine and pivoting to kairos revelation in the same breath. It cannot account for the fact that a man sat down in anguish, wrote a letter to his estranged wife, and through the simple act of reading it aloud to a brother, had it turned inside out by One who was present in the call but not listed as a participant. The natural mind would explain away the moment I told Samuel to replace Emily’s name with his own as an insightful therapeutic reframe — and miss entirely that it was not my insight. The human faculty, however emotionally sophisticated, operates within the boundary of what it can observe and deduce. But the Spirit of God — the רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — the breath / wind / Spirit who hovered over the formless void before the first word was spoken — is not constrained by what the conversation appears to be on the surface. He moves through the gaps between sentences, through the pause before a man says *dude, what the fuck*, through the moment when two men on a phone call realize that something just happened that neither of them planned. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I am still sitting with what happened on that call. Not in a mystical, untethered way — but in the way that a man sits with something that landed too precisely to be coincidental. Samuel read his letter. And before he finished, before I had offered any counsel or structure or thought, the Spirit had already told me what it was for. I was waiting to hear what Samuel would say about it first, and he said the truest thing: *I wrote it more for myself than for her.* And I knew then that what I had been given to say was confirmed, not invented. That is the nature of what I have come to understand as prophetic confirmation — it does not arrive as novelty alone, but as the agreement between what the Spirit pressed into me and what was already being pressed into the heart of the one I was speaking with. The letter, in its raw first draft, is an extraordinary document — not because it is polished, but because it is honest. Samuel wrote of a man fractured by the streets, by school, by people, finding in a woman an image of home and wholeness he had not yet become ready to steward. He wrote of love at first sight under the orange glow of a Cracker Barrel hostess stand, of a marriage that never got a fair shot, of grief too heavy for one a.m. to contain. And every word of it was true. But the Spirit said: *that is your mirror, not your message to her.* Because the love in that letter — the tenderness, the grief over the version of yourself she received, the wish that you had been more whole when you met her — that is not primarily a word for Emily. That is Christ’s word to Samuel about Samuel. It is the voice of the One who saw Samuel in the streets and in the fracture and in the move to the other end of the country, and loved him there, and is still loving him toward the man he has potential to be. This connects to something deep in the theology of the mirror in Scripture. James 1:23–24 speaks of the man who looks at his face in a mirror and immediately forgets what he looks like after he walks away. The Word of God functions as a mirror — not to condemn, but to reveal. And sometimes the Spirit uses what we write for others as the very mirror we most need to look into. Samuel’s letter to Emily was, in the grammar of the Spirit, a letter from Christ to Samuel: *I saw you before you were whole. I loved you before you were ready. I have not stopped.* The fact that Samuel could write tenderness toward someone who had caused him genuine pain is not the product of his own emotional maturity alone — he said it himself: *I don’t think I would have ever had the strength to do that. I only think that strength comes from within him.* That is the confession of a man who is beginning to understand that the καρπός (karpos, KAR-pos) — fruit — of the Spirit is not manufactured by willpower. It is grown by abiding. And this brings me to what became the theological spine of the whole conversation: active waiting. My brother named it exactly right. I had said that my posture is to wait for Christ’s return, that it is my top priority — and my wife had read that as surrender, as giving up. But I tried to explain what I meant: I gave up on the old life, not on life itself. I have actually gained the only hope I have ever had. And Samuel said it with precision: *it’s active waiting.* This phrase carries more theological weight than it might appear to. The Greek word ὑπομονή (hypomonē, hoo-pom-on-AY) is typically translated *patience* or *endurance* in the New Testament, but it does not mean passive inactivity. It is the compound of ὑπό (*hypo*, under) and μένω (*menō*, to remain, to abide) — it means *to remain under*, to stay in place under the weight of something rather than running from it. It is the endurance of a soldier who holds the line, not the withdrawal of one who has abandoned the field. The parable of the ten virgins in Matthew 25 is the image I returned to in the call. Five were wise and five were foolish, and the distinction was not in their desire or even their initial preparation — all ten had lamps, all ten went out to meet the bridegroom. The distinction was in what they carried with them into the waiting. The wise ones had oil in their vessels alongside their lamps. The oil is not incidental. In the Hebrew Scriptures, שֶׁמֶן (shemen, SHEH-men) — oil — is the substance of consecration, of anointing, of priestly preparation. The wise virgins carried the ongoing substance of communion with the Spirit into their waiting, and when the bridegroom was delayed, they were not caught empty. Active waiting is not merely patience — it is preparation through communion. It is keeping the conversation alive with God while you hold the position He has placed you in. I said something in the call that I want to sit with more carefully: that I am waiting for the Spirit to prompt me, and trusting that He will prompt me when necessary. I drew on the image of the רוּחַ (ROO-akh) hovering over the void — the same Spirit who was present before anything was made, who knows what He is doing, who does not miss cues or forget assignments. If I genuinely trust that Spirit — and I do, or I am learning to — then the right posture is not anxious striving to manufacture the next step, but attentive abiding until He speaks. This is not the same as laziness. It is the posture of Samuel the prophet as a child — *Speak, LORD, for Your servant hears* (1 Samuel 3:9, NKJV) — lying in the temple, available, still, ready to receive. It is the posture of the disciples in the upper room, waiting in Jerusalem as Jesus commanded (Acts 1:4), not knowing when the Spirit would fall, but remaining in position. My brother offered an image during the call that has stayed with me. He described walking on a beach with God holding your hand, and a coworker named Joe approaching and asking why you are walking in that direction when everyone else is playing volleyball at the ocean. Joe cannot see the hand. Joe cannot see the path. And so Joe’s question, though well-meaning, is oriented entirely toward the visible — what he can observe and measure. This is why well-meaning people who love us can ask questions that feel like accusations without intending them as such. They are not seeing what we are seeing, because the path is only revealed to the one walking it in communion with the One who set it. Proverbs 3:6 says, *In all your ways acknowledge Him, and He shall direct your paths* (NKJV). The directing is His; the acknowledging is mine. That is the covenant of active waiting. What followed in the conversation — my reflection on the pedagogical style of Jesus — is something I have been tracing in what I have been calling the green markings. Jesus teaches primarily through questions. He does not immediately correct and redirect; He first asks a question that exposes the inadequacy of the asker’s current framework. *Who told you that you were naked?* (Genesis 3:11, NKJV). *Who do you say that I am?* (Matthew 16:15, NKJV). *Why are you so fearful?* (Mark 4:40, NKJV). The Greek word for this kind of instruction is παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) — fatherly training / discipline — but it carries within it the image of the relationship between a father and a child in the ancient world, where training was not merely academic but formational, relational, embodied. Jesus does not just transfer information to His disciples; He shapes them through encounter. When I come to Him angry and He responds with *Why?*, that single word is παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) — it is not dismissal, it is formation. He is training my prophetic sense by pressing me to examine the premises beneath my frustration. This connects to what I said toward the end of the call about the Greek concept of restoring covenant brotherhood. I could not recall the specific term in the moment, but what I was reaching toward is the concept embedded in Matthew 5:23–24, where Jesus says that if you bring your gift to the altar and remember that your brother has something against you, you should leave your gift there and go be reconciled first. The word translated *reconciled* is διαλλάγηθι (diallagēthi, dee-al-LAH-gay-thee) — a compound verb that means to thoroughly change the relational standing between two parties, to move them from opposition back to accord. Jesus places this above the act of sacrifice, which is a staggering reversal of the Temple’s prioritization. The offering can wait. The brother cannot. And the reason is not merely ethical — it is theological: the Body of Christ is constituted by the quality of its internal bonds. A body with severed joints does not function. The reconciliation of believers is not a secondary concern; it is the structural integrity of the ekklesia. I told Samuel that this priestly call to reconciliation extends even toward enemies — not naively, not without wisdom, but at minimum: *offer them a seat at the table.* He said it is a hard thing, and most of us cannot. I agreed. Jesus never said it was easy. He said it was the way. And then He walked it. He offered the seat at the table to the one who would betray Him, washed his feet, broke bread with him. That is not sentimentality. That is the most costly kind of faithfulness — the kind that does not wait for the other person to deserve it. I want to return, in closing this meditation, to the image of the letter — because it has not let me go. My brother sat at one a.m., sleepless, heartbroken, writing to someone he called *mine* and *darling*, grieving a love that came at the wrong time into a version of himself that was not yet ready to receive it. And the Spirit turned the letter around and said: *this is for you.* Not to shame Samuel for having written it, but to reveal to him what he already carries — a capacity for tenderness, for self-examination, for grief that does not curdle into hatred. These are not natural products of a man who has been cheated on and lied to. These are fruits of the Spirit already at work in him. The letter is evidence of a work in progress — a work, as Philippians 1:6 says, that He who began it is faithful to complete. *Being confident of this very thing, that He who has begun a good work in you will complete it until the day of Jesus Christ* (NKJV). Samuel does not have to finish the work. He just has to keep walking with the One who is finishing it. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation presses on me — and I offer it gently but clearly — is the way modern Western Christianity has largely abandoned the practice of spiritually substantive friendship. What happened in that call was not a discipleship program or a small group curriculum. It was two men being honest with each other inside the presence of God, and the Spirit moved. The Church has professionalized its relational structures to the point where the kind of conversation Samuel and I had would be deemed too raw, too unfiltered, too theologically informal for a sanctioned setting. But Jesus built His movement on exactly this: unpolished men, honest tables, the Spirit moving through ordinary exchange. If we want the kinds of moments where a letter gets turned into a mirror, we have to cultivate the kind of friendships where letters get read aloud. The second challenge is to those of us who have confused active waiting with passive resignation — or, on the other side, confused restless striving with faithfulness. Both errors are costly. The man who sits still and calls it trust while refusing to be faithful in the small things is deceiving himself. But the man who cannot stop moving long enough to hear the Spirit’s prompt is also deceiving himself, substituting productivity for communion. The παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) of the Father involves both — seasons of pressing forward and seasons of lying still in the temple, available. Discerning which season you are in requires the very communion that active waiting cultivates. Third: the convenience culture critique that arose organically in our conversation is worth pressing into spiritually. The koinonia — κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-no-NEE-ah) — fellowship / sharing in common — that constitutes the Body of Christ is not a drive-through experience. It is not consumable on demand. American Christianity has often tried to make it so — streamlining church into content, replacing presence with broadcast, substituting comfort for formation. But κοινωνία (koy-no-NEE-ah) requires friction, proximity, inconvenience, the kind of honest exchange that cannot happen through an algorithm. The isolation I described — going an entire month without meaningful human contact, checking myself out at every station of life — is not just a cultural problem. It is a spiritual one. And the Church has largely failed to offer an alternative because it has adopted the same architecture of convenience it was meant to resist. Fourth: the theology of reconciliation that emerged in our conversation challenges a deeply embedded habit in modern Christian culture of spiritualizing avoidance. We call it boundaries. We call it wisdom. Sometimes it is. But sometimes what we name as wisdom is simply the unwillingness to bear the cost of διαλλαγή — of truly reestablishing covenant bond with someone who has wronged us or whom we have wronged. Jesus did not say *go reconcile when it feels safe.* He said *go before you make your offering.* The priority is disruptive. It requires leaving the altar. That disruption is intentional. Fifth: the moment in the call where the Spirit pressed a word through me that I had not consciously prepared — *the letter is not for Emily, it’s for you* — is a reminder that prophetic ministry does not require a stage. It requires proximity, availability, and the willingness to say what the Spirit gives rather than what is socially comfortable. Samuel needed someone in that moment who was listening at two levels simultaneously: to him, and to the Spirit speaking through him. This is the ordinary prophetic ministry of the Body of Christ — not spectacular, not performative, but precise and personal and transformative. Every member of the Body has access to this. The question is whether we are cultivating the kind of interior silence and exterior attentiveness that makes it possible. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity is not the absence of raw honesty — it is raw honesty submitted to the Spirit’s reframing. Samuel did not pretend the grief was not real. I did not pretend my wife’s concern was not real. We brought the real things into the space of the conversation, and the Spirit moved through them. Maturity means learning to bring what is actually happening into the presence of God and trustworthy brothers, and to hold it there long enough for the Spirit to show you what it is really for. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Kingdom obedience in this season, for those of us in the posture of active waiting, looks like faithfulness in the small and the unglamorous — the weld on the box, the honest conversation at midnight, the letter you don’t send, the question you let Jesus ask you without deflecting. It looks like keeping the oil in your vessel so that when the cry goes out at midnight — *the bridegroom is coming!* — your lamp is not empty. Obedience is the ongoing work of communion, not the dramatic gesture of a single decisive moment. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this call models is one of listening before speaking, of waiting for confirmation before declaring, of holding what the Spirit gives without forcing it prematurely. I waited until Samuel finished reading the letter. I had already received what the Spirit pressed into me, but I waited. And when the confirmation came from Samuel’s own mouth — *I wrote it more for myself* — I knew the word was ready to be released. This is the rhythm of the prophetic servant: receive, confirm, release. Not receive and immediately broadcast. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who knows what it is to be mid-conversation with a brother and suddenly realize that You were in the room the whole time — not as a visitor, but as the Host. I thank You that Your רוּחַ (ROO-akh) does not require a sanctuary to move, that You speak through phone calls at midnight and letters written in grief and questions You press into men who think they are just venting. I ask You to continue the work You have begun in my brother Samuel — to let the mirror of that letter do what mirrors are for, to show him the tenderness he carries as evidence of Your hand in him, to remind him that the strength to write grace toward one who hurt him could only have come from You. I ask You to anchor my own posture of active waiting in genuine ὑπομονή (hoo-pom-on-AY) — not the passivity of resignation but the faithfulness of a servant who holds the position until the Master speaks. Let me be found with oil in my vessel. Let the Body of Christ recover the sacred inconvenience of real κοινωνία (koy-no-NEE-ah), the kind that turns letters into mirrors and conversations into altars. You are faithful to finish what You begin. I agree with that Word over my brother and over myself. Let it be so. To the Father, through the Son, by the Spirit — all honor, all glory, all completion belongs to You.
