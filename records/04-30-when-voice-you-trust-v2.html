# 04-30 Call Reflection: Samuel’s Spiritual Threshold — Faith, Calling, and the Cross **Title:** 04-30 at 18:50 — When the Voice You Trust Is the Wrong One **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother Samuel walk through the streets of the UK late at night, speaking to me through broken signal and broken reasoning, I kept sensing that the real conversation was not about newsletters, Tennessee, money, or gangsters — it was about a man who has heard God’s voice and is terrified of what it would cost him to fully obey it. The tension that ran beneath every thread of this call was not theological confusion; it was the age-old collision between the voice of the Spirit and the voice of the self, between genuine encounter and premature application. What arrested me most was not what Samuel said he believed — it was the distance between what he confessed and where he was standing when he confessed it. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the kind of spiritual condition I witnessed in Samuel is not rare. It is the condition of an entire generation of men and women who have genuinely encountered God, carry real spiritual sensitivity, and yet remain suspended in a kind of perpetual threshold — hearing clearly enough to feel conviction but not yet surrendered enough to obey fully. They are close. They can articulate the things of God. They are not enemies of the cross. But they are not yet sons in full inheritance either, because the one step that would settle everything — the reception of the gift of salvation through the cross of Jesus Christ — remains somehow untaken, deferred, circled around rather than walked through. This is not a minor pastoral concern. This is the hinge on which eternity turns. And so what happened in this conversation matters not only for Samuel, but for every beloved brother or sister who has stood at exactly this threshold and found a thousand reasons to stay just outside. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind listening to this call would likely produce several compassionate but ultimately insufficient frameworks for what it heard. A psychologist might observe that Samuel is experiencing cognitive dissonance — simultaneously drawn toward financial ambition and spiritual legitimacy, unable to fully reconcile the two, and managing the tension through restlessness, late-night wandering, and a kind of manic social energy. A life coach might celebrate the newsletter breakthrough as entrepreneurial proof of concept and encourage him to follow the momentum. A secular philosopher might interpret his longing for Tennessee as a displacement of grief — the geography standing in for a relationship, a season, a version of himself that felt more whole. Even a well-meaning but shallow Christian observer might immediately validate every open door as “God’s blessing” and encourage Samuel to “step into his calling” without pausing to ask whether the foundation beneath the calling has yet been laid. Each of these readings contains a grain of something real. But they share a common failure: they cannot perceive the spiritual architecture underneath the psychological and circumstantial surface, and so they would address the symptoms while the root remains untouched. **The Limitation:** There is a particular kind of darkness that is not the absence of light but the presence of a light that has not yet been received — and the natural mind has no category for this. Samuel is not in the dark in the way a man who has never heard the gospel is in the dark. He is in the particular dimness of one who has stood close enough to the fire to feel its heat, has dreamed prophetic dreams, has sensed the drawing of the Holy Spirit, has confessed the name of Christ in a significant moment in Tennessee — and yet something fundamental has not yet been transacted between his soul and his God. The natural mind cannot diagnose this, because the natural mind measures spiritual life by sincerity, by moral effort, by spiritual curiosity, and by the presence of religious vocabulary. But the Spirit of God measures it by one thing: whether the cross of Jesus Christ has been received as the foundation. Without that transaction, all the gifts, all the open doors, all the spiritual sensitivity, all the theological conversation — these are rain falling on unbroken ground. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It Listening to Samuel, I kept returning to a word that sits at the very center of what he is navigating — the Greek κλῆσις (klēsis, KLAY-sis) — which means a divine calling or summons, a specific invitation from God into a particular role or station. Samuel is genuinely convinced that there is a κλῆσις (KLAY-sis) on his life toward wealth and marketplace influence. And I do not dismiss that. But what I kept pressing into during this call is that a calling without a foundation is not a building — it is a pile of materials that the first storm will scatter. Jesus said precisely this at the end of the Sermon on the Mount: the one who hears His words and does not do them is like a man who builds his house on sand. The house may look impressive in fair weather. The storm reveals the foundation. What I notice is that Samuel kept returning to the question of discernment — asking whether the prompting toward wealth and financial building was from God or from his own desire. And I want to be honest here: I was pressing him toward listening to his conviction, toward trusting the Holy Spirit’s check within him. And that remains true. But I am also now sitting with the deeper layer of what was actually happening in that exchange. The conviction Samuel kept feeling was not merely about the ethics of a newsletter subscription model. It was about the unfinished business between his soul and the cross. The Holy Spirit was not primarily warning him away from money — the Spirit was pressing him toward the one thing he had not yet fully surrendered to: the gift of salvation through Jesus Christ. Paul writes in Philippians 1:6 that “He who has begun a good work in you will complete it until the day of Jesus Christ.” There is something the Lord began in Samuel — something that broke open in Tennessee, something that produced a genuine confession and a real moment of encounter. But a work begun is not a work completed. And what I kept hearing underneath all of Samuel’s energy, his late-night walking, his newsletter enthusiasm, his longing for Tennessee — was the unfinished quality of that beginning. The Greek word for the repentance that would close that gap is μετάνοια (metanoia, meh-TAH-noy-ah) — a complete turning of the mind, a reorientation of the whole inner life toward God. It is not a single emotional moment. It is a settled transactional reality: the cross received, the sin acknowledged, the gift accepted, the Lordship of Christ fully embraced. What struck me most sharply in this call was Samuel’s admission: “I can’t accept the gifts.” He meant the financial opportunities, the open doors, the marketplace calling. But I heard something deeper. He cannot accept gifts because he has not yet fully accepted the gift — the one that makes all other gifts possible, the one from which all true inheritance flows. There is a Hebrew concept that speaks directly to this: the idea of בְּרָכָה (berakah, beh-rah-KAH) — blessing that flows from covenantal relationship. In the Abrahamic covenant, the blessing was never independent of the relationship. God did not offer Abraham wealth and then ask him to figure out the relationship later. The relationship was the source from which the blessing flowed. Samuel is attempting to receive the stream while the covenant connection to its source remains unsettled. No wonder the abundance feels wrong to him. His spirit knows something his mind is still arguing about. There is also something deeply significant in Samuel’s fear of the Holy Spirit — and I want to treat this with great care, because he was right to push back against those who tell him not to fear. The fear of the Lord — יִרְאַת יְהוָה (yir’at YHWH, yeer-AHT yah-WEH) — is the beginning of wisdom according to Proverbs 9:10, not the end of spiritual maturity. Every true encounter with the holiness of God produces a particular species of holy fear. Isaiah cried, “Woe is me, for I am undone!” (Isaiah 6:5). Peter fell at Jesus’ knees and said, “Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, O Lord!” (Luke 5:8). John, on Patmos, fell at the feet of the glorified Christ “as dead” (Revelation 1:17). Samuel’s fear is not a sign that he is far from God. It may actually be a sign that he has been closer than he realizes — close enough to sense the consuming holiness that lies on the other side of full surrender. What his fear needs is not to be dismissed. It needs to be consecrated, walked through, and resolved at the foot of the cross. I also cannot pass over the warning I gave about the streets. Samuel told me he felt safe walking late at night through dangerous areas, and while I honor the genuine sense of God’s presence he described, I pressed him on something that I believe the Spirit was pressing through me: do not mistake grace for license. In Joel 2:14, the prophet asks, “Who knows if He will turn and relent, and leave a blessing behind Him?” And that conditional tone matters. God had already given Samuel a prophetic dream with specific instruction. To then act contrary to that instruction and lean on the general truth of God’s protection is to treat the specific word lightly in favor of a general theological comfort. The scriptures are full of people who had genuine encounters with God and then perished — not because God’s power was absent, but because they treated His specific instruction as optional. Saul is perhaps the most painful example. The anointing was real. The calling was real. The destruction came through repeated, rationalized disobedience to specific divine instruction. When I quoted “Christos en emoi” in the middle of the call — drawing from Paul’s declaration in Galatians 2:20, “Christ in me” — I was not being clever. I was reaching for the one anchor that I knew could hold through every competing current pulling at Samuel in that moment. The phrase in full is: “It is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me.” This is not a theological slogan. It is the grammar of a life that has passed through the cross and come out the other side. The “I” that once ruled — the “I” that chases Tennessee, that tests newsletter schemes, that walks dangerous streets at midnight to prove something — that “I” has been crucified with Christ. And in its place, something new lives. Samuel has not yet fully died to his old self. That is not a condemnation. It is the diagnosis that the Great Physician has been pressing toward through every broken-signal conversation, every late-night street, every open door that feels simultaneously right and wrong. I think about Paul writing from prison — his letter to the Philippians, overflowing with joy and contentment even in chains — and I think about the arc of Paul’s language across the epistles. In his early letters, he calls himself δοῦλος (doulos, DOO-loss) — a bondservant or slave of Christ. By Ephesians and Philippians, he calls himself a prisoner of Christ. Not demoted — deepened. The joy did not diminish with the confinement. It intensified. Because Paul had arrived at the place where the external circumstance — prison, chains, trial — could not subtract from the internal reality. “I have learned, in whatever state I am, to be content” (Philippians 4:11). The word “learned” there is the Greek μεμάθηκα (memathēka, meh-MAH-thay-kah) — I have been trained, I have come through the course of instruction. Contentment is not natural to the human condition. It is forged in the classroom of suffering and surrender. Samuel is in that classroom right now, even if he cannot see it yet. And then there was the moment near the end of the call when Samuel said that his heart is yearning for Tennessee. And I spoke plainly to him: that is probably the problem. Because on the phone call that cracked something open spiritually — the Tennessee call, the one where he confessed his sins and said the only thing he wanted was to know Christ — that was the real Tennessee. The place, the geography, was just the container. But now the container has become the longing, and the content has been misplaced. This is the subtlety of idolatry that the prophets described with such aching clarity: we take what God gave us as a vehicle and begin to worship the vehicle. We fall in love with the burning bush and forget the voice. Tennessee is not the calling. Christ is the calling. Tennessee may or may not be the road. But the destination has only ever been one thing. What grieves me and also gives me hope is that Samuel’s own mother spoke into this moment — in the background, imperfect and frustrated and loving — saying essentially the same thing that I was saying from across the ocean. There was something poignant in that. The Word was not only coming through me. It was coming through the streets, through the conviction, through the broken signal, through the dream he had already received, through his mother’s voice. This is what Scripture means when it says that God speaks once, yes, twice — though man does not perceive it (Job 33:14). The Lord had been speaking to Samuel through multiple instruments simultaneously. Whether Samuel hears it is not in my hands. I can only, as I told him, be a signpost. I can point to Jesus. I cannot be Jesus for anyone. The Habakkuk passage that surfaced in my spirit during this call — “Though the fig tree may not blossom, nor fruit be on the vines… yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation” (Habakkuk 3:17–18) — is the anthem of a man who has crossed from circumstantial faith to covenantal faith. Circumstantial faith says “I trust God because things are going well.” Covenantal faith says “I trust God because He is God, regardless of what I see.” Samuel is being invited into that crossing right now. The newsletter making five hundred pounds in a week is not the sign. The conviction he feels in the midst of the blessing is the sign. God is not withholding the blessing to punish him. God is revealing that the blessing apart from the Giver will consume him. This is the mercy underneath the discomfort. The conversation ended with unresolved signal and an unresolved soul — and I am sitting with that. I am not worried, in the sense of faithless anxiety. But I am sober. Because I know that what Samuel is carrying is not a theological debate. It is a kairos moment. A crossroads. And every crossroads eventually requires a direction. He cannot stand at the intersection forever. The ground does not stay level beneath those who delay the transaction that heaven is waiting on. But I also trust this: the God who broke something open in Tennessee has not abandoned what He began. And the dream He gave Samuel was not a threat. It was an act of love from a Father who is not willing to lose His son to the streets, to money, to Tennessee, or to anything else that bears the name of this world. ## Implications and Challenges There is a particular temptation in spiritually sensitive circles to celebrate the anointing without insisting on the foundation. We can become so accustomed to identifying gifts — prophetic dreams, spiritual discernment, marketplace favor — that we affirm the manifestation while leaving the foundation question unasked. Samuel’s situation exposes this tendency clearly: a man with genuine spiritual encounters, a real prophetic dream, real financial gifts opening, and real theological vocabulary — who has not yet fully received the gift of salvation through the cross of Jesus Christ. We must be willing to name this gap without condemnation, because leaving it unnamed is the greater cruelty. Modern Western Christianity has largely made peace with the idea of a gradual, low-stakes drift toward God — as though salvation were a process of gradual self-improvement rather than a decisive transactional reality centered on the cross. But the New Testament language is consistently the language of death and resurrection, not renovation. You do not renovate the old man; you crucify him. And until that death is embraced — until μετάνοια (meh-TAH-noy-ah) is genuine and foundational — all the gifts, all the callings, all the open doors remain structurally unstable. This does not mean God is not present or not working. It means the work is not yet finished. There is also a challenge embedded in Samuel’s instinct that feeling safe in a dangerous place is spiritual confidence. This is one of the subtler distortions that genuine spiritual experience can produce: the belief that closeness to God cancels the consequences of specific disobedience to His word. Grace is real. God’s protection is real. But God already spoke through a dream, and to walk contrary to that specific word while claiming the general protection of His presence is not faith — it is presumption. The Israelites made this mistake repeatedly. They leaned on the ark of the covenant as a talisman even when the holiness of God had departed from them. The ark became a magic box rather than the dwelling place of the living God. We must not do the same with our sense of God’s nearness. The chase for happiness — and I said this directly in the call, and I want to press it further here — is one of the most elegant traps the enemy lays for men of genuine spiritual calling. Because happiness is not wrong. God does want His children to have abundant life. But happiness pursued as a destination becomes the very thing that keeps a man from the narrow way. The narrow way does not feel happy in the way the world defines happiness. It feels costly. It feels like loss — the loss of the old self, the loss of the familiar comforts, the loss of the life that the flesh would have built. And yet on the other side of that loss is what Paul calls the “peace of God, which surpasses all understanding” (Philippians 4:7). The Greek here is εἰρήνη (eirēnē, ay-RAY-nay) — not the absence of conflict, but the wholeness and completeness that comes from covenantal alignment with God. Samuel is chasing happiness. He needs to be chasing εἰρήνη (ay-RAY-nay). Finally, and I say this with great gentleness: there is a challenge to those of us who speak into brothers and sisters in these threshold moments. I told Samuel the truth. But I also must examine whether I spoke it with enough clarity about the one non-negotiable thing. It is not enough to correct doctrine and leave the cross uncentered. Every theological conversation, every rebuke, every redirection must ultimately bend toward the same answer: Christ crucified, risen, and received. That is the one corrective that cannot be substituted. I can be a signpost. But every signpost must point to the same place. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This call is a living illustration of the difference between spiritual sensitivity and spiritual foundation. Sensitivity without foundation produces a man who can feel the conviction of the Holy Spirit and still walk through dangerous streets at midnight rationalizing his safety. Maturity means learning to distinguish between the voice that flatters and the voice that sanctifies — and then choosing the voice that sanctifies even when it costs us something. For those of us walking with brothers and sisters in threshold moments, maturity also means having the courage to name the foundation question clearly rather than simply adjusting their theology around the edges. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The response this calls forth is, first and most urgently, a return to the cross as the non-negotiable center of every conversation about calling, gifting, blessing, and destiny. Before marketplace calling. Before Tennessee. Before newsletters. Before money or manifestation of any kind: Is the foundation of the cross received? Has salvation been embraced not merely as a theological position but as a lived and settled transactional reality? That is Kingdom obedience in this moment — for Samuel, and for every one of us who may have allowed the gifts to crowd out the Giver. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this moment calls for is patient, sober intercession — not anxious, but alert. I told Samuel I would pray for him, and that my intercession might change. This is what genuine prophetic intercession looks like: it is not a one-time spiritual compliment offered to make someone feel loved. It is a sustained standing before God on behalf of a brother who is close to the threshold, asking the Father of lights to complete the work He began. The prophetic posture is to watch and pray, to speak truth and then release, to point and then trust the Pointer rather than the signpost. We do not save anyone. We bear witness. And we trust the God who does not lose what He has called. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You from within the inheritance You have sealed in the blood of Your Son — not as a stranger but as a son, not as a petitioner without standing but as one whose prayers You have said You hear, a servant of the Most High God made co-heir through the finished work of Calvary. I give You thanks for this conversation, for the broken signal and the unresolved ending, because I trust that You do not lose threads. You hold every thread. I ask You, Father, by the power of the Spirit who searches the deep things of God, to complete the work You began in my brother Samuel — not the work of financial success, not the work of Tennessee, but the work of the cross, the work of death and resurrection, the work of μετάνοια (meh-TAH-noy-ah) that turns a soul from the world and plants it firmly in You. Let Your εἰρήνη (ay-RAY-nay) — the peace that passes all understanding — be the thing he cannot stop reaching for. Guard him on those streets. Honor the prophetic warning You gave him through that dream. Let every open door be tested against the foundation, and let the foundation be nothing other than Christ crucified and risen. I stand in agreement with what You have already spoken over him. I stand in agreement with Your will and Your way. Not my will but Yours be done. And Lord, keep me faithful — keep me willing to be the signpost and nothing more, to speak truth and leave the salvation to You, to love without possessing, to intercede without controlling. You are the God of all grace, the Father of lights, in whom there is no shadow of turning. Have Your way in my brother. Have Your way in me. In the matchless name of Jesus Christ, who came in the flesh, who died and is risen, and who is coming again — Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |----------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Philippians 1:6 — “He who has begun a good work in you will complete it” | ἔργον (ergon, EHR-gon) — work / deed | The good work is not merely circumstantial improvement but the deep inner transformation begun by God and guaranteed to completion by His faithfulness, not the recipient’s performance. | | Galatians 2:20 — “It is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me” | Χριστὸς ἐν ἐμοί (Christos en emoi, khree-STOS en eh-MOY) — Christ in me | This is the grammar of crucifixion-and-resurrection applied to the personal biography.
