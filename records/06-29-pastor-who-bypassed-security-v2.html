# 06-29 Personal Meditation: Intercessory Prayer, Prevenient Grace, and God’s Sovereign Pursuit of a Prodigal **Title:** 06-29 at 17:26 — The Pastor Who Bypassed Security **Recorded:** 2026-06-29 17:26:59 **Source Discernment:** A — Personal Spoken-Word Revelation **Classification:** Personal Meditation ## Opening Observation I was leaving a voicemail for one of my closest friends, and somewhere between the laughter and the longing for him to call me back, the whole story of what God did for him came spilling out of me again — and I realize it never stops being holy. A pastor walked into a hospital room uninvited, unbothered by security, carrying a word that someone had prayed for a man who was not yet ready to receive it. That word found its mark anyway. I am sitting with the weight of that — the way God moves without our permission, without our tidiness, and without our timeline. **Why This Matters:** There is a version of faith that waits for people to get cleaned up before God shows up for them, and there is the faith that Scripture actually describes — a God who sends His messengers through locked doors, into chaotic rooms, to men who are not yet worthy and may not become worthy for a long time. This matters for every intercessor who has wondered whether their prayer reached anyone, for every believer who has written off a prodigal friend as too far gone, and for every saint who has confused sanctification with the prerequisite for salvation rather than its fruit. If we misunderstand how God initiates, we will stop praying the right prayers — and we will stop trusting the strange, sovereign ways He answers them. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind looks at this story and sees a warm, encouraging coincidence — a chaplain doing his rounds, a sick man in a vulnerable moment, and a friend who happened to be praying at the right time. It files the hospital pastor’s arrival under “good timing” and notes with cautious optimism that Mikey seems to be doing a little better, going to church now, drinking a little less. It appreciates the sentiment but quietly reserves judgment, because the man is still drinking, still using, and the metrics of transformation are not yet convincing. The natural mind is not cruel in this assessment; it is simply operating within the horizon of what it can measure. It tracks behavior and waits for the numbers to change before it calls something a miracle. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind cannot perceive what actually happened in that hospital room, because what happened was not primarily medical or psychological — it was covenantal. Something was set in motion in the unseen realm before a single visitor stepped through that door, and no amount of sociological analysis of chaplaincy programs or emotional vulnerability in illness can account for a man being introduced to Jesus by a stranger who said, *someone prayed for you*. The natural mind cannot hear the voice that said *pray for his sanctification, pray that he might know Me* — and because it cannot hear that voice, it cannot understand why the answer to that prayer looks messy, why provision and protection are already flowing to a man who has not yet arrived at holiness, and why that is not a contradiction but a confirmation of grace. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I did not start by praying the obvious prayer. That is the thing I keep coming back to. Norman called me, told me Mikey was in the ER, not doing well, and my first instinct — the instinct of any loving friend — was to pray for recovery. But I paused. I went to the Spirit and I asked, *what do I pray for him?* And what came back was not a prayer for physical healing. What came back was a prayer for encounter. The voice of Jesus came down and said, *pray that he might know Me — sanctification, salvation.* I want to sit with the weight of that instruction, because it reorders everything about how I understand intercession. The Greek word for this kind of intercessory standing is not complicated, but it is profound. When Paul writes in Romans 8:26 that the Spirit Himself makes intercession for us with groanings that cannot be uttered, the word is ὑπερεντυγχάνω (hyperentynchanō, hoo-per-en-TOON-khan-oh) — a compounded word that means to meet with, to light upon, to plead on behalf of, with the prefix *hyper* indicating that the Spirit goes beyond what we ourselves are capable of articulating. What I experienced in that moment of asking was something like that — being caught up into a prayer that was larger than my own affection for my friend, a prayer that the Spirit Himself was already praying, and into which I was simply invited to agree. I did not author that intercession. I received it. The prayer I prayed was for Mikey’s σωτηρία (sōtēria, so-TAY-ree-ah) — salvation, wholeness, deliverance, the full rescue of a human being from the dominion of darkness. And the instrument God chose to begin answering it was a pastor who walked past hospital security as though it were not there. I do not want to rush past that detail. The text of Acts 12 comes alive for me here — the angel who passed through iron gates to reach Peter in prison, the gate opening of its own accord, the kind of access that belongs to a Kingdom whose messengers are not ultimately stopped by institutional barriers when God has authorized their mission. I am not saying this pastor was an angel. I am saying that God’s emissaries move in authority that human security systems were not designed to contain, and that His word will not return void simply because a door was locked. What arrests me further is the word the pastor carried. He did not walk in with a theological argument or a tract. He walked in and said, *somebody prayed for you.* That sentence is itself an act of evangelism — it tears open the veil between the invisible and the visible and makes the unseen world suddenly, undeniably present to a man lying in a hospital bed. Mikey was confronted not merely with a stranger, but with the evidence that someone who loved him had stood in the gap before God on his behalf. That is the power of intercession made known — it becomes a testimony. It says, *you were not forgotten. You were prayed for. There is a God who heard.* And here is where the theology of prevenient grace comes rushing in. Mikey did not clean himself up before any of this happened. He was in the ER — which in the context of what I know about him likely means his body was suffering the consequences of his choices. And yet it was precisely *there*, in the wreckage, that the Lord sent His word. This is the pattern of the whole canon. Joseph is in the pit before he is in the palace. The prodigal son comes to himself while he is still among the swine. The woman with the issue of blood presses through the crowd in her uncleanness, not after it. God does not wait for human readiness as a prerequisite for divine initiative. He moves *into* unreadiness, because He is the one who creates readiness by His very approach. Now Mikey is going to church twice a week. He is still drinking — though less. Still using on the side. And his business has exploded. I want to linger here because I think this combination of facts is deeply instructive about how the Kingdom actually grows in a person’s life. We want transformation to be linear, clean, and rapid. We want the sanctification to precede the blessing. But what I am watching in Mikey’s life is something far more patient and far more gracious than that — I am watching God keep His covenant with a man who is only beginning to understand that he is in a covenant. The provision is not a reward for Mikey’s obedience; it is a mercy that is meant to lead him to repentance, as Paul writes in Romans 2:4 — the kindness of God is the agent of transformation, not just its byproduct. The Hebrew concept that comes to mind is חֶסֶד (hesed, HEH-sed) — the lovingkindness, the covenant faithfulness of God that does not terminate when its object is failing. David writes about it in nearly every Psalm. It is the steadfast love that pursues, that follows hard after, that will not let go. Mikey’s exploding business in the midst of his mess is חֶסֶד (HEH-sed) in action. God is not endorsing the disorder; He is outrunning it with goodness, wooing a man whose heart is beginning to turn toward the One who already turned toward him first. I said to the recording, and I mean it with everything in me — more than anything I could wish for myself, I have watched my God move through this man. That statement is not false modesty. It is the discovery of something that intercession produces in the intercessor: a hunger for other people’s breakthrough that eclipses my own comfort and my own advancement. When I see Mikey going to church, when I hear that a pastor walked into that room and said *someone prayed for you* — I am undone in a way that I am not undone by my own answered prayers. There is something about standing in the gap for another life and then watching God honor it that produces a quality of joy and awe that I cannot manufacture for myself. It is the joy of the midwife who does not carry the child but whose hands are the first to hold it. The faithfulness I am testifying to here is not Mikey’s faithfulness — not yet, not fully. The faithfulness is God’s. The Hebrew writers had a word for this too: אֱמוּנָה (emunah, eh-moo-NAH) — steadfastness, reliability, the quality of being utterly trustworthy in one’s commitments. This is what Lamentations 3:23 declares when it says that God’s mercies are new every morning — *great is Your faithfulness*. The faithfulness being celebrated there was not Israel’s. Israel had just watched Jerusalem burn. The faithfulness being celebrated was entirely God’s, measured against the rubble of human failure. That is what I am celebrating when I celebrate what happened to Mikey. Our God is faithful to the prayers of His servants even when — perhaps *especially* when — the one being prayed for is still in the rubble. And this brings me to something I cannot escape: the prayer itself was the turning point, not the hospital visit. The hospital visit was the answer; the prayer was the hinge. When I asked the Spirit what to pray and received the instruction to pray for salvation and sanctification rather than mere recovery, something was established in the heavenly places. Ephesians 6:12 reminds us that we do not wrestle against flesh and blood — and by implication, our intercession is not merely a social act of goodwill toward a friend. It is a spiritual act that moves principalities. When I agreed with heaven’s desire for Mikey’s soul, I became a co-laborer in a cosmic drama whose stage was larger than a hospital room in Fargo. The pastor who bypassed security did not do so by force. He did so because the way had already been cleared in prayer. What strikes me most deeply as I sit with all of this is that Mikey does not fully know yet what has been purchased for him, or how it was purchased. He knows a pastor came. He may know that people were praying. But the full texture of the intercession, the exact nature of the prayer, the instruction that came from the voice of Jesus — that is still hidden from him. And yet it is operative. The Kingdom works like this. The seed goes into the ground and the farmer sleeps and rises, and the seed sprouts and grows, *he himself does not know how* — Mark 4:27. Mikey is growing. He does not yet fully know how. That is not a problem; that is the mystery of the Kingdom at work in a man’s life in real time. ## Implications and Challenges There is a quiet but pervasive habit in modern Christianity of praying the safe prayer — the prayer for comfort, for recovery, for things to stabilize — rather than asking the Spirit what to actually pray. I was confronted with that habit in the moment Norman called me, and I almost defaulted to it. The challenge this meditation issues is direct: are we asking the Spirit what to pray, or are we defaulting to what feels compassionate without consulting the One who sees the whole person, the whole future, and the whole redemptive purpose? Compassion is good. Spirit-directed intercession is better. One manages a situation; the other changes a destiny. We also carry an unexamined assumption that God’s blessing requires our moral readiness. We say it is not works-based, but when we see someone like Mikey — still using, still drinking, but going to church and thriving in business — we can feel quietly uncomfortable. We want the blessing to wait for the behavior. But the Scriptures do not support that discomfort; they confront it. The rain falls on the just and the unjust. The Father runs toward the returning son before the son has finished his prepared speech. Grace precedes deserving, always. If we cannot celebrate God’s goodness toward those who are not yet fully obedient, we may find ourselves, like the elder brother, standing outside the feast. The intercession I offered for Mikey was not public, not celebrated, and produced no immediate visible result for months. This is a word for those who have prayed and waited and seen nothing yet. The prayer I prayed in that unseen moment had already dispatched a man through a hospital’s security before I ever knew it. The answers to genuine Spirit-directed intercession often move faster in the unseen realm than we perceive in the visible one. Do not despise the hiddenness of your intercession. What was bound and loosed in prayer does not wait for your announcement to become effective. There is also a challenge here for how we categorize testimonies. I called this man’s transformation — or the beginning of it — a testimony to faithfulness. But I want to be careful and honest: Mikey is not yet fully sanctified, and pretending otherwise would not serve him or anyone who hears his story. The testimony is not *Mikey got it all together*. The testimony is *God showed up for a man in a mess and refused to leave him there*. Those are very different testimonies, and only the second one is fully honest and fully glorious. When we round our testimonies up into neat resolution, we inadvertently communicate that God only shows up after the mess is resolved. The rawer, truer testimony — God found me in the disaster and has been faithful even while I am still figuring it out — is the one that reaches people who are still in their disasters. Finally, I want to name what I said almost in passing and which deserves to be heard directly: *more than anything I could wish for myself, I have watched my God move through this man.* That sentence represents a maturity of love that the Spirit produces in genuine intercessors — the ability to find more joy in another person’s encounter with God than in one’s own advancement. This is not self-denial as martyrdom; this is the love of 1 Corinthians 13 in action. It does not seek its own. It rejoices when truth wins, even when the victory belongs to someone else’s story. This is what the Body of Christ is meant to feel when one member is reached and recovered. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity begins at the intersection of compassion and consultation — feeling the urgency to pray for someone *and* stopping to ask the Spirit how to pray before defaulting to the obvious request. It means holding the complexity of a Mikey — a man who is simultaneously messy and moving toward God — without either whitewashing his condition or abandoning him to it. It means letting the patience of God’s process reshape our impatience for resolution. **For Kingdom Obedience:** We are called to intercede — not generally, not habitually, but specifically and responsively. Kingdom obedience in this context means cultivating the kind of relationship with the Holy Spirit where we can receive directional prayer for specific people in specific crises. It also means bearing witness to what God does, telling the story faithfully so that others can locate themselves in its pattern of grace. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture here is one of watchful gratitude — watching God move in another person’s life, remembering what was prayed, and testifying to the faithfulness of the One who answered. It is the posture of a friend who does not give up on a prodigal, who keeps calling, who says *we still gotta get together, I miss you* — because the ordinary human tether of friendship is also a cord that the Holy Spirit uses to keep a man connected until the full harvest comes. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I thank You for Mikey. I thank You that You know his name, that You dispatched a servant to walk through doors that should not have opened, carrying a word that should not have found him — and yet it did, because You had already received the prayer. You instructed me to pray for his sanctification and his salvation, and I agreed with You, and You have been faithful to that agreement ever since. I ask now that You continue the work You have begun in him — that חֶסֶד (HEH-sed) would run so fast and so far ahead of his disorder that he cannot help but turn fully toward You, that the seeds planted in that hospital room would grow into a harvest that neither of us can yet imagine. I stand as one of Your servants who agreed with Your will between the heavens and the earth for this man’s soul, and I thank You that Your word does not return void. I thank You that You are the God who bypasses security to reach the ones You love. Finish what You started. Bring him all the way home. And let us both be standing in that room together, whole, laughing at the faithfulness of the God who heard a prayer in Fargo and sent a pastor through a locked door. To You be all glory, forever and ever.
