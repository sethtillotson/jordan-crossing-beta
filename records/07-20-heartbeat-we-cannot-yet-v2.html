# 07-20 Personal Meditation: Pregnancy, Covenant Love, and the Sacred Ordinary **Title:** 07-20 at 14:43 — The Heartbeat We Cannot Yet Hold **Recorded:** 2026-07-20 14:43:44 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Personal Meditation ## Opening Observation I am sitting with a phone call — ordinary in its texture, unremarkable by the world’s measure — and yet something in me will not let it pass without pausing to recognize what was actually present in it. Two people who love each other, separated by miles and obligations, speaking across the distance about a heartbeat they have not yet learned to hold in their hands. There is a tenderness here that the rushing world would simply scroll past, but I find myself arrested by it — because hidden inside the ordinary is often where the Lord does His most faithful work. **Why This Matters:** The domestic and the divine are not strangers to one another in Scripture. The God who parted seas and raised the dead also bent low to form man from dust, to open barren wombs, to notice when a woman was bleeding and had not yet been healed, to call the tired and heavy-laden to rest. If we train ourselves only to look for God in the spectacular — in the thunder, the earthquake, the fire — we will miss the still small voice that comes after, and we will miss the holy weight of a tired pregnant woman saying *I couldn’t sleep last night* and a husband driving home who says *just go to sleep, I tell you all the time.* Covenantal love is almost always quiet. And it is almost always worth meditating upon. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation Through the lens of natural reasoning, this is simply a check-in call between a married couple navigating the early-pregnancy season — practical logistics, mild medical concern over a possible UTI, affectionate banter about a cat, discussion of a Doppler device on Amazon, and the small negotiations of dinner plans. The world would file this under *slice of life* or *mundane domestic exchange* and move on without a second thought. At most, a secular observer might find it sweet, perhaps note the care and humor present in the relationship, maybe appreciate the cross-cultural warmth between two people from different worlds who have built a life together. But the world has no framework for what is actually happening beneath the surface of this conversation — no grammar for the sacredness of ordinary covenant, no eyes to see what the Spirit sees when two become one flesh and that one flesh is now carrying a third. **The Limitation:** The natural mind is trained to measure significance by scale and drama — by the size of the moment, the scope of the impact, the number of people watching. It filters out the small and the quiet almost automatically, because it cannot perceive that the small and the quiet are often the very places where Heaven is most concentrated. A galloping heartbeat heard through a Doppler, a mango craving, six vials of blood drawn to protect a life not yet born into the world — these things do not register as theological. But the Spirit sees differently. What looks like tired small talk is, to the eyes of faith, a man and a woman participating in the oldest and most sacred creative act entrusted to human beings — the bearing and tending of new life made in the image of God. That is not small. The natural mind simply lacks the sight to perceive it as large. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I listen back to this conversation, one of the things that strikes me first is the sheer faithfulness embedded in the ordinary. Gladys is exhausted — she said she didn’t sleep until three in the morning, she is possibly fighting an infection, she has had blood drawn six times over, and she is still getting up to go to work. That is not weakness. That is a picture of what Paul calls in Colossians 1:11 being *strengthened with all might, according to His glorious power, for all patience and longsuffering with joy.* The body is laboring. The spirit is enduring. The will is choosing to keep moving. I see my wife in this conversation and I am moved by how much she is carrying — not just the child within her, but the full weight of being human while a miracle is quietly happening inside her. The heartbeat conversation arrests me in a particular way. We were talking about a Doppler — a little device you can order from Amazon that lets you hear the baby’s heartbeat at home. And I said something that I want to sit with now: *every time you’re scared, you can just hear the baby’s heartbeat, and it’s perfect love.* I said that almost casually, mid-conversation, the way things come out when you are not trying to be profound. But the Spirit is pressing on it now. There is something deeply true in that instinct. Sound — the sound of a living heartbeat — as the antidote to fear. As the evidence of life. As the reassurance that what you cannot yet fully see or hold is still *there*, still *alive*, still *moving*. And I cannot help but hear in that the echo of what faith itself does. Faith, according to Hebrews 11:1, is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen. The baby is real. The baby is alive. But Gladys cannot yet hold that baby, cannot yet look into that face. All she has right now is the evidence — the heartbeat, the movement, the morning sickness, the iron being pulled from her blood. These are the *substance* and the *evidence* of a life that is present but not yet fully revealed. That is almost exactly the grammar of faith. We live in a time between promise and fulfillment, hearing the heartbeat of a Kingdom that has been inaugurated but not yet fully disclosed. The midwife said the baby was hiding — *playing hide and seek,* she laughed — and then the heartbeat came through sounding like a galloping horse. Something in me lit up at that image. A foal running. Hidden, but unmistakably alive. The prophet Zechariah speaks of the Lord riding on a colt, the foal of a donkey, coming in lowliness — not on a warhorse, not with imperial thunder, but in smallness. And yet that smallness was the very arrival of salvation. I am not forcing a typology here; I am simply noticing that the Kingdom of God has a consistent habit of hiding its greatest glories inside what looks small and unimpressive to the natural eye. The baby galloping in the womb. The King riding a foal into Jerusalem. The grain of wheat that must fall into the ground and die before it can bear much fruit. Iron. I kept coming back to the iron — *you need more iron, the baby is already eating all your iron.* In the natural sense I was thinking of nutritional deficiency, and that is real and worth tending to. But the Spirit is drawing something out of that image now. Iron in the ancient world was associated with strength, endurance, the refining fire. Daniel’s statue had feet of iron and clay — strength mixed with fragility. The Psalms speak of breaking nations with a rod of iron. But here the iron is being given away, being absorbed by new life. What the mother carries is being transferred into the child. There is a sacrificial economy at work in pregnancy that mirrors something deep in the nature of love itself — that genuine love does not merely speak from abundance; it *gives from its own reserves.* Gladys is literally pouring her strength into someone she has not yet met. That is agape before it has a face. ἀγάπη (agapē, ah-GAH-pay) — covenantal, self-giving love. The word John uses in his first letter: *God is ἀγάπη.* Not merely that God loves with agapē, but that God *is* it — that the nature of God and the nature of this particular kind of love are the same substance. When I watch my wife give her iron, her sleep, her comfort, her body to the nourishment of our child, I am watching something of the character of God made visible in the domestic. This is not sentimental romanticism. This is theology enacted in the flesh. There is a moment in the call where Gladys says she is *so hungry* but she already ate. She cannot explain it. She is full and hungry at the same time, tired and awake, uncomfortable in her own body. I recognize in that the particular wilderness of pregnancy — not quite yourself, not yet arrived, living in the *in between* of two different realities. And the wilderness has always been the place where God provides. Manna came in the wilderness. Water came from the rock in the wilderness. The children of Israel learned what their hearts truly contained in the wilderness. Deuteronomy 8:3 reminds us that God let them hunger so that He could feed them — *that He might make you know that man shall not live by bread alone, but man lives by every word that proceeds from the mouth of the Lord.* The hunger Gladys feels, I believe, is the body’s way of crying out that something new is being formed and that the ordinary provisions of yesterday are not quite enough for what is happening today. That is a spiritually honest condition. When God is doing something new in us, the old provisions often do not stretch far enough — not because God is withholding, but because He is expanding us for more. The mango. I smiled when she asked for a green mango — sour, Filipino, specific. And I said yes without hesitation. I will bring a mango home. I will make the eggplant dish. These are the small acts of covenant faithfulness that rarely make it into sermons but are absolutely present in the life of real love. Ruth gleaning in the fields for Naomi. Joseph storing grain through the fat years for the lean ones. The Shunammite woman preparing a room for Elisha — not because a prophet commanded her, but because she perceived a holy need and responded to it with concrete provision. Love in Scripture almost always has hands. It does not float in abstraction. It drives home with a mango. It makes the Udo Express because she is too tired to cook. The love that is truly ἀγάπη (ah-GAH-pay) incarnates itself into the practical, and it does so quietly, without requiring applause. I find myself also meditating on the word *tired* — how many times it appeared in this short conversation. Tired. Exhausted. Cannot sleep. Hungry. Uncomfortable. Sneezing. Possibly infected. Six vials of blood drawn. Working late anyway. There is a cost to bearing life that we do not speak of often enough in the church. We celebrate the pregnancy announcement, the baby shower, the birth. But the season between the announcement and the birth — the long stretch of discomfort, uncertainty, medical appointments, body-changes, sleepless nights — that is the hidden labor. It is not glamorous. It is not shareable in a highlight reel. And yet it is sacred. It is, in the deepest sense, the *travail* that Paul uses in Galatians 4:19 when he says *My little children, for whom I labor in birth again until Christ is formed in you.* The Greek word there is ὠδίνω (ōdinō, oh-DEE-no) — to be in the pains of childbirth. Paul uses the experience of pregnancy and labor as his metaphor for the cost of genuine spiritual formation in others. The agony is real. The tiredness is real. And the fruit is coming. The midwife’s words linger with me — *your baby is hiding, huh? Playing hide and seek.* And then the heartbeat broke through: loud, galloping, unmistakable. I see in that a rhythm I have lived in my own faith walk. There are seasons when the promise seems hidden, when I press the Doppler to my chest and hear only silence or static, when I wonder if what I believed was real is still actually there. And then — without announcement, without ceremony — the heartbeat breaks through, galloping like a young horse running through an open field, and I remember: *it was always there.* The hiding was never abandonment. The silence was never absence. Psalm 139:13 declares that God wove us together in the secret place of the womb — the very hiddenness of the womb is where the work of knitting happens. What is being formed in the dark is not being neglected; it is being *made.* I want to close this body of meditation on the simplest and most weighty thing that happened in this conversation. At the end, as Gladys was rushing to get ready for work, I said: *Okay, bye. I love you. Bye.* And she was already moving, already gone. No great oration. No profound farewell. Just *I love you* — the thing that the whole conversation had already been saying in every question, every offer of food, every concern about her health, every laugh about the cat. The words at the end were just the verbal confirmation of what the entire exchange had already been demonstrating. And I think that is how it should be with our lives before God: that when we finally speak the name of Jesus, when we finally break into conscious prayer or corporate worship, we are simply vocalizing what our whole lives — the tired mornings, the faithful ordinary days, the iron given away in love — have already been quietly declaring. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation raises is against our tendency to partition the sacred and the secular — to reserve spiritual attention for Sunday mornings and prayer meetings, and to move through domestic life as though it were spiritually inert. This conversation is a corrective. The home is not a waiting room for the real spiritual life. It *is* the spiritual life, enacted in flesh and time. The discipline of noticing God in the ordinary requires deliberate cultivation; it does not happen automatically. Second, the Western Christian imagination has been heavily shaped by a theology of the spectacular — we wait for the big move, the dramatic word, the sweeping revival. But Scripture’s pattern is consistently one of hiddenness before manifestation. The baby hidden in the womb. The seed hidden in the ground. The leaven hidden in the flour. If we cannot honor the hidden season, we will be unprepared for the revealed one, because we will not have learned the posture of faithful tending that the hidden season requires. Third, there is a word here for husbands and fathers in particular. The call I had with Gladys was not long. It was not doctrinally structured. But it was *present.* I was asking questions, listening to her answers, noticing what she needed, and moving toward her. Presence is not passive. Presence is a choice, made again and again in small moments. Ephesians 5 calls husbands to a sacrificial, Christ-like love — not love as feeling, but love as constant orientation toward the flourishing of the one entrusted to your care. A mango and a willingness to cook dinner is not a small thing. It is the enactment of a covenant. Fourth, this meditation gently confronts any tendency to undervalue the ministry of mothers — pregnant women in particular. The church must not reduce pregnancy to a biological event or a social milestone. A woman carrying a child is participating in an act of profound co-creation with God. She is being *spent* — her iron, her sleep, her body — in service of a life she has not yet fully met. That deserves to be honored with the weight it carries. Finally, there is a word here about the Doppler as spiritual metaphor. We live in a world saturated with noise, and we often mistake the absence of clear spiritual sensation for the absence of God. But the heartbeat of the Kingdom is always there. Sometimes it takes a quiet moment, a deliberate pressing in, a willingness to sit still and listen — and then the galloping breaks through. Do not mistake hiddenness for absence. Press in. The heartbeat is real. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity means developing eyes for the holy embedded in the ordinary. It means refusing to wait for the spectacular before you engage the sacred. The God who numbers the hairs of our heads is the same God who is knitting a child together in secret. A mature faith learns to hold both truths simultaneously — the vastness and the intimacy — without collapsing either one. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Kingdom obedience looks like showing up in the small, consistent, incarnational moments: driving home, bringing the mango, asking how she slept, making dinner when she is too tired. These are not distractions from Kingdom life. They *are* Kingdom life, structured into the fabric of covenant. Obedience is almost always local before it is large. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture here is one of attending — of pressing the Doppler to the life God has given you and listening for the heartbeat that is already present. It is the posture of one who has learned that God hides things not to confuse us but to grow us, and who therefore does not panic in the quiet seasons but continues faithful in the ordinary, trusting that the gallop is coming. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I thank You for the gift of ordinary days and for the grace to see them through the eyes of the Spirit rather than the eyes of familiarity. I thank You for the life growing in secret, for the heartbeat that gallops like a young horse in the hidden place, for the covenant that shows itself in mangoes and eggplant and six vials of blood and *I love you* at the end of a rushed goodbye. Lord, I ask You to sustain Gladys in this season — to strengthen her with all might according to Your glorious power, to heal any infection that has come against her body, to give her sleep that is deep and restorative, and to make the iron in her bones sufficient for what she is carrying. I ask You to protect and knit together the child You are forming in the secret place. I stand as one of Your servants, as a co-heir under the New Covenant, and I agree with Your will — that this life being formed is known to You, counted by You, and covered by the blood of Your Son. I declare that the hiding season is not the lost season, and that what You have begun You are faithful to complete. Let our home be a place where the ordinary is continually hallowed, where love puts on hands and goes to the kitchen and asks *are you sleeping* — and in doing so, reflects the face of Christ who bent low to wash feet and break bread and say *it is finished* with the quiet authority of one who had been paying attention all along. To You be glory in the home, in the womb, in the tired mornings, and in the galloping heartbeats we have not yet learned to hold.
