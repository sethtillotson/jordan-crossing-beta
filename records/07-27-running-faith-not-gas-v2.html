# 07-27 Reflection: Faith in the Ordinary — Driving on Empty **Title:** 07-27 at 12:17 — Running on Faith, Not on Gas Gauges **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I was captured here in the middle of an ordinary, chaotic morning — a cat trapped in the heat, a car falling apart mid-drive, borrowed gas money, a queasy pregnant wife, a friend in jail, and a gas gauge hovering near empty. In the swirl of it, one small sentence rose up out of my own mouth and startled me: *“I drive by faith, not by gas gauges.”* Something in that offhand joke carried more truth than I intended, and I want to sit with what the Spirit was pressing through the noise. **Why This Matters:** Most of the Christian life is not lived on the mountaintop; it is lived in the sputtering engine, the borrowed fifty dollars, the nausea, the “I don’t want to go to work but I have to go to work.” If faith cannot survive the litter-box mornings, it is not the faith of Scripture. This matters for discipleship because endurance is forged not in dramatic trials but in the relentless ordinariness of provision-by-the-day — the manna economy, where you gather only enough for today and must trust again tomorrow. Learning to see God in the mundane is the difference between a faith that performs on Sundays and a faith that drives on empty on a Monday. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation To the natural eye, this is simply a stressed household barely holding together. A married couple lives paycheck to paycheck, borrowing money through an app to make it to the next fill-up. The wife is pregnant, unwell, and overwhelmed; the husband’s car is disintegrating around him; a close friend has been jailed; and the pets are adding chaos to an already fragile scene. The worldly mind would name this precarity, perhaps counsel better budgeting, a more reliable vehicle, firmer boundaries — and would read the husband’s cheerful “it’s okay, it’s just falling apart” as denial, a coping mechanism to avoid facing how thin the margins really are. **The Limitation:** But natural reasoning can only measure the gauge; it cannot measure the God who has always specialized in the empty tank. The flesh sees scarcity and concludes that peace in scarcity must be delusion, because the flesh cannot conceive of a Provider it cannot see on the dashboard. It reads calm as avoidance because it has no category for a peace that “surpasses all understanding” (Philippians 4:7). What the worldly mind misses entirely is that God is often nearest not when the tank is full, but when the sputtering forces a man to lift his eyes past the instruments to the One who has never yet let him coast to a stop. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I listen back to this ordinary morning, I am struck first not by the crisis but by the tenderness threaded through it. My wife, sick and queasy, still stops to coo over Sassy the cat, to celebrate that she is “getting fit,” to laugh at the kitten in the sink. In the middle of borrowing money and dreading work, love keeps leaking out sideways. And I am seeing that this is exactly how the Kingdom often looks on the ground — not grand, but faithful in small tendernesses. “He who is faithful in what is least is faithful also in much” (Luke 16:10). Then comes the line that arrests me: *“I drive by faith, not by gas gauges. The engine is literally sputtering right now.”* I said it half in jest, but the Spirit will not let me leave it as a joke. It is a near-echo of Paul: “For we walk by faith, not by sight” (2 Corinthians 5:7). The Greek there for walk is περιπατέω (peripateō, peh-ree-pah-TEH-oh) — to walk around, to conduct one’s whole manner of life. Paul is not describing a heroic leap but a daily gait. And here I am, doing exactly that in a beat-up car, my whole *manner of life* structured around trusting God for the next mile. I notice the gauge itself becomes a kind of parable. The world says the gauge is the truth — when it reads empty, you stop. But faith knows there is a truth beneath the reading. This is the constant scriptural pattern: the widow of Zarephath had a handful of flour and a little oil, enough for one last meal before death, and yet the barrel did not run dry through the whole drought (1 Kings 17:14). The gauge said empty; the God of the gauge said *enough*. I am not romanticizing poverty — I am recognizing the daily bread economy Jesus taught us to pray inside of: “Give us this day our daily bread” (Matthew 6:11). Not this month’s. This day’s. Something deeper stirs when I hear my own words about the broken car: *“Did you know you have to fall apart before you get put back together?”* I said it lightly, but the Spirit is pressing it. This is the whole shape of the Gospel in a single line. The seed must fall into the ground and die before it bears much fruit (John 12:24). The outer man is decaying while the inner man is renewed day by day (2 Corinthians 4:16). I was talking about a piece of trim slapping against the car, but I was speaking prophecy over my own soul without knowing it. I am seeing that the sputtering engine and the falling-apart car are honest icons of the human vessel. Paul calls us “earthen vessels” — ὀστράκινος (ostrakinos, oss-TRAH-kee-noss), fragile clay pots — precisely so “that the excellence of the power may be of God and not of us” (2 Corinthians 4:7). A car that never broke down would tempt me to trust the car. A tank that never ran low would tempt me to trust my planning. The very fragility is the invitation. God keeps me driving on empty so that I never confuse the vessel with the Provision. I cannot ignore the shadow running under the whole morning either — my wife’s grief over George, her best friend, sitting in a jail cell for a misdemeanor, the small hope that six hundred fifty dollars might buy his release. There is a whole Kingdom theme buried here: bondage, ransom, and the longing for a friend to come home. I feel the ache of it. And I am reminded that the whole Gospel is a bail that was paid — not with silver or gold, but “with the precious blood of Christ” (1 Peter 1:18–19). The Greek λύτρον (lutron, LOO-tron) — ransom, the price of release — is what Jesus said He came to give (Mark 10:45). My wife’s instinct to pay the balance and set her friend free is a faint echo of what the Father has already done for every one of us who were locked away. I am also convicted by the honesty of the dread — hers and mine both. “I don’t wanna go work today.” “I don’t want to go work, but I have to go work.” There is no false spiritual bravado here, and I think the Spirit honors that. Even Jesus in Gethsemane did not pretend the cup was easy; He said “let this cup pass from Me” and then “nevertheless, not as I will, but as You will” (Matthew 26:39). The obedience is not the absence of reluctance. The obedience is the *nevertheless*. “We have to go work, honey” is a small Gethsemane, a yielding of the will to the necessary and the good. And through the whole scene there is a coming child — the pregnancy, the nausea, the cramps, the strange cravings for sour soup. New life is being knit together in the very body that feels least well. I am seeing the Kingdom pattern again: life comes through travail. “A woman, when she is in labor, has sorrow because her hour has come; but as soon as she has given birth to the child, she no longer remembers the anguish, for joy” (John 16:21). The sickness now is not a sign of absence; it is the price and promise of a life being formed. The Spirit is teaching me to read the discomfort as gestation, not as defeat. Finally, I am arrested by how the recording ends: *“Okay, bye! Love y’all!”* After the borrowed money, the broken car, the jailed friend, the nausea, the reluctant work — love is the last word. And I hear in that the whole verdict of heaven over an ordinary faithful morning. This is what abiding looks like when no one is watching: not perfect, not comfortable, but running on faith, tender in the chaos, and closing every hard hour with love. ## Implications and Challenges Let me hold this gently, because the Spirit’s aim here is not condemnation but sight. **First, a challenge to the prosperity distortion.** Much of modern Western Christianity quietly equates a full tank with God’s favor and an empty one with His displeasure or my failure. But Scripture never promises a full gauge; it promises daily bread and a faithful Giver. If I only feel blessed when the barrel is full, I have believed a gospel of the gauge, not the gospel of the Cross. **Second, a challenge to the tyranny of the visible.** We are trained to treat the instrument reading as final reality. Faith does not deny the reading — the engine really was sputtering — but it refuses to let the reading be the last word over a God who has been faithful through every previous empty. **Third, a word about avoidance versus peace.** I want to be honest: cheerfulness *can* be denial. So the discernment is this — am I saying “it’s okay” to avoid dealing with the car, or to declare that God is bigger than the car? The fruit tells the difference. Peace still acts, still borrows the fifty, still fills the tank, still shows up for work. Denial refuses to act. Faith works. **Fourth, a caution against spiritualizing real need.** The ransom for George is a beautiful echo of the Gospel, but he is also a real man in a real cell, and my wife’s grief is real. The Spirit does not want me to turn suffering into a tidy metaphor and skip past compassion. Both are true at once: it points to Christ *and* it demands love now. **Fifth, an invitation to see the ordinary as holy ground.** The greatest danger is not the crisis; it is the assumption that God is only present in the extraordinary. This morning — cats, bananas, litter boxes, and all — was saturated with Him. If I miss Him here, I will miss Him everywhere. And so I return to grace: none of this is a rebuke of a struggling household. It is a hallowing of one. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity is learning to read reality by two gauges at once — the honest one on the dashboard and the truer one of God’s faithfulness — and to trust the second without denying the first. It is the ability to say “the engine is sputtering” and “I always make it” in the same breath, because both are true. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience here looks like the *nevertheless* — going to the work I do not want, filling the tank with borrowed grace, showing up for the child on the way, loving the sick and the chaotic and the imprisoned. It is faithfulness in what is least, trusting that the Master is watching the small things. **For Prophetic Posture:** This trains me to watch for God in the mundane, to speak faith even when the gauge reads empty, and to hold both lament and hope without collapsing either. The prophet is not the one who only hears God in the whirlwind, but the one who hears Him in the litter-box morning and testifies, “He is here.” ## A Closing Prayer Father, I thank You that You have never once let me coast to a stop, though I have driven on empty more times than I can count. As Your servant and Your son, an heir of Your Kingdom, I make agreement with Your Will over my little household: that You are the God of the barrel that does not run dry and the tank that carries me one more mile. I bring You my wife in her sickness and the child being formed in travail — knit that little one together, sustain her body, and let joy swallow the anguish. I lift George in his cell before You, the God who pays every ransom, and I ask for mercy and freedom according to Your wisdom. I surrender the falling-apart car and the falling-apart places in me, believing that You take apart only to put back together in Christ. Teach me to walk by faith and not by gauges, to work when I do not want to, and to end every hard hour the way this one ended — with love. In the name of Jesus Christ, who came in the flesh, who paid my bail with His own blood, and who is putting all things back together.
