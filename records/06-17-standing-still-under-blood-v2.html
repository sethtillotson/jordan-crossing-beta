# 06-17 Call Reflection: Spiritual Warfare, Addiction, and Abiding in Christ **Title:** 06-17 at 12:35 — Standing Still Under the Blood in the War Behind the Ordinary **Recorded:** 2026-06-17 12:35:03 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened through this call, I noticed how quickly ordinary life became a doorway into spiritual discernment. Business strategy, substance use, fatigue, temptation, phone static, demonic fear, and the authority of Christ all appeared in one living thread. What arrests me is not merely that spiritual warfare was discussed, but that the conversation kept circling back to one question: whose voice is speaking, and under whose authority am I standing? **Why This Matters:** This matters because discipleship is not lived in abstract theological categories; it is lived in lunch breaks, work rhythms, cravings, sales calls, family rooms, tired mornings, old patterns, and unseen pressures. If I only recognize spiritual warfare when it looks dramatic, I may miss the quieter battle where identity is formed or fractured. The Body of Christ needs discernment that is neither paranoid nor naive, neither theatrical nor dismissive. We need to learn how to see the Lord in the mundane, resist the enemy in the hidden places, and stand in the authority of Jesus without becoming proud, reckless, or fear-driven. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind could interpret this call as a mixture of entrepreneurial ambition, recovery language, stress management, friendship, and religious imagination. A worldly reading might say that the conversation moved from business scaling to sobriety struggles, then into fear responses, trauma patterns, and symbolic explanations for unexplained experiences. It might explain the talk of spiritual warfare as a way of giving meaning to anxiety, addiction, family history, and disturbing events. It may see the brother’s experience of being grabbed or sensing evil as psychological stress, environmental fear, or the mind trying to make sense of uncertainty. **The Limitation:** There is mercy in noticing psychological, emotional, and practical realities, because human beings are embodied and our nervous systems do matter. But the limitation of the natural frame is that it cannot properly weigh the unseen without reducing it either to fantasy or pathology. Scripture never teaches me to ignore the body, but it also never permits me to pretend the spiritual realm is unreal. The worldly mind may identify patterns, triggers, and coping mechanisms, but it cannot discern the voice behind a lie, the authority behind a rebuke, or the blood-bought identity that causes fear to lose its legal claim. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with this call, I am seeing that the battlefield is often disguised as normalcy. It is not only in the dramatic moment when a brother describes a door moving, a presence touching him, or evil pressing into a room. It is also in the first thought upon waking, the subtle voice that says, “I am too tired,” “I cannot obey today,” “I need this to function,” or “I am alone in this.” The Spirit is pressing on me again: before I agree with a thought, I must ask whose voice it is. Jesus said, “My sheep hear My voice, and I know them, and they follow Me” (John 10:27, NKJV). That means there are other voices, and the sheep must learn recognition. The issue is not merely whether a sentence appears inside my mind, but whether that sentence bears the tone, truth, and fruit of the Shepherd. The enemy can dress accusation in the clothing of realism. The flesh can dress laziness in the clothing of exhaustion. Fear can dress unbelief in the clothing of caution. But the voice of Christ leads into truth, obedience, life, and communion with the Father. I am also seeing that spiritual warfare has many layers. Sometimes a spirit may be actively present and oppressive. Sometimes the battle is not an immediate presence but an old pattern, a wound, a lie, or a trained reflex that was planted long ago and now operates almost automatically. That does not make the battle less spiritual. Paul says we are to bring “every thought into captivity to the obedience of Christ” (2 Corinthians 10:5, NKJV). The battlefield is not only outside of me; it is also in the architecture of agreement within me. This is where I feel a holy correction. If I only rebuke external darkness but never let the Lord renew my mind, I may be shouting at a battlefield while preserving the enemy’s old roads through my inner life. Romans 12:2 says, “be transformed by the renewing of your mind” (NKJV). The Greek word νοῦς (nous, noos) — mind / understanding — carries the sense of perception, comprehension, and moral reasoning. The Lord is not merely changing my behavior; He is changing the faculty by which I interpret reality. He is training me to perceive from union with Christ instead of from fear, appetite, trauma, or self-protection. This becomes especially sobering when addiction enters the conversation. I heard the language of progress, restraint, compromise, justification, usefulness, and tension. I know that terrain. I know the difference between something that once enslaved me and something I am tempted to call manageable because it appears to help me function. The Spirit does not deal with me by shallow slogans. He searches motives. He asks whether a thing is serving obedience or becoming a substitute comfort, whether I am practicing liberty or negotiating with bondage. Paul writes, “All things are lawful for me, but all things are not helpful. All things are lawful for me, but I will not be brought under the power of any” (1 Corinthians 6:12, NKJV). That phrase “brought under the power” exposes the real question. The question is not only, “Can I do this?” but “Is this mastering me?” The Greek ἐξουσία (exousia, ex-oo-SEE-ah) — authority / right / delegated power — helps me feel the weight of it. In Christ I have been brought under His ἐξουσία, not the authority of cravings, not the authority of old coping mechanisms, not the authority of productivity, not the authority of fear. Yet I must be careful not to turn conviction into condemnation. There was real testimony in the call: alcohol being cut off, cigarettes and vaping being left behind, the movement toward greater self-control. These things matter. The Lord often sanctifies in layers, and I should honor every true movement toward freedom without pretending partial freedom is the same as full surrender. Grace celebrates progress, but grace also keeps inviting the whole man onto the altar. When my brother spoke about being pressed in the spirit to get things done, I recognized something important. There is a difference between being driven and being led. The enemy drives with pressure, panic, scarcity, and accusation. The Holy Spirit leads with conviction, clarity, urgency, and peace. The Hebrew רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — Spirit / breath / wind — reminds me that God’s movement is living breath, not mechanical force. When the רוּחַ (ROO-akh) of God moves, He may bring urgency, but He does not need to imitate torment to produce obedience. The conversation also exposed how easily the Church can become embarrassed by the warfare Scripture plainly describes. A brother can speak of evil, oppression, or unseen attack, and a room full of respectable religious people may look at him as though he is unstable. But Jesus cast out demons. The apostles confronted unclean spirits. Paul warned that we do not wrestle against flesh and blood, “but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this age” (Ephesians 6:12, NKJV). A Christianity that has no category for darkness will often have no authority against it. At the same time, I sense another correction: having a category for darkness does not mean becoming fascinated with darkness. The center is not demons, manifestations, generational patterns, or pagan objects. The center is Christ crucified, risen, enthroned, and returning. Even when occult objects, idols, charms, or false worship appear, the issue is not that they rival God. They do not. The issue is whether people have entered agreements with lies and whether fear has magnified what Christ has already defeated. This is why the image of the golden calf came alive in the conversation. Israel built a visible substitute while Moses was on the mountain receiving the commandments. Exodus 32 is not merely about ancient idolatry; it reveals the human craving for a manageable god when the living God feels hidden. Idols are often formed in waiting. When people cannot endure holy delay, they craft something they can control. The modern golden calf may be money, substances, productivity, spiritual theatrics, comfort, sexuality, reputation, or even ministry success. Anything can become a calf if I ask it to stabilize me apart from the presence of God. And yet, standing under the blood changes everything. I said in the call that when Abba looks at me, He sees His Son. That is not poetic exaggeration; it is covenantal reality. “There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus” (Romans 8:1, NKJV). The enemy traffics in accusation because accusation seeks to separate me from confidence before God. But if I am in Christ, my standing is not built on my emotional intensity, my spiritual gifting, or my ability to identify demons. My standing is built on the blood of the Lamb. Revelation says, “And they overcame him by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony” (Revelation 12:11, NKJV). The blood is not a religious metaphor only; it is the legal and spiritual ground of victory. The Greek μαρτυρία (martyria, mar-too-REE-ah) — testimony / witness — shows me that testimony is not merely telling stories; it is bearing witness in a courtroom sense. When I testify to what Christ has done, I am not hyping myself up. I am agreeing with heaven’s verdict over against the accuser’s claim. This is also why fear loses its throne when identity becomes settled. “Be not afraid” is not sentimental encouragement; it is a command rooted in revelation. If I know who I am in Christ, then darkness does not get to define the room. The presence of evil may be real, but it is not ultimate. The enemy may threaten, accuse, imitate, or intimidate, but he cannot dethrone the Son. My confidence is not that I am strong in myself, but that I am hidden in the One to whom every knee must bow. Still, authority must never be handled casually. In Acts 19, the sons of Sceva tried to use the name of Jesus as a technique, saying, “We exorcise you by the Jesus whom Paul preaches” (Acts 19:13, NKJV). The evil spirit answered, “Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are you?” (Acts 19:15, NKJV). That question is terrifying if I am performing authority without abiding. The name of Jesus is not a spell. It is covenant access, relational union, and surrendered participation in His reign. So when I spoke about rebuking darkness, I felt the need to anchor it in intimacy. Authority flows from abiding. Jesus said, “Abide in Me, and I in you” (John 15:4, NKJV). The Greek μένω (menō, MEN-oh) — to remain / abide / dwell — is not a momentary emotional high. It is staying, continuing, dwelling, remaining in living union. The one who remains in Christ does not need to manufacture spiritual aggression. He stands, speaks truth, obeys, and lets the authority of Christ govern the atmosphere. This is why fasting and prayer came into the reflection. When Jesus taught that certain kinds do not go out except by prayer and fasting, He was not giving a mechanical formula; He was exposing dependence. Fasting crucifies the illusion that appetite is lord. Prayer crucifies the illusion that I can act apart from communion. Together they train the servant of God to stop reacting from flesh and start moving from union. The issue is not performance; it is nearness. As I consider the whole call, I keep returning to the phone microphone being cleaned. It was such a simple, almost humorous moment, but it became an image. For a long time, communication had been distorted because debris had gathered in the place meant to receive and transmit sound. How much of my spiritual life is like that? The Lord may be speaking, but old residue, neglected maintenance, and accumulated grit can make everything sound muffled. Sometimes discernment begins with cleaning the receiver. And that leads me back to the question of voice. The war is not only about resisting obvious evil; it is about becoming clean enough, quiet enough, surrendered enough, and Scripture-formed enough to recognize the Shepherd quickly. I do not want to spend my life shouting because I never cleaned the place of hearing. I do not want to mistake my own echo for the Spirit, or the enemy’s whisper for my own identity. I want my inner man tuned to Christ. The Spirit is revealing that real warfare is both more ordinary and more holy than many imagine. It is in addictions, cravings, ambition, fatigue, household atmospheres, inherited patterns, false worship, and fear. But it is also in worship, truth, repentance, fasting, prayer, testimony, and the blood. I am not called to panic. I am called to stand. And standing is not passivity. Ephesians 6 says, “having done all, to stand” (NKJV). Standing means I refuse to abandon my place in Christ. I refuse the accusation. I refuse the counterfeit comfort. I refuse the fear of man that mocks spiritual reality. I refuse the pride that uses spiritual language without intimacy. I stand under the blood, in the name of Jesus, with Scripture in my mouth, love in my heart, and the fear of the Lord above every other fear. ## Implications and Challenges Grace teaches me to honor partial victories without baptizing unfinished surrender. When a brother has left alcohol, cigarettes, or vaping behind, I should rejoice sincerely. But grace also asks whether the remaining dependencies are being brought into the light. The goal is not to shame one another, but to love one another toward full freedom in Christ. This challenges a modern Christian habit of treating spiritual warfare as either superstition or spectacle. Some dismiss everything unseen as psychological. Others become obsessed with demons and manifestations. Scripture gives a better way: sober discernment, Christ-centered authority, and obedient abiding. It also challenges the Western desire for a faith that remains respectable. Many believers are comfortable speaking about stress, trauma, and habits, but uncomfortable speaking about evil spirits, territorial darkness, or demonic oppression. Yet the ministry of Jesus does not allow me to erase the unseen realm to preserve cultural credibility. At the same time, this word challenges reckless spiritual confidence. The name of Jesus is not a tool for the unsubmitted ego. If I want to walk in authority, I must walk in communion. The sons of Sceva warn me that spiritual language without relational union can become dangerous imitation. This meditation also confronts the way productivity can become a false justification for compromise. If something helps me perform, that does not automatically mean it is holy. The deeper question is whether it strengthens obedience, preserves love, and remains submitted to Christ’s lordship. Finally, grace calls the Body into patient discernment with one another. We must neither mock a wounded brother nor flatter him into delusion. We listen, test, pray, anchor in Scripture, and point one another back to Jesus, who alone is Lord over the seen and unseen. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This calls me to examine the voices I agree with, especially the ones that sound like my own. Maturity means learning to distinguish conviction from accusation, leading from driving, liberty from bondage, and authority from presumption. It means letting Christ renew my νοῦς (noos) until my perception becomes obedient to Him. **For Kingdom Obedience:** This calls us to bring every habit, craving, ambition, fear, and spiritual experience under the lordship of Jesus. We do not obey darkness, appetite, or cultural embarrassment. We obey Christ. We repent where needed, stand where needed, fast and pray where needed, and speak truth where lies have occupied ground. **For Prophetic Posture:** This trains the saints to watch without panic, discern without pride, and testify without fear. The prophetic posture is not fascination with demons, but agreement with heaven. We abide in Christ, listen for the Shepherd’s voice, expose false agreements, and stand as witnesses under the blood of the Lamb. ## A Closing Prayer Father, in the name of Jesus Christ, I thank You that You have not left us blind in the war behind the ordinary. I stand before You as Your son, a co-heir with Christ, covered by the blood of the Lamb, and I agree with Your will between heaven and earth. Teach me to recognize the voice of the Shepherd and to reject every counterfeit voice that comes through fear, appetite, accusation, pride, or old patterns. Clean the receiver of my heart, renew my mind, and train my hands for obedience without making me reckless or proud. I ask You to strengthen my brothers and sisters in the Body, especially those battling addiction, fear, oppression, and confusion. Let us walk as Your servants, Your witnesses, and Your prophets in the earth, not by performance, not by hype, but by abiding union with Jesus. Let every false agreement be broken, every hidden idol be exposed, every demonic intimidation be silenced under the authority of Christ, and every weary saint be restored to courage. All glory belongs to You, Father, through Jesus Christ our Lord, by the power of the Holy Spirit.
