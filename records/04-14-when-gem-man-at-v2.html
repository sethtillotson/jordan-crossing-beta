# 04-14 Conversation Reflection: Kingdom Encounter, Testimony, and Discipleship Through the Margins **Title:** 04-14 at 12:30 — When the Gem Is the Man at the Bus Stop **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Conversation Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother Samuel recount his day, something arrested me — not the new phone, not the espresso martinis, not the clever contract trade — but the almost offhanded way he said it: *“The cool part was lunch with Thomas.”* He had done something he had never done before, and he knew it mattered more than everything else that day. And then, listening to him, I found my own memory rising — the drive into Ybor City, Christmas with no family, a homeless man all cleaned up from a stranger’s kindness, and the beach he hadn’t seen in years. The Spirit was doing something in that call, weaving two testimonies together across two continents into one thread of Kingdom revelation. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ is not a theological society where we merely exchange doctrinal positions and sharpen our arguments. It is a fellowship of the *encountered* — those who have been touched, transformed, undone, and sent. When Samuel sat across a lunch table from Thomas, something ancient was activated in the economy of the Kingdom. When I drove into a neighborhood that the comfortable avoid on Christmas Day, something was at work that no seminary class could have produced. These are not feel-good stories about charity. These are the precise moments where the ecclesia breaks out of its own walls and becomes what Jesus actually described — a city set on a hill, salt rubbed into a wound, a light that cannot be hidden. The question this conversation presses upon me is whether I truly believe that the encounter with the broken and the outcast is not an optional add-on to discipleship, but its very substance. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation Through the lens of natural reasoning, this conversation looks like two young men catching up — one celebrating a financially savvy phone deal, a spontaneous act of generosity toward a homeless stranger, and a couple of well-earned drinks; the other sharing a memory from Florida and musing on spiritual calling. The natural mind would likely identify the lunch with Thomas as an admirable act of social compassion, praiseworthy in a humanitarian sense, the sort of thing that builds community resilience and addresses urban poverty one meal at a time. It would commend the self-awareness in Seth’s reflection about shifting his priorities — perhaps interpreting it as a healthy psychological reorientation, a man learning to invest in meaningful experiences rather than chasing external achievement. Iron sharpening iron would be read as a useful metaphor for peer accountability. And the whole exchange would be filed under *good conversation between friends who share a faith tradition*, warm and relatable, but ultimately in the realm of ordinary human connection and benevolent social impulse. **The Limitation:** But that reading leaves out the One who sent Samuel to the bus stop in the first place. It leaves out why the bus didn’t come, why Thomas was standing there at that particular moment, why a young man who by his own admission had never done this before suddenly stood up and said *“Mate, are you sleeping rough?”* — and then *sat down* with him. Natural reasoning has no category for divine appointment. It cannot perceive the invisible choreography that places two people in the same moment, opens a mouth, softens a heart, and makes a meal into a sacrament of presence. The flesh reads coincidence where the Spirit reads orchestration. It reads sentiment where the Spirit reads sending. And it reads personal growth where the Spirit reads the advance of the Kingdom through consecrated and available vessels. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I am sitting with what happened in that conversation, and I am seeing something that I want to name carefully. When Samuel said *“That wasn’t even the cool part — the cool part was lunch with Thomas,”* he was, perhaps without fully knowing it, recapitulating an ancient pattern. There is a moment in Luke 10 when the seventy return to Jesus, electrified by their own ministry, saying that even the demons were subject to them in His name. And Jesus does not dismiss the miracles — but He redirects: *“Do not rejoice in this, that the spirits are subject to you, but rather rejoice because your names are written in heaven”* (Luke 10:20, NKJV). The reorientation Jesus demands is always toward the deeper thing, the truer gem. Samuel found the gem, and he named it himself. That is not a small thing. What strikes me further is the name of the man at the bus stop. Thomas. I do not wish to project beyond what the transcript supports, but I notice that Thomas is the name of the disciple most remembered for his struggle with belief apart from encounter. *“Unless I see in His hands the print of the nails, and put my finger into the print of the nails, and put my hand into His side, I will not believe”* (John 20:25, NKJV). Thomas wanted direct experience. He was not satisfied with testimony alone. And the Thomas at the bus stop — unnamed in the Kingdom’s ledger to most of the world — was sitting in his own wilderness, having been turned away from a shelter, having reached out and found the friends not there. Into that precise moment of isolation and disappointment, God sent Samuel. Whether this Thomas will come to belief, I do not know. But I know that the Lord’s table was set in the presence of need, and a seat was offered, and the name of Jesus was spoken over it. This connects me to a pattern that runs all through the Gospels. Jesus consistently approaches people in the margins of public life — the tax collector up in the sycamore tree, the woman at the well at the wrong hour of the day, the leper outside the gate, the demoniac in the tombs. The disciples likely had theological conversations about all of them afterward. But Jesus sat with them first. He ate with them. He asked for water. He said *“Come down — I must stay at your house today”* (Luke 19:5, NKJV). There is something in the physicality of shared presence — the table, the meal, the eye contact — that carries weight no sermon can fully replicate. When Samuel sat down with Thomas for lunch, he was doing something the Kingdom does through flesh and bone. Then I think about what I shared from my own memory: Christmas in Tampa, the drive into Ybor City, the man who had just been shown love by an elderly couple that morning — showered, shaved, clothed — and was now being offered a beach trip and ice cream in the afternoon. What strikes me now, sitting with it again, is the layering. One couple covered his morning. We covered his afternoon. Neither party knew the other. Neither planned the coordination. The man experienced, in a single day, what the early church in Acts 2 and 4 was described as producing together — a community where *“nor was there anyone among them who lacked”* (Acts 4:34, NKJV). Except this was not a community doing it. It was strangers, moved independently by the same Spirit, arriving at the same man on the same day. That is the invisible economy of the Kingdom operating outside institutional walls. The conversation then moved into something I have been wrestling with — the question of testimony versus argument. I said to Samuel: *if I try to convince you with theology and doctrine, you can out-argue me or simply choose not to believe. But if I tell you what I lived, you cannot tell me I didn’t live it.* And the passage that rose in me was the one about the man born blind in John 9. When the Pharisees dragged him in for questioning about Jesus, pressing him for theological classification of the One who had healed him, he simply said: *“One thing I know: that though I was blind, now I see”* (John 9:25, NKJV). The Greek word for *know* there is οἶδα (oida, OY-dah) — a perfect-tense verb meaning not just that he presently knows, but that he has come to know and the knowing stands. It is settled, experiential, irreversible knowledge. He was not offering a theological system. He was offering a testimony — and they could not refute it. They sent him away in fury, not in triumph. Fury is what powerlessness looks like wearing an authoritative mask. Samuel pressed this further with a gesture I found remarkably precise. He held his hands at two different heights — *if you read scripture and it hits here, but if you’ve had the encounter it hits here.* And the faith with which you preach is calibrated accordingly. This echoes something the Apostle Paul describes in 1 Corinthians 2 — that his preaching did not come with *“persuasive words of human wisdom, but in demonstration of the Spirit and of power, that your faith should not be in the wisdom of men but in the power of God”* (1 Corinthians 2:4–5, NKJV). The Greek word translated *demonstration* is ἀπόδειξις (apodeixis, ah-po-DAY-xis) — a term meaning proof, decisive evidence, visible demonstration. Paul’s argument was not built on the cleverness of his rhetoric. It was built on what God had done through him and in him. People recognized the weight. Samuel is right: something in the human soul — even the unregenerate soul — can sense when someone is preaching from encounter rather than from mere information. I told Samuel plainly: I went through a season of academic theology. My eschatology, my exegesis, my doctrinal categories — all of it was solid. But it did not transform the interior of my life. Knowledge without encounter is what James describes as the faith of demons — *“You believe that there is one God. You do well. Even the demons believe — and tremble!”* (James 2:19, NKJV). The demons have perfect orthodoxy. They know exactly who Jesus is. What they do not have is surrender, encounter, love, or covenant. And so right doctrine without lived encounter with the living God produces, at best, impressive articulation — not transformation, not the image of Christ being formed within, not the κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-no-NEE-ah) that is the actual heartbeat of the Body. What transforms is described in what I shared next — and I want to sit here a moment, because I do not say it lightly. I spoke of looking into the eyes of purifying blue flame. I am not elaborating beyond what I said. But I will say this: the biblical witness is consistent that the fire of God is not merely destructive — it is *refining*. Malachi 3:2–3 speaks of the Lord as a refiner’s fire who will sit and purify the sons of Levi, purging them like gold and silver, that they may offer *“an offering in righteousness”* (Malachi 3:3, NKJV). The Hebrew word for refine there is זָקַק (zaqaq, zah-KAHK) — to strain, to distill, to purge away the dross. And the seat of the Refiner matters: He *sits*. This is not a passing flame. This is patient, methodical, intimate fire. When I described feeling that fire go into the wicked parts of the soul that I did not fully know were there — and burning them away — I was describing zaqaq. And that cannot be replicated by study alone. The conversation then turned to iron sharpening iron — both Samuel and I circling Proverbs 27:17: *“As iron sharpens iron, so a man sharpens the countenance of his friend”* (NKJV). And I want to name what we both acknowledged: that sharpening requires pressure, friction, heat. It is not comfortable. The edges of iron do not become keen through gentle proximity. Something is actually removed from one blade in the process of sharpening the other. And that means that real fellowship — Kingdom brotherhood — will sometimes produce discomfort, challenge, even temporary pain. Samuel received what I said to him about still striving with grace, without offense, because he has settled something in himself about what this kind of brotherhood is for. That is maturity. That is the mark of someone who has begun to understand that the community of Christ is not primarily a comfort station but a refinery. Samuel said something I have not stopped hearing: *“You would be striving further from God had you always been comfortable.”* And this is the wilderness principle. Israel’s wilderness was not punishment alone — it was formation. Deuteronomy 8:2 says: *“And you shall remember that the LORD your God led you all the way these forty years in the wilderness, to humble you and test you, to know what was in your heart, whether you would keep His commandments or not”* (NKJV). The wilderness reveals what is in the heart by removing every external comfort that ordinarily obscures it. This is what God is always doing — not because He delights in suffering, but because He is after the real man inside the comfortable one. And that man can only be revealed, refined, and restored through the process that removes the scaffolding. I want to close the meditation body with where Samuel went next — the admission that today, with all its dimensions, was *a win*. And I do not dismiss his joy. But I do want to say something about what a Kingdom win actually looks like: it starts with Thomas at a bus stop and ends in a name written down somewhere that matters forever. The phone deal, the martinis — these are passing things. But the chat with Thomas about Jesus? That has the texture of the eternal. And Samuel knew it. He named it himself. That is the Spirit at work in a man who is paying attention. ## Implications and Challenges Let me begin with this: both Samuel and I are men in process. I say what follows not from a high place but from a common one — standing in the same need of grace that I am about to name. The first implication the Spirit presses through this conversation is that the Church has often confused *education in the faith* with *transformation by the Spirit*. We build curricula, complete courses, earn certifications in biblical studies, and emerge articulate but sometimes unchanged in the deep interior. This is not a polemic against theological training — proper study is essential and irreplaceable. But training that never drives a person to the actual encounter with God, to the fire that zaqaq-refines, to the experience that produces irreversible οἶδα-knowing, will produce at best a well-informed Christian who can argue but cannot testify. The Body of Christ in this generation urgently needs both the word and the encounter — and it must be honest about which one it has been prioritizing. The second implication is about where we locate the mission field. Samuel walked past Thomas at a bus stop and *stopped*. Most of us — and I include myself — walk past Thomas every day. We have built our spiritual lives into carefully managed schedules of church attendance, small groups, devotional apps, and worship playlists, all of which are good things in themselves, but which can collectively insulate us from the divine appointments happening in the streets outside. The Kingdom moves through available people, not primarily through busy ones. The question I am asking myself is whether I am available to the interruption of the Holy Spirit or only to the schedule I have already constructed. Third — and this connects to the iron sharpening iron we discussed — the modern Western Christian culture has largely privatized discipleship. We expect growth to happen in solitude, through personal devotions, without the grinding friction of real community that sees us and challenges us. But the sharpening of Proverbs 27:17 is inherently relational. You cannot be sharpened by a blade you never meet. And the sharpening is not always pleasant conversation — sometimes it is the friend who names what you are still striving in. The willingness to receive that word without offense, as Samuel demonstrated, is itself a mark of the Spirit’s work and a discipline that must be cultivated. Fourth, there is the question of testimony versus argument as the primary currency of Kingdom witness. We have spent enormous cultural energy making Christianity intellectually defensible — and that work has its place. But the man born blind did not defend a theological position. He defended a life that had been changed. Our apologetics, our debates, our social media arguments about doctrine — none of them carry the irreversible weight of *“I was blind, and now I see.”* This does not mean we abandon truth-speaking. It means we recognize that the most persuasive thing we carry is not the sharpness of our argument but the authenticity of our transformation. Finally, I want to name something about comfort and calling. I said in the conversation that placing my whole life in God’s hands looks from the outside like retreat — like a religious withdrawal into Bible reading and podcast-making. And yes, there is something in me that still notices how that looks to others. But Samuel said it plainly: had I always been comfortable, I would never have found God in the way I have. The process of dispossession — of releasing control, of trusting what I cannot yet see — is not a failure of ambition. It is an act of faith in a God who has shown Himself faithful in the wilderness seasons I have already walked. The California trip, the AI sales, the future plans — *if it is not in God’s will, it isn’t going to happen.* I said that, and I meant it. And I am choosing to mean it again, today, as I write this. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Real maturity is not the accumulation of correct doctrine — it is the progressive conformity of the inner life to the One whom the doctrine describes. As I grow, I must regularly ask: is my knowledge of Scripture outpacing my encounter with the God Scripture reveals? Am I articulate about transformation while remaining personally untransformed in the areas that most need the refining fire? Maturity means I no longer fear the fire — I seek it. It means I can receive a word of sharpening from a brother without defensiveness, and offer one without pride. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience looks like doing what Samuel did — stopping at a bus stop, asking a hard question, sitting down for lunch with someone the city has rendered invisible, and speaking the name of Jesus over shared food. It looks like driving into Ybor City on Christmas because someone needs to celebrate with somebody, and we are the somebody available. It looks like releasing the grip on plans and outcomes, trusting the unseen economy of a God who sends people into divine appointments that their calendars never scheduled. Kingdom obedience is almost always inconvenient, almost always unplanned, and always worth it. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this conversation calls for is one of *available attentiveness* — eyes open to Thomas at the bus stop, ears tuned to the Voice that says *“stop here, turn here, sit here,”* and a life sufficiently disentangled from worldly striving that there is room to respond. The testimony is the weapon, and the testimony is forged in the encounter, and the encounter requires availability. To abide in Christ is not passive — it is the active posture of one who has decided that whatever He initiates is more important than whatever I was already doing. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I thank You — not with the thin gratitude of someone who does not know what You have done, but with the thankfulness of a man who has stood in the wilderness, who has looked into the eyes of purifying flame, who has sat across a lunch table with someone the world has counted as nothing and seen a son of Heaven looking back. I make agreement with Your will today: that the Thomases of this world are not background characters in a story about our comfort — they are the story. They are the ones for whom You left the ninety-nine. I receive the sharpening that comes through brotherhood, the formation that comes through the wilderness, and the fire that comes through encounter. I ask You to keep us from the comfort that would insulate us from the appointment, from the knowledge that would substitute for transformation, and from the argument that would crowd out the testimony. Let us be men and women who know — with the settled, irreversible οἶδα-knowing of the one born blind — that once we were blind, and now we see. Let every Thomas we encounter have the chance to sit at the table You set before us, and hear the Name spoken over the bread. You are the Lord who choreographs the bus that does not come so that a young man named Samuel would still be standing at the stop when Thomas arrived. We are grateful. We are Yours. Let the Kingdom come through us in ways we did not plan and could not have manufactured. To the Father, through the Son, by the Spirit — forever and ever.
