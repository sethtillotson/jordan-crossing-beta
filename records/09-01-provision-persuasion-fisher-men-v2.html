# 09-01 Reflection: Sales Calls, Persuasion, and God's Provision **Title:** 09-01 at 07:21 — Provision, Persuasion, and the Fisher of Men: One Morning's Call **Recorded:** 2026-09-01 (segments spanning 07:21 – 07:59) **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection --- ## ⚡ Opening Observation Across a single morning of overlapping sales calls with my brothers Samuel and Jaden — and a young prospect named Cooper on the line — the Spirit pressed several questions into me at once: how do we persuade a soul without violating it, where does true sufficiency come from, why did peace arrive before provision, and how does the enduring mercy of God break through even a dropped connection? What began as ordinary business became a mirror of the human heart and the character of the God in whose image these men were made. The holy and the logistical were not in separate rooms; they were the same breath. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Kingdom of God does not wait for sanctified settings to make its approach. The world of selling is one of the most honest mirrors we have for the human heart, because it lays bare how we treat one another when something is wanted from us. My brother Samuel said plainly that a salesperson should not be convincing somebody to buy but demonstrating where the value is; Jaden testified with real grief about a program that stripped people of the little they had; and both men entered rest before any deal closed. An entire generation is being catechized daily by voices that preach a gospel of self-belief, and the same tools of persuasion the world has perfected as weapons are constantly tempting those of us who carry the gospel. The Body of Christ is either sanctified in the details of its speech and its trust — down to the small words nobody else would notice — or it is not sanctified at all. And a young man named Cooper, who claims no spirituality, was watching how covenant brothers actually treat one another. --- ## 🌍 The Worldly Mind's Interpretation Read naturally, this morning is simply competent sales work, and shrewd. Two experienced men bring a new recruit into their agency, build rapport, demonstrate a custom platform, dangle recurring commissions and a trip to Paris, and differentiate themselves from a predatory competitor to build trust. Jaden's account of being fleeced of eleven thousand pounds reads as smart positioning; nothing sells your integrity like exposing a rival's fraud. A behavioral coach would say the team managed their state well under financial pressure, that their calm confidence closed deals, and that Cooper's fear of failure was correctly diagnosed as self-doubt and remedied with self-belief — "the best people who perform in sales are the ones who just believe they could." The talk of prayer, of "God's perfect timing," of rent landing "to the exact pound," would be filed under motivational framework, coincidence, and healthy team culture. It is competent, human, and entirely explicable without any reference to God. **The Limitation:** But the natural reading can name the mechanics of trust while remaining completely deaf to why trust is sacred in the first place. It can observe that ethical selling works better without asking why the human soul recoils from being manipulated, why Jaden still carries a wound years later, why "strip away everything they have" landed with the weight of a moral crime. It hears self-doubt and prescribes self-confidence, never suspecting that Cooper's ache — "I'm just not as good as I thought I would be" — might be the beginning of wisdom rather than the enemy to be conquered. And it cannot explain why the peace came *before* the proof: if calm were merely a performance strategy, it would still be anchored in the expectation of a result, yet these men rested when they had no evidence at all. The flesh sees a team; it cannot see a temple. It sees a technique; it cannot see that a person made in the image of God was being treated either as a resource to be harvested or as a soul to be reverenced. --- ## ✝️ What the Spirit Reveals Through It I keep returning to the two hours before the close, when Samuel said to me plainly, "Now I could be all stressed and worried about this, but it just literally wouldn't do any good." And then he simply stopped. He did not white-knuckle his way toward the deal; he laid it down. When his voice comes back after the close, there is an almost disbelieving gratitude — "that couldn't have gone any better." The provision confirmed the peace; it did not create it. Jaden said the same from his side: "before I jumped on that call I just had peace, bro. I had peace. I had joy knowing that dad is going to deliver." Two men, separately, resting in the dark. That is not the way anxiety normally releases. Ordinarily we are only relieved *after* the good news. But Samuel entered the shalom while the outcome was still hidden, which means the peace was not a reaction to circumstances at all — it was a posture toward the Father. The word to Israel through Isaiah opens over this: "In quietness and confidence shall be your strength" (Isaiah 30:15), and the tragedy of the same verse, "But you would not." Israel was offered rest as strength and refused it, choosing the frantic help of Egypt instead. What I witnessed was the opposite of that refusal — men choosing the quietness first, then watching the strength arrive. The rent came "to the exact pound," and I heard myself telling Samuel of George Mueller, who prayed down to the penny and simply waited. The Hebrew underneath the refrain is שָׁלוֹם (shalom, shah-LOME) — not merely the absence of conflict but wholeness, completeness, the state in which nothing is missing and nothing is broken. When Samuel refused to stress, he was not suppressing worry; he was stepping into שָׁלוֹם (shah-LOME), a wholeness the empty bank account had no power to contradict. And this is the deep thing: it is not a feeling produced by good news but a gift that precedes the news, because it flows from covenant, not circumstance. Jaden deepened it further, telling us he has stopped addressing God formally. "I haven't been calling him God. I haven't been calling him like Elohim and Yahweh. It's just... Dad." I answered, "Yeah, Abba Father," almost reflexively. אַבָּא (Abba, AH-bah) is the Aramaic word of intimate address, the very word Jesus prayed in Gethsemane: "Abba, Father, all things are possible for You" (Mark 14:36). Paul says the Spirit Himself teaches us to cry it — "you received the Spirit of adoption by whom we cry out, 'Abba, Father'" (Romans 8:15). Jaden did not learn this from a book. The Spirit of adoption was teaching a young salesman to say *Dad*, and out of that intimacy came his peace before the call. Jesus grounds His entire teaching against anxiety in fatherhood: "your heavenly Father knows that you need all these things. But seek first the kingdom of God... and all these things shall be added to you" (Matthew 6:32–33). The Greek προστίθημι (prostithēmi, pros-TITH-ay-mee) — to add, to place alongside — tells me provision is never the main thing; it is placed alongside the kingdom. We chased the kingdom in that two-hour conversation, and the sale was added. But the Spirit will not let me stay only in celebration, because there was a tension I raised on purpose. I told Samuel I heard something in the sales language I could not let pass: "There's absolutely no reason to believe that you can't make four thousand dollars in thirty days." My counsel was small but, I believe, Spirit-given — reframe it as a question. "Based on what you've seen, do you believe you could make that in that time?" When the promise comes out of our mouth as a guarantee and the result does not come, they hold contempt for *us*; but when it comes as their own honest answer to an honest question, we have not manufactured anything false. "Kind of like how God frames things in questions," I said. He asked Adam, "Where are you?" He asked Elijah, "What are you doing here?" The God who knows all things still questions us, because a question honors the freedom of the one being asked. This is the same wound Jaden carried from his own past. "I was dumb as hell, bro. Like I paid like eleven and a half k." He described the tactic without flinching: a person says they only have five thousand, and the answer comes back, "well, okay, you can't afford this, but how much could you really invest right now?" He almost pleaded into the phone, "the person clearly doesn't have the money for it, why are you trying to strip away everything they have?" His outrage is not really about money. He is grieved that people were *deceived* — that the marketing seemed genuine until you were inside and discovered a machine for extraction. The wound is the discovery that he was seen as prey. The first echo that opens over this is the serpent in the garden. The oldest sale in Scripture was a lie dressed as value: "You will not surely die" (Genesis 3:4). The enemy did not force Eve; he *demonstrated* a false value and let her strip herself of everything she had. Every manipulation since Eden works the same way, corrupting the will through the eyes. The word that gathers this for me is πείθω (peithō, PAY-tho) — to persuade, to win over — a morally neutral word that lives in the borderland between honest persuasion and manipulation. Paul says, "knowing therefore the terror of the Lord, we persuade men" (2 Corinthians 5:11) — holy πείθω (PAY-tho). But the same root's shadow is what he renounces: preaching "not with persuasive words of human wisdom" (1 Corinthians 2:4). The difference is never in the smoothness of the words; it is in whether you seek the other person's good or their surrender. So Paul opens his heart: "our exhortation did not come from error or uncleanness, nor was it in deceit" (1 Thessalonians 2:3), "nor did we use flattering words... nor a cloak for covetousness" (2:5). His word for deceit is δόλος (dolos, DOH-los) — bait, cunning, the guile a fisherman uses to hide the hook inside the worm. Jaden's old program was pure δόλος (DOH-los). And Jesus took that fishing image and redeemed it: "Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men" (Matthew 4:19) — the only fishing where the fish is set free rather than consumed. Then, at the tail of another call, the eternal question slipped in — Jaden's, almost offhand: "Do you have any like spiritual beliefs?" It stopped me, because in the middle of teaching a young man that his sufficiency lies within himself, the question of what he actually worships broke the surface. And then it was gone; the frame had no room for it. But I do not believe it was accidental. Cooper had just been asked to name his deepest fear and, fumbling, confessed: "I'm actually like get out there, and I'm just not as good as I thought I would be at it." That is not a sales objection. That is the confession of a soul. Samuel diagnosed it with real perception — "that sounds less like you're doubting others and more like you're doubting yourself" — and then prescribed exactly the wrong medicine, not from cruelty but because he only has one bottle on the shelf: "the biggest step you have to take is having that self-confidence." There is a Greek word that unlocks this. "Not that we are sufficient of ourselves to think of anything as being from ourselves, but our sufficiency is from God" (2 Corinthians 3:5). The word is ἱκανότης (hikanotēs, hik-an-OT-ace) — adequacy, competence, being enough for the task. Paul takes the exact question Cooper wrestled — *am I good enough, do I have what it takes* — and answers with a flat denial and a redirection. The world says your sufficiency is latent within you, waiting to be believed into existence. The Spirit says your adequacy was never yours; it is a gift received, not a muscle flexed. Peter on the water had the energy and the motivation, but his eyes on himself sank him — "Lord, save me!" (Matthew 14:30). Moses protested, "I am slow of speech," and God answered not with motivation but with "I will be with you" (Exodus 4:11-12). Gideon, the least, hears the same: "Surely I will be with you" (Judges 6:16). The answer to inadequacy in Scripture is never self-belief. And the very doubt they tried to eliminate may have been the door: "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 5:3). Paul learned to boast in exactly what the call was trying to abolish — "when I am weak, then am I strong" (2 Corinthians 12:9-10). Here the Kingdom mystery uncovers itself: the gospel is the one offer in the universe that requires the buyer's free, uncoerced consent, because what is purchased is love, and love cannot be extracted under pressure. "Whosoever will" (Revelation 22:17) is the entire posture of heaven. God, who could override every will in an instant, instead stands and knocks — "Behold, I stand at the door, and knock" (Revelation 3:20). Jesus persuaded relentlessly, yet He let the rich young ruler walk away sorrowful rather than close him with a discount (Mark 10:22). He grieved the loss instead of pursuing the sale. And the honesty and the provision are one seamless garment: the same God who provides the rent to the pound will not have us purchase provision with a subtly deceptive word. Proverbs warns, "A false balance is an abomination to the Lord, but a just weight is His delight" (Proverbs 11:1). The just weight belongs in our promises, not only our prices. The temptation to overpromise is, at root, a failure of faith — a quiet suspicion that the truth alone will not close. But if He gives to the exact pound, we are free to speak to the exact truth. There is a warning threaded through this for those of us who carry the gospel. Peter foretold that "by covetousness shall they with feigned words make merchandise of you" (2 Peter 2:3) — ἐμπορεύομαι (emporeuomai, em-po-ROO-om-ai), to traffic in, to trade for profit. That is precisely what Jaden's old program did to the desperate. The temptation to make merchandise of people is not out there in the sales world alone; it lives at the door of every ministry that measures itself in numbers. As I listen back, I hear the Spirit examining me, not them. I build platforms; I help present offers; I stand in the place of persuasion for a living. The question is not whether I persuade but whether my persuasion seeks the other man's true good or my own gain dressed as his good. And I am convicted elsewhere too. On the call I confessed I need the reimbursement "so my wife can stop worrying. She's always the one to worry." I said it lightly, but if the peace-before-provision is true, then it is true in my marriage, and I cannot leave her to carry the worry I have learned to lay down. To be present in the room where a false gospel of self was preached, and to add only a welcome — proximity is not neutrality. Ezekiel was made a watchman precisely so that he would speak, not merely observe (Ezekiel 33:6). And when the call ended with "God bless" — was that a hollow formula or a genuine intercession? The third commandment forbids taking the Lord's name in vain, and vanity is not only cursing but emptiness (Exodus 20:7). I want that blessing to be the latter. What settles on me is that Samuel's instinct to *be there* for every new rep, and Jaden's testimony that having a mentor on his first calls "gave him peace of mind," are shadows of true discipleship. The Kingdom way of bringing someone in is never to throw them onto the calls alone — it is presence, the elder standing beside the younger, ready to "pass it" or take it back. There is something Christlike in that safety net, an echo of the One who said "I am with you always" (Matthew 28:20). And Cooper, who "said he was not spiritual at all," was somehow in the room for all of it — the prayers, the thanksgiving, the correction, the laughter. This is the real opportunity: not to preach at him, but to let him witness what a genuine κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-noh-NEE-ah) — fellowship, common life, sharing — actually looks like. The κοινωνία (koy-noh-NEE-ah) of Acts 2, where they had all things in common, is not a meeting; it is a shared life where a man's rent is my concern and my brother's commission is his brother's joy. Cooper is watching a household, not a sales floor. Samuel named it exactly: "All we needed was a team Bible study." The close was a fruit, but as I said back, the Bible study itself was a fruit. The men did not study the Bible in order to close deals; the abiding produced both. "Abide in Me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself... no more can you, unless you abide in Me" (John 15:4). The world says lock in, grind, perform — and even Samuel reached for that language, "we just need to sit down and lock the fuck in." But Jaden corrected him beautifully: "It's not even that, bro. It's really just like you just need to just put it in God's hands." The younger brother preaching abiding to the elder is the Body working as it should. And the whiteboard undoes me — Jaden turned his camera to show what he had written: "God is my origin. God is my dad. Whatever is true of my father is true of me. You either work for something or you simply receive it." We are κληρονόμοι (klēronomoi, klay-ron-OM-oy), "heirs of God and joint heirs with Christ" (Romans 8:17), and an heir does not earn the inheritance; he receives it by birth. Later in the morning my brother Samuel read the whole of Psalm 118 aloud without stopping, correcting himself as he went — "the right hand of the Lord doeth violently. Sorry, valiantly" — pressing on through his own stumbles. He read "I shall not die, but live and declare the works of the Lord" (Psalm 118:17) over a connection that would, moments later, drop entirely. The refrain, "his mercy endureth forever," translates חֶסֶד (chesed, KHEH-sed) — covenant loyalty, steadfast lovingkindness, the love that has bound itself by oath and cannot walk away. When Jaden confessed "God is our Dad, and He does love us," he was confessing חֶסֶד (KHEH-sed) — not a mood in God but a covenant in God, the kind of love that keeps a man through "all of everything, just in the last few weeks." And the psalm's cry, "Save now, I beseech thee, O Lord," is הוֹשִׁיעָה נָּא (hoshi'ah na, ho-shee-AH nah) — the very word the crowds shouted at Christ's entry: Hosanna. This psalm reaches back to Passover, the last hymn Jesus plausibly sang before Gethsemane, and forward to Palm Sunday and the cross, gathering the whole drama of redemption — and my brother read it over a bad connection, unhurried, as though nothing were unusual about handling the crown jewels of the covenant during a scheduling call. The psalm is not a private meditation but a summons to a congregation: "Let Israel now say... let the house of Aaron now say... let them now that fear the Lord say... that his mercy endureth forever." Three times the same confession is called out of three groups, and here on this call were several brothers being drawn into one shared word. Samuel read it; Jayden confessed the Father's love; I answered with my own marveling. Without any of us planning it, we became the antiphonal chorus the psalm was written to gather. And "the stone which the builders refused is become the head stone of the corner" (Psalm 118:22) — Jesus takes this onto His own lips (Matthew 21:42), Peter preaches it before the Sanhedrin (Acts 4:11) and writes us into it as "living stones." What men discarded, God made the foundation. This is the deep grammar of the Kingdom: rejection is not the end of the story. Jayden prayed something the modern religious mind rarely prays: "I'm not asking that you would give us pretty much everything, but just bless us with opportunities... and see how you work within those opportunities." That is the prayer of a son who trusts the Father's heart more than a guaranteed outcome — "it is better to trust in the Lord than to put confidence in man" (Psalm 118:8). He asks for the door, not the whole house. And the same psalm that sings of enemies compassing about like bees also sings "the Lord hath chastened me sore, but he hath not given me over unto death" (Psalm 118:18). The Father who defends His son also disciplines His son, and both are חֶסֶד (KHEH-sed). This is what makes chastening bearable — the hand that corrects is a Father's hand, and it will never give us over unto death. Something in me had to repent as I sat with my own words. I confessed "I marvel at his glory... it's enough to write a book about," and my brother answered "and I did" — and then the connection dropped: "oh, and they're gone. Cool. I love internet." I heard the flicker of frustration in myself, that small irritation when the holy moment is interrupted. But the psalm rebukes me gently: this too is the day the Lord hath made. The dropped call is not the enemy of the worship; it is the tabernacle in which the worship is offered. I want to stop dividing my life into sacred moments and interrupted ones. And I was kept — not by my own strength, but "the Lord helped me." The only fitting response is the psalm's own, doubled as if once were not enough: "Thou art my God, and I will praise thee: thou art my God, I will exalt thee." My brother closed with the treasure: "we have riches and the treasures in heaven in His glory in Christ Jesus" — spoken not from a mountaintop but from the wreckage of a failed call. For the Body of Christ, this whole morning is a mirror held up to how thoroughly the world has borrowed our vocabulary while gutting its content. "God bless" was said. Spiritual belief was asked about. But the message preached with conviction was often a gospel of self, and I fear many of God's own children have been quietly catechized by it inside the church — that faith is a kind of self-confidence, that doubt is failure, that abundant life is measured in results and the adrenaline of the first win. The brethren need to hear that our confidence is *toward God through Christ* (2 Corinthians 3:4), never toward ourselves; that a soul won by manipulation is won to the manipulator, not to Christ; and that the way of *whosoever will* cannot be reconciled with the way of *how much could you really invest right now*. We are not built to be believed alone; we are living stones, set next to one another, bearing weight together, resting on the same Cornerstone the builders refused. --- ## 🤔 Implications and Challenges **The reordering of peace and provision confronts our outcome-worship.** Most of us have quietly built a faith that praises God retroactively — we thank Him once the deal closes, the diagnosis clears, the rent lands. But Samuel entered rest saying "I don't know how it's going to happen, but it's going to happen," and that rest was intact while the account was still empty. Isaiah's "in quietness and confidence shall be your strength" (Isaiah 30:15) is an indictment of every faith that waits for evidence before it will rest. The challenge is brutally simple: can you rest tonight, before you know? If your peace requires the proof, it is not yet שָׁלוֹם (shah-LOME). **Honest speech in our vocation is a holiness issue, not merely a technique.** "There's absolutely no reason to believe you can't make four thousand dollars in thirty days" is a sentence that manufactures hope we cannot guarantee. Reframing it as an honest question is not sales polish; it is refusing to bear false witness (Exodus 20:16) and refusing the δόλος (DOH-los) of bait around a hook. Much of modern evangelism has quietly adopted the sales funnel as its model — we measure decisions, engineer emotional peaks, apply gentle pressure at the altar, and call the results fruit. But Jaden's grief exposes the other side of manipulation: the person eventually discovers they were harvested, and the betrayal poisons the very thing that was sold. The just weight of Proverbs 11:1 must live in our promises, not only our prices, or the church risks producing converts who will one day feel about it the way Jaden feels about that program. **The self-help gospel must be confronted where it has colonized faith.** These men preach with conviction that a person's deepest problem is insufficient self-belief, and the modern Western Christian has too often baptized this and called it faith. Scripture confronts it directly: "our sufficiency is from God" (2 Corinthians 3:5), not from mustered-up confidence. When Cooper spoke a true word about himself — "I'm just not as good as I thought I would be" — the immediate reflex of everyone present was to talk him out of it, yet the Lord Himself calls poverty of spirit blessed (Matthew 5:3). We must learn to let a brother sit in his honest inadequacy long enough for grace to find him there. The doubt is not always the enemy; sometimes it is the doorway. **The Father-language of a young man exposes our formal, distant prayers, and challenges our romanticizing of the hard sell.** Jaden stopped saying Elohim and Yahweh and started saying "Dad," and out of that intimacy came peace before the call; the Spirit of adoption *cries* אַבָּא (AH-bah) (Romans 8:15), and to refuse the intimacy is to refuse the Spirit's own instruction. At the same time, we sometimes admire the pushy evangelist as courageous when he may simply be overriding wills the way the world does. Yet Jesus let the rich young ruler leave, grieving rather than pursuing (Mark 10:22), because He honored the man's freedom more than His own success rate. Boldness in the Kingdom is the courage to speak the whole truth plainly, not the aggression to refuse a no. **Witness by shared life confronts our program-driven evangelism, and proximity is not neutrality.** Cooper, who claims no spirituality, was never argued into anything; he simply watched brothers carry each other's burdens — real κοινωνία (koy-noh-NEE-ah), not the ninety-minute outline. The Acts 2 church did not primarily explain the common life; it *had* one, and the Lord added to their number. But I was on that call too, and I said "God bless," and I must reckon that to be present where a false gospel is preached and add only a welcome is to risk complicity in the formation of a soul (Ezekiel 33:6). We must not let holy words become the decorative punctuation of unholy frames, nor take the Lord's name in the vanity of emptiness (Exodus 20:7). **The "lock in and grind" reflex must be corrected even inside faith communities, and grace received must reach home.** Samuel, a faithful brother, still reached for "we just need to lock the fuck in," and it took the younger Jaden to say, "put it in God's hands." This is the pull of the flesh even in devout men — to make abiding into another performance — and John 15:4 severs that root: no branch bears fruit by striving, only by remaining. I feel the edge of it personally, because I cannot preach peace-before-provision to my brothers while leaving my wife as the family's designated worrier. Faith that ends at the sales call and never reaches the kitchen table is not yet faith. Begin here with grace: most who do this mean well — but the way of *whosoever will* still calls us higher. --- ## 🚀 What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity is learning to enter שָׁלוֹם (shah-LOME) before the outcome is known, and to persuade the way God persuades — with relentless demonstration of value and absolute reverence for the other person's will. We stop measuring our conversations by whether we got the yes and start measuring them by whether we sought the other's true good. We learn a mature relationship with our own inadequacy, neither wallowing in it nor fleeing into self-confidence, but carrying it to the cross where alone it is answered, knowing that ἱκανότης (hik-an-OT-ace) is received, not achieved. The mature saint would rather grieve a genuine no than manufacture a hollow yes, and would rather rest in the dark than white-knuckle the daylight. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience here is concrete and unglamorous: guard the tongue in the marketplace, reframe a guaranteed promise into an honest question, and renounce every manipulative tool, even the ones that work. It means refusing to make merchandise of the people we are sent to gather, structuring our witness so that integrity is the foundation and not the marketing, and trusting that the God who provides to the exact pound does not need us to shade the truth to survive. It means carrying one another's burdens as literally as Samuel carried Jaden's commission — a κοινωνία (koy-noh-NEE-ah) where another's provision is our joy — and never throwing a new believer onto the calls alone. And it means letting the peace reach home, so my wife rests too. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture trained here is antiphonal and unhurried — the willingness to say aloud "his mercy endureth forever" and wait for a brother to answer, to read the whole psalm even while stumbling, to declare the works of the Lord even as the connection fails. It watches how souls are handled in the marketplace, the pulpit, and the family, discerning the difference between the fisher of men who sets free and the trafficker who consumes. It waits for the moment the eternal question surfaces in unexpected places — the dropped call, the sales script, the offhand "do you have any spiritual beliefs" — and is ready to give it an answer the frame itself could not hold. Like our Lord at the young ruler's departure, we testify plainly and then leave the door of the will to the One who alone should hold its key, trusting that *whosoever will* is enough, because it is God's own chosen way. --- ## 🔥 A Closing Prayer Abba, Father — and I say it deliberately as Jaden taught me again, not Elohim from a distance but Dad up close — I thank You that the peace came before the provision, that You taught my brothers to rest in the dark before You ever moved the money, and that You provided rent to the exact pound. I thank You that You never once made merchandise of me; that when You could have overridden my will in an instant, You chose instead to knock and to wait, to demonstrate on a cross the surpassing value of Your love and then to leave the door of my heart in my own trembling hand. Forgive me, Lord, for every time I have wanted the yes more than I have loved the person, for every genuine-seeming word that cloaked a covetous motive, for every hook hidden in the bait; purify my persuasion that it would seek only the true good of the one before me and always bear the light of full disclosure. Forgive me too where I have preached rest I have not carried home; let my wife enter this שָׁלוֹם and cease to be our household's designated worrier. I lift up Cooper, Jaden, and Samuel, all made in Your image; guard Cooper from the crushing burden of being his own savior, let the question that slipped into that call become a planted seed, and teach him to see in us not a company or a meeting but a family birthed of Your Spirit. As one of Your servants standing between the heavens and the earth, I make agreement with Your will that our speech would be a just weight, that our brotherhood would be true κοινωνία and not a costume, and that we would be heirs who receive rather than slaves who strive, abiding in the Vine so that both the study and the harvest come as fruit of the same life. I shall not die, but live and declare Your works; save now, I beseech You, O Lord — and I give it all back to You before the next call ever connects, because You are a Father, and a good one. To the God who persuades without violating, who saves without coercing, whose mercy endureth forever, be all the glory.
