# 08-09 Call Reflection: Consecrating a Christian Business Above Any Contract **Title:** 08-09 at 16:08 — When Zeal Rides In Like a Wave: Building on a Higher Covenant Than Any Contract **Recorded:** 08-09, spanning 16:08 through 21:00 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I sit with this long day of recorded exchanges between myself and my brother and business partner Samuel, I notice a peculiar mixture rising in my spirit — genuine excitement over provision, and a quiet unease I could not name in the moment. We were rejoicing that every setter is a professing Christian, that the Lord had provided, that leading with grace opened doors. And yet, threaded through all our celebration was the language of coarse jesting, the naming of a “mustard seed program” for gain, and a young man’s grandmother dying mentioned almost in passing. What began in intercession — a Hannah-like vow that this whole endeavor would remain in the Lord’s name — kept bending, again and again, toward equity splits, contracts, and the appetite for men’s praise. Twice we said aloud that we are ultimately “under a higher covenant than any contract could give us.” Something arrests me across all of it: I cannot separate how I do business from Whom I belong to. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the way we invoke the name of the Lord in our business dealings is not a neutral thing. To say “the Lord has provided” is either a holy confession or a hollow garnish, and the difference lies not in the words but in the fear of God that either governs or does not govern our tongues and our motives. So many good things begin at an altar and end in a showroom; a vision conceived in prayer can drift, almost imperceptibly, into a machine that runs on its own fuel, forgetting the One who breathed it into being. For disciples who are also builders, workers, and providers — those who will read this and those who labor alongside me — the question is not merely “will I tithe?” but “will the whole thing remain consecrated?” The discipleship of a believer is not confined to prayer closets and Sunday gatherings; it presses into calendars, commissions, hiring practices, equity clauses, and how we treat the setters and closers who become, as I said aloud, “literally the face of our company.” The Spirit does not divide the sacred from the vocational, and this is the weightier matter I cannot let the thrill of thirty booked calls silence. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation By natural reasoning, this is simply a good day — perhaps the best day. Two capable partners were hired to recruit and manage a team, a system of standard operating procedures was written, thirty motivated setters were assembled, and the projected math promises real income. The Christian identity of the team is a competitive advantage, a shared value that builds trust and reduces friction. The “mustard seed program” for underprivileged youth reads as generosity and social good. The evening’s talk of fiscal years, anti-dilution clauses, solicitor-drafted contracts, and refusing to sell controlling stakes to strangers is exactly the shrewdness two ambitious young founders should exercise. Running a Bible study that builds morale sets them apart from “forex guru guys.” The clean break from Emily reads as healthy boundary-setting. The talk of giving to charity, of no Rolexes and no cars, reads as admirable idealism — a moral guardrail against the corrupting effect of sudden money. Even the celebratory profanity, the relief at never having to book a meeting again, the rush of hearing fifteen people say “thanks boss” — these are the honest emotions of young men finally seeing the fruit of their labor. The world would call this wisdom, momentum, and even a kind of blessing. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind cannot weigh a thing it cannot see, and what it cannot see is the seam where holy language and unholy motive are being stitched together. It can measure conversion rates and admire airtight equity clauses, but it cannot detect the subtle inversion hidden in the sentence “God is going to be touching parts of so many all across the world, and it’s going to be because we started a Christian company” — where the enterprise becomes the cause and God becomes the effect. It reads “I need more thanks bosses, that’s my biggest motivation I’ve ever had” as harmless enthusiasm, unable to perceive how praise from men quietly competes for the throne only God should hold. It sees a business plan with a charitable clause; the Spirit sees a covenant being tested before it is even fulfilled. What looks like idealistic policy — rigid rules about what may or may not be purchased — the Spirit exposed to me as potentially the very trap the enemy sets for the sincere: a righteousness of appearance rather than a purity of heart. The flesh, however clever with contracts, is blind to the finer contracts written on the heart. Only the Spirit can pierce that seam and show us what is really being built. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I listen back to these conversations, the first thing the Spirit presses on me is the repeated confession, “the Lord has provided.” I want to honor that instinct, because it is a good one — the desire to attribute increase to God rather than to ourselves. Scripture commands exactly this posture: “And you shall remember the LORD your God, for it is He who gives you power to get wealth” (Deuteronomy 8:18). The Hebrew there is חַיִל (chayil, KHAH-yeel) — power, wealth, strength — and it is God-given, meant to keep man remembering the Giver. So I do not want to crush this reflex; I want to purify it. The question the Spirit is asking me is not whether we said the right words, but whether we said them in the fear that Deuteronomy 8 was actually written to produce. And then I notice something that unsettles me. In the very same breath as “the Lord has provided,” our speech was laced with coarse language, and the Kingdom itself became a marketing category. James’s grandmother died, and it passed by like a weather report. The Spirit is not condemning me here — He is holding up a mirror. For the mouth “that blesses our God and Father, and with it we curse men, who have been made in the similitude of God. Out of the same mouth proceed blessing and cursing. My brethren, these things ought not to be so” (James 3:9–10). There is a Greek word that keeps surfacing — εὐλογία (eulogia, yoo-lo-GEE-ah) — blessing, the good word spoken. Its shadow-word is when blessing becomes mere technique. When I said, “ever since I proposed to you, why not close off the niche and literally open with grace and blessings in the Lord Jesus Christ,” I have to ask honestly before God: was that a confession of Christ, or a conversion tactic? εὐλογία (yoo-lo-GEE-ah) that flows upward to God and outward to men is holy; εὐλογία (yoo-lo-GEE-ah) deployed to “close off the niche” has crossed into something Scripture names elsewhere. Because Paul warns of “men of corrupt minds and destitute of the truth, who suppose that godliness is a means of gain. From such withdraw yourself” (1 Timothy 6:5). The Greek there is πορισμός (porismos, po-ris-MOS) — a source of gain, a means of profit. The danger Paul names is not making money while being godly; it is treating godliness itself as πορισμός (po-ris-MOS). When a team is assembled specifically because “every single one is Christian,” and this is celebrated primarily as a business advantage, I must let the Spirit search whether we have quietly begun to use the household of faith as a market. Yet the same Paul says, “godliness with contentment is great gain” (1 Timothy 6:6) — εὐσέβεια (eusebeia, yoo-SEH-bay-ah), reverence, joined to contentment, is itself the true gain. The setters being Christian is not the problem. The problem would be if their faith is valued chiefly because it makes them trustworthy salesmen rather than because they are beloved brethren whose souls I am accountable to shepherd, not merely to deploy. Then I come to the “mustard seed program,” and here the Spirit presses hardest — and across both conversations it kept returning. The parable of the mustard seed is Jesus’ picture of the Kingdom of Heaven, the κόκκος (kokkos, KOK-kos), the smallest seed that becomes a tree where “the birds of the air come and nest in its branches” (Matthew 13:31–32). But in our talk, the name was attached to a plan “designed for atheist kids,” a place to refer those who don’t qualify, where the only spiritual requirement is attending huddles “where we preach and talk about sales.” The mustard seed is not a holding pen for the unqualified; it is the Kingdom itself. Jesus ate with tax collectors and sinners precisely inside the fellowship, not in a referral tier (Luke 5:30–32). I feel the Spirit asking whether our structures welcome the unbeliever toward Christ or quietly sort him away from the warm center where he might actually meet Him. Let the mustard seed be a welcoming tree, not a waiting room. The Hebrew concept underneath all of this is קֹדֶשׁ (qodesh, KO-desh) — holiness, that which is set apart, that which must not be treated as common. The recurring failure of Israel’s priesthood was that they “have not distinguished between the holy and unholy” (Ezekiel 22:26). As I listen to us mix profanity with praise, parable with pipeline, the death of an old woman with the excitement of a calendar, I hear the Spirit naming this exact failure in miniature. The small failures to distinguish קֹדֶשׁ (KO-desh) from the common are precisely how a heart is slowly discipled into treating everything, even God, as ordinary. And here is the mercy: the Spirit did not wait until we had built an empire on a mixed foundation. He is speaking now, in the small hours of the thing, while it is still a mustard seed of its own — small enough to be uprooted and replanted rightly. As the evening turned to contracts, the Spirit pressed upon me the weight of what we said almost in passing: “you will answer to the Father for what you do to each other here.” That is not a decorative flourish; it is the true foundation. “Unless the LORD builds the house, they labor in vain who build it” (Psalm 127:1) — בָּנָה (banah, bah-NAH), to build, is vain unless the LORD is the true Builder. Every anti-dilution provision we discussed is a shadow of a deeper reality: that faithfulness between brothers is finally kept by God, not by lawyers. Much of our energy went into guarding against betrayal — the sneaky purchase of sold equity, the stranger who might walk in and say “get out.” That vigilance is not wrong; Nehemiah built with a trowel in one hand and a weapon in the other (Nehemiah 4:17). But Judas sat inside a covenant community and betrayed with a kiss, and no clause stopped him. What guards a brotherhood is not the tightness of the paperwork but the softness of the heart before God, for “the heart is deceitful above all things” (Jeremiah 17:9). Something arrests me in the phrase “I need more thanks bosses, that’s my biggest motivation I’ve ever had.” I do not want to shame my brother for a genuine joy. Yet I recognize the same appetite Jesus named when He said the Pharisees “loved the praise of men more than the praise of God” (John 12:43). The Greek δόξα (doxa, DOK-sah) — glory, honor, weight — reveals the danger: human applause has real weight, and the soul that begins to feed on it will slowly starve for the applause of God. I feel the Spirit inviting both of us to let our deepest motivation be the Father’s “well done,” not the team’s “thanks boss.” I sit longer with our shared vision of a Bible study gathering Serbians, a Mexican in Paris, Nigerians, Filipinos, Australians, Americans, and English believers around one table — a foretaste of “a great multitude which no one could number, of all nations, tribes, peoples, and tongues, standing before the throne” (Revelation 7:9), every ἔθνος (ethnos, ETH-nos) present. That longing is holy. And I hold to what I said: I do not want a study where people merely “know Scripture well”; I want revelation to “come through everybody’s mouths, not just the host,” for Paul writes, “when you come together, each of you has a psalm, has a teaching, has a revelation” (1 Corinthians 14:26) — the οἰκοδομή (oikodomē, oy-ko-doh-MAY), the building up of the many. Yet even here the Spirit refines me. When my brother asked, “are you willing to disciple that one?” and pressed, “I need you to ask the Lord what to do,” I answered, “I know what to do. He’s told me what to do.” There is a confidence born of genuine preparation, and there is a confidence that has quietly stopped asking. The safest posture for one who hosts a Spirit-filled gathering is continual inquiry, like David who “inquired of the LORD” again even after past victories (2 Samuel 5:19, 5:23). The Spirit also brings me to the Sikh man who was removed, whom my brother interpreted as “the Lord confirming that this is not a Christian.” Here I must tread carefully and lovingly. If someone’s conduct genuinely made them unfit for a role, that is a reasonable judgment. But I feel a caution rising: I do not want to baptize a hiring decision as divine confirmation about a soul’s standing before God. The Lord alone “knows those who are His” (2 Timothy 2:19) — γινώσκω (ginōskō, gih-NOSS-koh), to know intimately — and He desires that man’s salvation as much as anyone’s. Men see conduct, not the soul’s standing. There is a moment I keep returning to, where I reached for Scripture to settle a matter of accountability, gesturing toward confronting an offending brother “between you and him alone” (Matthew 18:15), and Paul’s severe word to “deliver such a one to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that his spirit may be saved” (1 Corinthians 5:5). I honored the text by naming its original context. Yet these are covenantal disciplines for the household of faith, aimed at repentance — not primarily employment policy. The word beneath it is παραδίδωμι (paradidōmi, pah-rah-DID-oh-mee) — to hand over, deliver up — the same verb used of Judas handing over Jesus and of the Father who “did not spare His own Son, but delivered Him up for us all” (Romans 8:32). To hand someone over is a fearsome thing. If I ever exercise anything like this in leadership, it cannot be casual severance; it must carry the weight of one who knows what it costs to be handed over. Then I hear myself call the setters and closers “literally the face of our company.” Something in the Spirit quickens, because the Hebrew פָּנִים (panim, pah-NEEM) — face, presence — is how Israel spoke of the nearness of God: “Make Your face shine upon Your servant” (Psalm 31:16); “If Your Presence does not go with us, do not bring us up from here” (Exodus 33:15). To be someone’s face is to carry their presence into a room. If those who work under us are the face of the company, then I who claim to represent God carry a far higher face. This is why my instinct about public reachability was not mere naïveté. I said that if I am to represent my God and the covenant we have, “I need to hide nothing” — remembering the One who said, “I spoke openly to the world… and in secret I have said nothing” (John 18:20). There is a holy transparency proper to a servant of the covenant, a willingness to be found; I named it aloud as “a fire burning inside for public ministry.” That fire connects to the design principle that most delighted me: “everything that anybody does in the system, if you put value in, it gets value back.” What the Spirit shows me is that we stumbled, perhaps unknowingly, onto a reflection of a Kingdom law — “Give, and it will be given to you: good measure, pressed down, shaken together, and running over” (Luke 6:38); “whatever a man sows, that he will also reap” (Galatians 6:7). A system that rewards genuine contribution regardless of where one starts is a faint image of the God who is no respecter of persons. But here the Spirit presses a tension. The great longing voiced was to reach “full automation,” to “sit back and let it do its thing,” to be “done.” The Kingdom is never a set-it-and-forget-it machine. The Master in the parables does not automate His vineyard; He knows His servants by name and returns to reckon face to face (Matthew 25:19). “Be diligent to know the state of your flocks” (Proverbs 27:23). My brother said, “no one should have to know our names for this business to operate” — and I understand it as a philosophy of direct contact, but I do not want anonymity to become insulation from the people whose labor becomes the face I answer for before God. I also spoke of being “a valuable mediator if there are tensions or problems within higher levels,” and that word is not neutral for a believer. “There is one Mediator between God and men, the Man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5) — μεσίτης (mesitēs, meh-SEE-tace), one who stands in the middle to reconcile. He has committed to us the ministry of reconciliation (2 Corinthians 5:18–19). “Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God” (Matthew 5:9). This dignifies the very work my brother wanted to spare me from. And I am seeing that the great tension running through this entire day is the question of ordering — what is the tree and what is the branch, what is the cause and what is the effect. We kept saying the company will produce the ministry, the Bible study, the prayer circles, the global touch of God. But Jesus reverses that entirely: “Seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things shall be added to you” (Matthew 6:33) — the βασιλεία (basileia, bah-sih-LAY-ah), the reign of God, must be sought first; all else is the “added” fruit, never the cause. God is not offended by our building; He gave Bezalel skill to work with gold for the tabernacle (Exodus 31:3–5). The question He presses is one of throne and altar: is this company laid on His altar, or is His name laid on this company’s marketing? The same fire that fell on Elijah’s soaked altar will fall on ours if the altar is truly His (1 Kings 18:38). This brings me to where it all began, which my brother named himself — a Hannah moment. He drew the line to Samuel: “I’ll let you have a child, but that child has to go.” Hannah’s vow in 1 Samuel 1:11 is not a transaction but a surrender before the answer arrives; the word is נָדַר (nadar, nah-DAR) — to vow, consecrate. She receives the blessing already surrendered. My brother said the Lord told him plainly, “I will let you have this, but it will be in my name,” and I believe that is the load-bearing wall of this venture. This is why I found myself saying the ministry must remain the main objective “even if it’s not public that way yet.” The Lord is not asking for a Christian veneer on a secular engine; He is asking that the whole be offered as one living sacrifice — our λογικὴν λατρείαν (logikēn latreian, lo-gee-KANE lah-tray-AN), our reasonable spiritual worship (Romans 12:1). Then came the exchange I keep returning to. My brother laid down a rule with real conviction: no Rolexes, no cars, twenty-five thousand to charity. And I felt genuinely impressed to soften that — not to loosen devotion, but to protect it from becoming law. Paul writes, “So let each one give as he purposes in his heart, not grudgingly or of necessity; for God loves a cheerful giver” (2 Corinthians 9:7) — ἱλαρὸν δότην (hilaron dotēn, hee-lah-RON DOH-tane), a hilarious, radiant giver. And in Colossians 2:23 he warns that regulations — “Do not touch, do not taste, do not handle” — “have an appearance of wisdom in self-imposed religion,” ἐθελοθρησκείᾳ (ethelothrēskeia, eth-el-oh-thrase-KAY-ah), “but are of no value against the indulgence of the flesh.” The flesh can wear asceticism as easily as luxury. A man who refuses the Rolex to feel holy has simply relocated his idol into his own self-image. The Spirit’s word through me was mercy: “He would rather have you buy that Rolex if that’s what brings you into more proximity to Him.” That is not permission for indulgence; it is the exposure of the heart as the only true altar. The real safeguard is not a purchasing policy but a consecrated heart, for “the LORD weighs the hearts” (Proverbs 21:2) — תֹּכֵן (tokhen, toh-KHANE), He assays, like a refiner testing the true weight of metal. My brother’s passion for trafficked children, his vow of fifty percent to rescue work, his willingness to live on five thousand a month to fund it — these are beautiful, and the Lord weighs them not by their size but by their source. Yet the Spirit presses me toward a fuller stewardship than charity-versus-luxury. “If anyone does not provide for his own, and especially for those of his household, he has denied the faith” (1 Timothy 5:8) — προνοεῖ (pronoei, pro-no-EH-ee), to provide for, to foresee. Giving is not always giving away; sometimes it is giving in — pouring the blessing into the very lives God set before you. When I asked the Lord for more finance to steward, He answered instead with a child — “No, here, have this life to steward instead.” A child bears the image of God, and stewarding a soul is nearer to the Father’s heart than stewarding a sum. We kept measuring in dollars, and the Lord kept answering in lives. And I hear the Kingdom mystery underneath my brother’s raw confession: “The Lord had to make me broke to make us rich.” That is the pattern of the cross — “though He was rich, yet for your sakes He became poor, that you through His poverty might become rich” (2 Corinthians 8:9); ἐπτώχευσεν (eptōcheusen, ep-TOH-khyoo-sen), He became poor. The emptying precedes the filling. When I admitted that if I had not been starving because of this company I never would have returned to the Lord, I testified to the same mercy — the famine drove me home, as it drove the prodigal to his senses (Luke 15). Finally, I return to the tenderness in the exchange — my brother needing “a little break” while he moves and prepares for his baby, asking me to “take up the reins.” That is the real substance of brotherhood: “Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ” (Galatians 6:2) — βαστάζω (bastazō, bahs-TAD-zoh), to carry. The covenant we spoke of is proven not in the clause but in exactly this: when he is weak, I will carry the load. And even the audio cutting out at the end, where I said “that was the Lord saying it’s time for me to go steward my family,” feels like a parable — the Lord closing the meeting Himself, redirecting me from the good work of planning to the nearer work of loving. ## Implications and Challenges I want to begin with grace: the desire to honor God as Provider, to build with integrity, to disciple, to gather the nations, to help the underprivileged, to rescue trafficked children, and to work alongside believers are all good and rare desires, and I will not let the enemy shame them into paralysis. - **On treating Christian identity as a brand asset.** When we celebrate that “every single one is Christian” primarily as a competitive edge, we risk reducing brethren to reliable inventory. The challenge is to love these thirty souls as people I am accountable to, not producers on a calendar — bearers of the image of God, not statistics. - **On coarse speech wrapped around holy confession.** Casual profanity mingled with praise has been normalized as authenticity. But “let no corrupt word proceed out of your mouth, but what is good for necessary edification” (Ephesians 4:29). Reverence in speech is the fruit of the fear of God, and it is a question of whether the mouth that names itself a representation of God is consecrated. - **On borrowing sacred language to sanctify commercial aims.** Naming a program after a Kingdom parable while gearing it toward gain challenges me to stop baptizing a sales funnel with the name of a parable Jesus told about His reign. Any structure that refers the unbeliever *away* from the warm center of fellowship must be re-examined against the Jesus who drew sinners near. - **On the idol of automation and disengagement.** Western entrepreneurial culture treats “passive income” and “sitting back” as the promised land. But the Kingdom knows no faithful servant who buries his engagement and calls it rest. Efficiency is a servant, not a lord; I must build systems that serve people rather than systems that let me disappear from them. - **On the appetite for praise and provision mistaken for approval.** The rush of “thanks boss” and the full calendar both felt like divine endorsement. But abundance is where the heart most easily forgets God (Deuteronomy 8:11–14); Jeshurun “grew fat and kicked” (Deuteronomy 32:15). Maturity is receiving increase with trembling gratitude rather than triumphant relief, and letting the Father’s approval outweigh men’s applause. - **On proving devotion by prohibition.** Modern Christian culture loves a visible sacrifice because it is measurable. Yet self-imposed severity has “an appearance of wisdom” while leaving the flesh untouched. The harder, quieter work is the examined heart, which no one can see and no one can applaud — and remembering that an hour of Kingdom strategy is not a substitute for presence at the dinner table. - **On discerning souls versus discerning fit, and weaponizing Scripture.** Removing someone for conduct can be right; declaring it heaven’s verdict on their salvation is spiritual overreach. And when I cite Matthew 18 or 1 Corinthians 5 for a hiring policy, I must test whether I am submitting to the text or recruiting it. Let Scripture govern the aim of my actions, not merely decorate them. I end in grace: none of this requires abandoning the work. It requires consecrating it. The mustard seed is still small enough to plant in the right soil, and the Father disciplines those He loves. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity means I can rejoice in provision while letting the Spirit examine the motives beneath the rejoicing — auditing my heart before I audit my books. It means my mouth is disciplined enough that blessing and cursing do not flow from the same spring, that I hold both the Rolex and the charity loosely while holding the heart’s intent tightly, and that I stop compartmentalizing faith from work, letting the Lordship of Christ audit my calendars, hiring, speech, and giving alike. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience calls me to consecrate this venture — to refuse godliness as a means of gain, to steward the brethren as souls rather than assets, and to strip Kingdom language off anything not genuinely serving the Kingdom. It calls me to seek the Kingdom first, letting the company be an offering rather than the engine, receiving every blessing already surrendered like Hannah handing Samuel to the Lord before she ever weaned him. And it calls me to keep the doors wide to the unbeliever, that the mustard seed would grow into a tree where every weary bird finds rest. **For Prophetic Posture:** A prophetic posture means I watch the full calendar with the same vigilance I would watch an empty one, and I keep inquiring of the Lord like David before each battle rather than assuming His voice when I have stopped asking. It means I remain reachable to God and to people even when the world counsels insulation, guarding the altar so the fire that falls is His fire and not our own enthusiasm — the kind of partner who will speak and receive the uncomfortable word in love, watching my own heart most vigilantly of all. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come as Your servant and co-heir, and I thank You genuinely for the provision You have poured out — for capable partners, for willing workers, for a full calendar and open doors, and for the good desires You have planted in us to disciple, to gather the nations, to rescue the stolen little ones, and to carry one another in weakness. I thank You that this whole thing was born in intercession, and I ask that You keep it there — in Your name, under Your hand, weighed on Your scales. I confess where I heard my own voice mixing praise with coarseness, Kingdom language with gain, where I reached for Your Word to justify rather than submit, and where my flesh can wear both luxury and austerity as idols. Search me, try my heart, and lead me in the way everlasting. Purify my tongue and my motives; teach me to love these thirty souls as brethren and not as instruments, and to steward my household and my marriage as consecrated fields. Reorder us so that we seek Your Kingdom first and let every enterprise be the byproduct of that seeking, never the reverse. Guard my brother from the sweetness of men’s praise and guard me from assuming Your voice when I have stopped asking. Make our Bible study a place where Your fire falls and Your revelation flows through every mouth, and make our doors wide, that the mustard seed would become a tree where every weary bird finds rest — a shelter, and not merely a funnel. And thank You for cutting the call when it was time to go love my wife; teach me that presence is worship too. I surrender this venture to You now, while it is still small enough to plant rightly — the code and the calling, the profit and the provision, the charity and the child — given back before it is even received. Not our name, but Yours be glorified, in the name of Jesus Christ, come in the flesh, amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |--------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Deuteronomy 8:18 | חַיִל (chayil, KHAH-yeel) — power, wealth, strength | Wealth-getting power is God-given, meant to keep man remembering the Giver, not crediting himself. | | James 3:9–10 | εὐλογία (eulogia, yoo-lo-GEE-ah) — blessing, good word | The same mouth must not carry both blessing and cursing;
