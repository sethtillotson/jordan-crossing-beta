# 04-07 12:33 Call Meditation: The Staircase You Cannot See **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation with brief post-reflection (live exchange between Seth and a brother named Samuel, captured in real time) **Personal Meditation: The Staircase You Cannot See — Wilderness, Weaponry, and the Posture of the Prophet** ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother Samuel describe what the Lord had begun to clarify for him — not a command to do a specific thing, but a posture and framework to inhabit — something in me recognized the very ground I had stood on not long ago. It is one of the most disorienting and sacred seasons a believer can enter: the moment God stops telling you *what* to do and begins showing you *who* you are becoming. The conversation that unfolded between us on that break — nine minutes stolen from a workday — carried more weight than its brevity suggested, and I find myself returning to it now with fresh eyes, aware that the Spirit had more to say through it than either of us fully articulated in the moment. **Why This Matters:** There is a generation of believers who are burning themselves out trying to locate a blueprint from God when God is not handing them a blueprint — He is forming a person. The confusion this produces is not incidental; it is part of the design. When Samuel described how he thought God would simply say *do this job* and it turned out to be something far more interior — a posture, a framework, a direction of character formation — he was touching one of the most consistently misunderstood dynamics in the entire arc of Scripture. From Abraham to Joseph to Moses to David to Jeremiah to Paul, God rarely gives a destination without first building a carrier. The destination without the character to hold it would destroy the person. This matters for every believer who is genuinely seeking God’s will in a season of rubble, because the rubble is not the problem — it is the construction site. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, when it hears a man say “God is giving me a posture and framework rather than a clear directive,” will almost immediately reach for one of two interpretations. Either it will conclude that the person has simply not received a clear enough answer yet and needs to keep seeking until something concrete emerges — as though divine guidance must always arrive in the form of a job title and a start date — or it will privately wonder whether the whole enterprise of waiting on God is a sophisticated form of spiritual avoidance, a way of deferring responsibility under the cover of holy language. The world is transactional in its deepest instincts. It expects guidance to look like a project plan: goals, timelines, measurable outcomes, and a clearly defined role description. Even within Christian culture, this transactional assumption quietly governs much of how we counsel one another about calling and vocation. We want to know what God said, what step to take next, and how soon we can expect the situation to resolve. **The Limitation:** But the formation God is doing in a season like Samuel’s — and like the one I have been walking through myself — is not transactional. It is covenantal, and covenantal processes operate by a logic the natural mind simply cannot access from the outside. A staircase on which you can only see the next step looks, from a distance, like a staircase going nowhere. The step you are standing on appears to be the last one. What the unassisted human eye cannot perceive is that the next step only becomes visible when your full weight is on the current one — and that the entire staircase is already built, already known, already held in the mind of a God who neither slumbers nor is surprised. The natural mind, absent the Spirit’s illumination, will always interpret invisibility as absence. But invisibility and absence are not the same thing, and this distinction is perhaps the hinge upon which the entire life of faith turns. ## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation As I sat with this conversation afterward, the first thing that arrested me was Samuel’s choice of words: he said God had “cleared a lot of things up” not by telling him what to do, but by showing him that the issue of *striving* — something I had apparently named in an earlier exchange — had been producing a kind of anxious grasping in him. What strikes me about this is how scripturally precise it is, even if neither of us reached for chapter and verse in that moment. The Hebrew concept of מְנוּחָה (menuḥah, meh-noo-KHAH) — rest, settled repose, the cessation of striving — is not passive inactivity. It is the confident stillness of one who has ceased fighting to establish what only God can establish. When Samuel said he “took it all to God” and found an answer there, he was practicing menuḥah, even if he would not have called it that. That is the first layer I want to sit with: that the posture God gives in a season of formation is itself the answer, not a stepping stone toward the answer. I said to Samuel during our call that God had dismantled his fortresses — everything standing between him and the Lord in terms of love, importance, and misplaced trust. I believe that is true, and I want to say it more carefully here than I had time to say it on my lunch break. The word the Spirit brings to mind is the one Paul uses in 2 Corinthians 10:4 — that the weapons of our warfare are not carnal but mighty in God for pulling down strongholds. The NKJV renders the Greek as ὀχυρωμάτων (ochyrōmatōn, oh-khoo-roh-MAH-tohn) — fortified places, castles of the mind and will erected against the knowledge of God. What I have observed — in my own life and now in Samuel’s — is that God does not always tear down a stronghold through confrontation alone. Sometimes He simply removes everything the stronghold was protecting. When there is nothing left to guard, the fortress loses its purpose and falls on its own weight. And what is left in the aftermath of a demolished fortress? Rubble. But I want to press on this image a moment longer, because Scripture does not leave us in the rubble. In Nehemiah, the walls of Jerusalem were rubble — charred, broken, a reproach to the nations. But the rubble was not the conclusion; it was the precondition for the rebuild. Nehemiah wept over it in chapter 1, yes, but by chapter 2 he was already receiving favor and resources from a pagan king. The rubble was the moment of maximum visibility for what God was about to do. Samuel is in a Nehemiah moment. The broken-down walls are not evidence of abandonment — they are evidence of preparation. This is where I want to trace the thread that came out of my testimony during that call — the one I have been struggling to fully articulate for about four months. When I heard Jesus tell me, clearly and simply, *read your Bible*, I initially received it as almost too simple — surely there was more. But I have come to understand that in the economy of the Spirit, simplicity is not shallowness. The command to read is, in Hebrew thought, connected to the command to listen. The Shema of Deuteronomy 6:4-5 begins with שְׁמַע (shema, sheh-MAH) — hear, listen, attend. The entire orientation of Israel’s covenant relationship with God begins not with a command to act but a command to *receive*. The foundation of every prophetic assignment is first an attentiveness, a trained listening. Jesus was not giving me a task — He was giving me a posture. The task was the posture. And the posture was the foundation. What I also said to Samuel — and what I want to slow down and develop here — is that everything I was compelled to do in the Spirit during that earlier season of apparent failure was in fact the weaponsmith’s work. I did not understand it at the time. The Bible studies that flopped, the meditations that went nowhere, the conversations that dissolved — all of it was, as the Spirit revealed to me in the middle of a weld, the buildup of character and skill for the weapon I had not yet been handed. This is consistent with what the Lord did to David. Before David ever faced Goliath with a sling, he had fought a lion and a bear in the wilderness while nobody was watching (1 Samuel 17:34-36). The wilderness assignments are always preparation for the public commission. The tragedy of our impatient age is that we abandon the wilderness before the lion and the bear — and then we wonder why we have no testimony to stand on when the giant appears. The framework Samuel received — what I called Watch, Wait, Work, drawn from my reading of Habakkuk, Haggai, and Zephaniah — was the first theological posture the Spirit ever gave me that has never required revision. I want to trace it briefly through those three prophets, because its durability is not accidental. Habakkuk 2:1 reads: *“I will stand my watch and set myself on the rampart, and watch to see what He will say to me.”* The prophet does not go looking for a strategy meeting with God. He climbs the watchtower, positions himself, and waits. Haggai is the prophet who calls the remnant to build — but the building he calls them to is not a program of human initiative; it is a response to specific divine instruction about a specific time. And Zephaniah ends with a vision of the Lord Himself rejoicing over His people with singing (Zephaniah 3:17) — the waiting posture is ultimately answered by the joy of the one you waited for. Together, these three minor prophets outline a prophetic posture that has not changed across dispensations: you watch for the hand of God, you wait in expectant faith, and you work when and how He directs — not before, not in your own strength, and not according to a timeline you constructed. The dimension of this that I touched on but want to develop further is the profound loneliness of the prophetic season. I mentioned Moses at the burning bush, Daniel with the angel, Ezekiel in his visions — these are men who encountered God in ways their communities could not fully process or verify. The burning bush was not a group experience. The vision in Daniel 10 left the men with Daniel trembling and fleeing, though they had not seen anything (Daniel 10:7). The prophetic encounter is frequently solitary not because God is being cruel, but because the formation it produces must go deep into the individual before it can go wide into the community. What I experienced during my own wilderness — the loneliness of having nobody to bring this to, not even my wife at the time, who was quietly concerned — was not a design flaw. It was the design. The prophet must know God as *their* God before they can declare Him as God to others. And this brings me to the sharpest edge of what I said to Samuel in those final minutes before I had to return to work: a prophet assigned to the prophetic office — not merely one operating in the gift of prophecy, which belongs to all believers as the Spirit wills (1 Corinthians 14:31) — is *always* confrontational to the established order, and the church almost always resists them. Jeremiah was thrown in a cistern by the very priests of the temple he was assigned to serve (Jeremiah 38:6). Micaiah ben Imlah told the truth to four hundred false prophets and a compromised king and was slapped in the face and thrown in prison (1 Kings 22:24-27). The pattern is not an anomaly — it is the credential. The prophet must pass through the season of absolute loneliness so that when the whole world turns its back, they already know that God has not. They have tested His faithfulness in the dark, and the dark did not swallow them. What this means for Samuel — and for every brother and sister who finds themselves in a season where God is forming rather than directing — is that the formation itself is the message. God is not withholding His will from you. He is enacting it. The posture He is building, the character He is shaping, the patience He is requiring — these are not obstacles between you and your assignment. They are the assignment. The weapon is not yet in your hand because you are not yet the person who can wield it without it destroying you. This is grace, not delay. I want to close this meditation body with something that came through powerfully in Samuel’s own words, which I want to honor: *“To have that ability to sit with it — that’s where all the secrets are revealed. When you sit with it, that’s where all the secrets are revealed.”* He said this himself, from his own encounter with God that morning by the ocean, and I believe it deserves to stand as its own piece of revelation. The Greek word for mystery in the New Testament is μυστήριον (mystērion, moos-TAY-ree-on) — a thing hidden, now disclosed to the initiated. The secrets of the Kingdom are not hidden *from* the one who sits with God; they are hidden *for* them, preserved in the place of intimacy, available only to the one willing to be still long enough to receive. Samuel found this not through striving but through surrender. That is the testimony the Spirit is building in him. ## Implications and Challenges Let me begin here with what I genuinely believe: most of the people reading this love God and are sincerely trying to follow Him. What I am about to say is not a condemnation of sincere effort. It is an invitation to examine whether the framework we have inherited for discerning God’s will is actually biblical, or whether it has been subtly shaped by a culture that prizes productivity, clarity, and speed above all else. The first challenge the Spirit is pressing on me is this: we have trained an entire generation to expect divine guidance to look like a life plan with quarterly milestones, when Scripture consistently shows us that God forms His servants through seasons of sustained, unresolved obscurity. The Psalms are full of the language of waiting — Psalm 27:14 says *“Wait on the Lord; be of good courage, and He shall strengthen your heart; wait, I say, on the Lord!”* This is not a transitional verse to get through on the way to the answer. The waiting *is* part of the answer. If we rush past the waiting season, we skip the courage-strengthening work that only happens there. The second challenge is one I feel personally, and I say this with love: there is a tendency among Spirit-filled believers to label every season of formation as spiritual warfare or demonic delay, when what is actually happening is divine patient craftsmanship. Not every slow season is an attack. Sometimes the slowness is the Spirit saying, *there is more I need to build in you before I can trust you with more.* Discernment between these two is one of the most important things we can cultivate, and it requires — again — the posture of sitting with God long enough to know the difference between His voice and the voice of our own impatience. Third: the loneliness of the prophetic call is real, and the church needs to repent of how it has handled the prophetically gifted in its midst. I say this without anger and with full acknowledgment that prophets are also capable of error and pride — we are not exempt from the need for covering, accountability, and love. But the pattern of stifling, dismissing, or simply ignoring the prophetically gifted — while celebrating the eloquent preacher and the visible worship leader — is a wound in the Body that has been left unaddressed for too long. The prophet does not need a platform. The prophet needs a community willing to be patient enough to test and receive what the Spirit is saying through them. Fourth: the distinction I made during that call — between a member of the Body moving in the gift of prophecy and a person appointed to the prophetic office — is not an elitist distinction. It is a pastoral one. Every believer may prophesy as the Spirit gives utterance (1 Corinthians 14:31). But the prophet of Ephesians 4:11 is a specific gift to the Body of Christ — an office, a calling, a formation — and the formation required for that office is longer, harder, and more solitary than the broader gift. Conflating the two leads either to inflation (everyone calls themselves a prophet) or deflation (nobody takes the prophetic office seriously). Both extremes impoverish the Body. Fifth, and finally: I want to say something about the relationship between simplicity and depth that I believe the Spirit surfaced through what Jesus told me — *read your Bible*. We live in an age of staggering theological content: podcasts, conferences, commentary series, systematic theologies, online courses. All of these have their place. But the Spirit’s instruction to me was not *consume more content.* It was to sit with the living Word and let the living Word do what it does: pierce, divide, illuminate, convict, heal, and form. If your devotional life has become primarily content consumption rather than sustained encounter with the text in the presence of the Spirit, I would gently ask whether you have substituted the commentary for the conversation. The Bible is not a reference book. It is a place of meeting. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** The meditation that emerged from this conversation is asking us to fundamentally reframe what we mean by progress in the Kingdom. Progress is not always visible movement. Sometimes progress is the deepening of roots that no one above ground can yet see. A tree in winter looks dead to the observer who does not know that the root system is expanding in the frozen ground, preparing for a spring that is not yet visible but is already certain. Spiritual maturity means learning to trust the process of formation even when — especially when — nothing on the surface appears to be happening. It means holding the tension between the covenant promise of God and the apparent silence of the current season without collapsing into either despair or forced activity. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this calls forth is, paradoxically, the obedience of stillness. Watch. Wait. Work when directed. Do not manufacture the assignment; receive it. Do not construct the weapon; allow it to be forged. The practical application for those in a season like Samuel’s is this: identify the small, clear thing the Spirit has already given you — the one undeniable directive, however simple it seems — and do that fully, daily, and without reservation. Do not wait for the staircase to reveal all its steps before you climb the one in front of you. Step fully onto the next visible step, and trust that the following one will come into view when your full weight is committed. **For Prophetic Posture:** For those who carry a prophetic dimension — whether in the gift or the office — this meditation calls us to embrace the watchtower as our permanent address. Not as isolation, but as orientation. The prophetic person is not meant to be constantly broadcasting; they are meant to be constantly receiving, so that when they speak, they speak with fidelity to what they have actually heard rather than with the pressure of a platform to fill. The Watch-Wait-Work framework is not a formula — it is a lifestyle of disciplined receptivity. And the loneliness it sometimes requires is not a punishment. It is the very thing that ensures that when the prophet stands and speaks, their confidence is in the word they carry, not in the approval of the crowd that hears it. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who has stood in the rubble and learned — slowly, painfully, and gratefully — that the rubble is holy ground. Thank You for every dismantled fortress, every flopped Bible study, every season of welding and waiting and wondering when the piece would come together. I see now, as I could not see then, that Your hand was never still — that every act of apparent silence was an act of precise formation. I pray now for my brother Samuel, who has taken himself to the water and to You, and who is receiving the gift of a posture rather than a plan. Lord, let him learn the watchtower early. Let him find in the Watch-Wait-Work rhythm not a burden but a homecoming — the place where the striving ceases and the receiving begins. I pray for every son and daughter who is sitting in a season of obscurity right now, wondering if they have missed You or been forgotten by You — let the Spirit bear witness to them this day that they are known, held, and being fashioned for something they cannot yet see. We are Your servants, Lord. We are the prophets to whom You have promised to reveal Your secrets before You act (Amos 3:7). We stand on that covenant with thanksgiving. We do not demand a timeline. We ask only for faithfulness — Yours, which is certain, and ours, which we offer back to You as an act of daily surrender. Let the weapon be forged in the time You have ordained. Let the commission come in the fullness of Your timing, not ours. And until then — let us read, and watch, and wait, and be found faithful in the small and the hidden. To the Lamb who was slain, and who lives and reigns forever, be all glory, honor, and power. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Habakkuk 2:1 — “I will stand my watch and set myself on the rampart, and watch to see what He will say to me.” | שָׁמַר (shamar, shah-MAR) — to watch, guard, keep vigil | The prophetic posture is not passive; it is an active, deliberate positioning of oneself to receive.
