# 04-10 Call Reflection: Spiritual Warfare, Theology of Mercy, and the Narrow Path **Title:** 04-10 at 14:42 — The Narrow Path Has Many Trees, and the Devil Also Sends People **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection --- ## ⚡ Opening Observation As I listened to this phone call unfold between Samuel and me — factory noise in the background, earbuds crackling, the hum of ordinary working life pressing in on every side — I was struck by how much of God's real business gets done in the margins of the world's noise. What began as a casual check-in between brothers became a river of theological disclosure: the cross custom-made for each bearer, the nature of mercy over punishment, the Lion who is also the Lamb, the deception dressed as holiness, and the very real possibility that a young woman at a worksite could be an instrument of spiritual warfare. None of this was planned. That is precisely why it matters. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ desperately needs to recover the practice of iron sharpening iron in the rhythms of ordinary life — not only in sanctuaries, not only in polished sermons, but in the midst of factory floors and fifteen-minute lunch breaks and phone calls made from the corner by the window where there is still just enough signal. The conversations I had with Samuel touched on nearly every frontier where the modern Western church has grown soft: the nature of God's mercy, the reality of spiritual warfare, the danger of self-righteousness masquerading as intercession, the custom nature of each believer's calling, and the very narrow but very full path that the remnant walks. If saints are going to be formed, if the remnant is going to hold, these conversations must happen — and someone must take the time to draw out what the Spirit was doing beneath the surface of them. --- ## 🌍 The Worldly Mind's Interpretation Looking at this conversation through purely natural eyes, it reads like exactly what it appears to be on the surface: two friends catching up during a work shift, joking about workplace dynamics, sharing stories about interesting coworkers, processing relational complications, and occasionally dipping into religious conversation. A psychologist might observe two young men using theological language as a social bonding mechanism — a shared framework that gives them identity and belonging. A sociologist might note that the theological moments arise organically from life pressure: the question about how to show God love, the anxiety about moral failure, the curiosity about whether a flirtatious coworker represents a threat. From this vantage point, the conversation is a normal human thing — friends processing life through the lens of their shared religion. The spiritual observations would be reduced to coping mechanisms, and Samuel's six-hour conversation with his coworker Jacob would be catalogued as a personality trait rather than a Spirit-driven event. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind cannot perceive what was actually moving through this conversation, because the Spirit of God does not announce Himself with a press release. He moves in factory corners and crackling phone signals and the offhand question — *How do you show God that you love Him?* — asked by a six-foot-five man from Zimbabwe who happened to spend six hours on a pallet line with someone God had placed there on purpose. The natural mind hears background noise; the Spirit hears intercession. The natural mind sees a distraction in the form of a nineteen-year-old Portuguese girl with yogurt on her hands; the Spirit sees a commission and an assignment from the other side. The natural mind categorizes Seth's response to the brother who questioned his podcast plug as a clever rhetorical comeback; the Spirit recognizes it as the utterance of a mouth that has been shaped by Scripture until it reflexively speaks in the grammar of the Kingdom. What happened in this conversation cannot be fully understood by those who have not been trained to listen beneath the surface of things. --- ## ✝️ What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with this conversation now and listen again to what the Spirit was doing beneath its surface, the first thing that arrests me is the image of intercession happening behind a mask on a factory floor. I said it almost in passing — that while everyone else is thinking about lunch, I am thinking about when the sky will roll back like a scroll and whether I ought to be interceding for the mercy of God over the world. But this is not a throwaway moment. This is the prophetic posture of the watchman, and it is one of the most misunderstood callings in the contemporary church. The prophet Ezekiel was told to be a watchman for the house of Israel (Ezekiel 33:7), and that watchman did not stand on a physical tower in an obvious place. He stood wherever God posted him. Sometimes that post is a corner by a window in a factory where the grinder runs twenty-four hours a day. What I recognize now is that the hiddenness of the consecration is part of the consecration itself. The word קָדַשׁ (qadash, kah-DAHSH) — to be set apart, to be holy, to be consecrated — does not require an audience. In fact, the presence of an audience often contaminates it. Moses was taken up to Sinai not because the mountain was a beautiful location but because separation from the people was the condition under which God could work through him. I spoke about this with Samuel in relation to Moses striking the rock, and it is worth pressing into more deeply. Moses was set apart — קָדַשׁ (kah-DAHSH) — and that setting apart was not merely ceremonial. It was the condition of his usefulness. When he allowed the people's frustration to become his own, and when he struck the rock out of self-righteous anger rather than holy obedience, he did not lose his relationship with God. God remained his friend. But he lost the specific grace attached to that specific assignment. This is a distinction the modern church has nearly entirely collapsed: the difference between being loved by God and being entrusted with a particular commission. The conversation Samuel had with Jacob about bearing crosses is a thread that runs all the way back through the canon. When I said that God custom-designs each person's cross because their gift is custom to them, I was reaching for something that Paul articulates in 1 Corinthians 12 and that the Spirit makes vivid in the diversity of the prophets. There is no generic cross. There is no off-the-shelf suffering. When God looks at a person — and I said this to Samuel and I feel the weight of it even more now — He and that person are, in that moment of divine regard, the only two things in His relational attention. This is not a denial of God's omniscience; it is an affirmation of His intimacy. The technical language for this in covenant theology is the personalization of divine election, but the experiential language is simpler: He knew me before He formed me in the womb (Jeremiah 1:5), and the cross He assigns me is shaped around the contours of who He made me to be. The question Jacob asked Samuel — *How do you show God that you love Him?* — is one of the most searching questions a believer can encounter, and Samuel's answer was, I believe, Spirit-led. He did not retreat into performance. He did not reach for a list of religious activities. He said: it is a question of faith. If you trust God, you know that He knows. And this is precisely what the Apostle John was pointing toward when he wrote that we love Him because He first loved us (1 John 4:19). The showing of love is not the proving of love. God is not a magistrate who requires evidence. He is a Father who already sees what is in the heart before the heart can form it into a word. As I told Samuel in that moment: *He sees what is in your heart when you can't even see it.* This is not cheap grace; this is the mercy of a God who is not fooled by performance and is not satisfied by it either. The exchange about the Orthodox monk and the Catholic priest points to something I want to draw out further, because it is easy to hear that moment as mere theological comparison and miss what the Spirit was actually pressing. Samuel noted that both traditions represent deep maturity in their own streams, yet each is focused on a different region of theology. I agreed, and then I said something I want to hold up to the light: that you have to come to the whole corpus of Scripture — the whole thing — alone with the Father, empty, willing to be filled with something that will make your chest hurt and make you burn. I am more convinced of this than almost anything else I have said publicly or privately. The canon of sixty-six books is not a repository of proof texts for pre-existing denominational conclusions. It is a living unity — a σῶμα (sōma, SO-mah), a body of witness — and it must be read as a body, not as a collection of supporting arguments for inherited positions. Both the errors I identified — praying through the rosary and an unhealthy sin consciousness — arise from the same root: reading a portion of the canon in isolation from the whole, and then allowing that portion to govern the entire framework of relationship with God. I want to dwell for a moment on the Christological meditation that arose from Samuel's question about moral failure and whether God would punitively punish him. Because what I said in that exchange was not a small thing dressed in casual language — it was a concentrated theology of the atonement. I said that God is not a punitive person; He desires mercy more than sacrifice (Hosea 6:6, Matthew 9:13). And then I traced the logic of the Incarnation: that the Son emptied Himself — and here the Greek term is critical — κένωσις (kenōsis, keh-NO-sis), the self-emptying of divine prerogative, the voluntary laying aside of the independent exercise of omnipotence in order to enter fully into human limitation. He became the sinless man, and because He had no beginning or end, His priesthood is eternal. And here is the pivot point that so much of the church misses: He is not a Levitical priest. He is a priest according to the order of Melchizedek, which is to say, a priest without genealogical qualification, without temporal limitation, without the need for annual atonement for His own sins, because He has no sins. This is the argument of Hebrews 7 in its full force. And then — and this is the image I shared with Samuel that I want to amplify — John in the Revelation (Revelation 5:5–6) is told to behold the Lion of Judah. He turns to look and sees a Lamb that has been slain, standing. Not a sequence. Not a lion who later became a lamb. Both simultaneously. The Lion-authority and the Lamb-sacrifice are not two phases of the same career; they are two dimensions of the same eternal identity. He is the High Priest and He is the sacrifice. He is the one who enters the Holy of Holies and He is the blood that makes entry possible. He is the one with all authority in heaven and on earth (Matthew 28:18) and He is the one whose wounds are still visible in His resurrection body (John 20:27). Any theology of God's severity that forgets the Lamb distorts His character. Any theology of God's tenderness that forgets the Lion does the same. The moment in the conversation about Samuel's exchange with the brother who challenged his podcast presence is, I believe, one of the most theologically loaded moments in the entire call, and I want to treat it with appropriate weight. Out of the abundance of the heart, the mouth speaks (Luke 6:45). When I wrote back to that brother, I did not write a rebuttal. What came through was a benediction wrapped in a rebuke wrapped in a blessing: *May His searching gaze burn away the dross until all that is reflected is Him, and the peaceable fruit of His righteousness clothe us.* The brother had nothing left to say. And Samuel recognized immediately what happened: the debate was won before it started, not because of rhetorical skill, but because the center of my testimony was not me. The moment someone challenges you for sharing the Word of Christ as though the platform is yours, they have already revealed where their center of gravity is. They have made the testimony about territory, and territory is not the Kingdom. The Kingdom has no territorial owners. It has servants. What I said next to Samuel is one of the deepest currents running through this conversation: *Deception is almost always dressed as holy.* I said this in the context of Job, and I want to press it further. Job was the most righteous man alive by God's own testimony (Job 1:8). And what undid him — what required God's rebuke from the whirlwind — was not sin in the traditional sense. It was the frame of his righteousness. *My righteousness was just mine. I was righteous.* Job had centered the narrative of his own holiness rather than recognizing that righteousness is a gift, a robe, a clothing that comes from outside the self. When God answered Job from the whirlwind — *Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?* (Job 38:4) — He was not being cruel. He was dismantling the only form of deception that could have persisted in a man that clean: the deception of self-sourced identity. This is identical to Moses's error at the rock. And it is identical to the error of the brother who policed the podcast space. Each of them was doing something that looked righteous from the outside. Each of them had made themselves the adjudicator of something that belongs only to God. And then there is the spiritual warfare dimension of the conversation, which I want to name plainly. I told Samuel directly: the fact that this young woman is distracting him right now, in the season where he needs to be in the quiet place where the Father can speak to his heart, is not merely a relational complication. It is literal warfare. I said it with a word that I want to make explicit here: just as God commissions and assigns and sends people, the enemy does too. There are children of darkness (Luke 16:8, John 8:44) who are sent on assignments, and the distinguishing mark of their approach is often that it arrives exactly at the point of greatest vulnerability, and it arrives with warmth and flattery and physical invitation. I am not saying the young woman is conscious of her role in this. I am saying that the timing matters, the vulnerability matters, and the distraction matters. Samuel is in a season of divorce proceedings, identity formation, and growing spiritual depth. This is precisely when the enemy attempts to install a substitute intimacy — something that will not require the depth of surrender that God requires but will produce enough counterfeit warmth to fill the space where hunger for God should be growing. The conversation ended with something I said that I want to preserve as its own meditation seed: *There's something to tell you, but I don't know what it is yet. It's getting jumbled with things. All things that are true, but there is only one you need to hear.* I believe this is the condition of many who carry a prophetic gift in this season. There is a fullness pressing. There is more in the reservoir than the current conversation can receive. And the discipline is not to pour all of it at once but to wait — to discern which of the true things is the one the other person needs in this moment. This is itself a form of consecration. Not everything that is true is what is needed right now. And the willingness to hold what is true in reserve, trusting that the Father will create the moment for its release, is one of the marks of a maturing prophetic gift. --- ## 🤔 Implications and Challenges The first thing this conversation challenges is the modern evangelical tendency to relocate spiritual life exclusively into designated sacred spaces — Sunday services, Bible studies, prayer meetings. The most theologically loaded moments in this transcript happened on a factory floor, on a pallet line, in a corner by a window, and in the margins of a phone call interrupted by grinding machinery. If we have trained ourselves to only recognize God's activity in formally religious contexts, we will miss the vast majority of what He is actually doing. The disciples did not receive their most formative instruction in the Temple. They received it on roads, in boats, at meals, and in the middle of storms. The second challenge is to the idea that theological discussion requires credentials or a platform. Samuel spent six hours talking about God with a man from Zimbabwe on a pallet line. That conversation — whatever its limitations — may have been one of the most genuinely apostolic exchanges either of them had that week. The modern church has professionalized theology in ways that have silenced the priesthood of all believers, making the people dependent on the credentialed class rather than equipped to encounter Scripture and Spirit directly. This is not a small administrative problem; it is an ecclesiological distortion with deep roots. Third: the conversation presses hard on the question of punitive theology. I stated clearly that God is not a punitive person, and I traced that claim through the kenōsis (keh-NO-sis) of the Incarnation and the eternal priesthood of Melchizedek. This is not cheap grace. Moses was not punitively punished, but he bore a real consequence for misrepresenting God's character. The distinction matters enormously for discipleship: believers shaped by punitive theology of God live in fear-based compliance rather than love-based obedience. This produces the kind of Christianity that is brittle under pressure, performative under observation, and hollow at the center. Fourth: the rebuke I received from the brother who challenged my podcast presence — and the response that came through — models something worth naming clearly. The appropriate response to spiritual pride in another is not counter-pride. It is a benediction. It is a response so rooted in the Word that the other person has nothing left to argue against, because you have not argued at all. You have simply reflected the light back. The peaceable fruit of righteousness (Hebrews 12:11) is the fruit of discipline received from the Father, and it shows up most visibly when we are challenged by those who are supposed to be on the same side. Fifth: the warning about deception dressed as holiness deserves to land heavily in the contemporary charismatic and prophetic communities especially. The most dangerous deceptions in the Body of Christ right now are not coming dressed as obvious sin. They are coming dressed as deep theology, apostolic authority, prophetic assignment, and spiritual maturity. The moment a brother uses the vocabulary of the Kingdom to protect his own territory, enforce his own platform, or adjudicate another's faithfulness to God, he has already stepped into the very deception he might be warning others against. The self-righteous intercessor, the proprietary theologian, the platform-guardian — these are not villains from outside the church. They are the church's own Job-temptation: doing the right things in the wrong frame. Sixth: the conversation about sleep, intercession, and the will of God in the night hours is a word for many in this season. I said it plainly: the worst thing you can do if the Spirit of God is keeping you awake is to take a melatonin and go to bed. This is not a medical prescription. This is a posture statement. The Spirit of God is not obligated to work around our sleep schedules. The watchman who insists on his rest at the moment of incoming alarm is not a watchman. He is a sleeper. This does not mean all insomnia is God. But it does mean that saints who have cultivated the practice of listening in the night should not be too quick to silence the signal. --- ## 🚀 What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** What this conversation calls me toward — and what I want to call every beloved brother and sister toward — is the practice of reading the whole canon, alone with the Father, willing to be burned. Not a portion of it through a denominational lens. Not the favorite passages that confirm what we already believe. The whole σῶμα (SO-mah) — the whole body of witness, from Genesis to Revelation — allowed to interpret itself, allowed to press against the places where our theology is comfortable and self-serving. Spiritual maturity in this season looks like a willingness to have one's inherited frameworks dismantled and rebuilt from the foundation of the text, in relationship with the Spirit who inspired it. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this calls forth is the obedience of the servant who goes where he is sent without requiring a dignified posting. A factory corner by a window. A pallet line. A crackling phone connection in a noisy industrial space. These are the posts God assigns to those He is training. The response of Kingdom obedience is not to wait for a more suitable environment but to intercede, to testify, to listen, and to speak the one true thing that the Father identifies out of all the true things available. And when a brother challenges the testimony, to respond with a benediction rather than a rebuttal. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this conversation models is one of hiddenness combined with readiness. I am behind a mask at a factory, interceding for the world, thinking about the scroll rolling back. Samuel is on a pallet line, spending six hours speaking about God with a man from Zimbabwe. Neither of these looks like what the world expects prophecy to look like. But this is exactly how the remnant operates in the age of the Church's wilderness — consecrated in hiddenness, fruitful in the margins, holding the signal even when the service is poor. --- ## 🔥 A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who stands in the gap between heaven and earth, as a servant of the Word and a co-heir of the covenant sealed in the blood of Your Son — the Lamb who is also the Lion, the sacrifice who is also the High Priest, the eternal Melchizedek who ever lives to make intercession. I thank You for brothers like Samuel — for the conversations that happen in the noise, in the margins, between calls and factory shifts and pallet lines, where You are doing the truest and deepest work of formation. Burn away every frame of self-sourced identity in us. Let no man's righteousness be his own, and let no woman's gift become her banner rather than Your glory. Where the enemy has sent an assignment against my brother in his season of vulnerability, I stand in agreement with Your purposes over his life and declare that the ground of his heart belongs to You — not to comfort, not to distraction, not to counterfeit intimacy. I ask for the spirit of the watchman to rest on every saint in this remnant who is being kept awake in the night hours: let them not silence the signal. Let them not take the melatonin when You are sounding the alarm. And over every voice that would use the vocabulary of the Kingdom to guard a territorial claim, let the searching gaze of Your holiness burn away the dross, until all that is reflected is You alone, and the peaceable fruit of righteousness clothe us — Your servants, Your prophets, Your witnesses — for the King who is the same yesterday, today, and forever. Amen. --- ## 📝 Scripture References & Original Language Notes | Passage (NKJV) | Original Language | Theological Insight | | --- | --- | --- | | Ezekiel 33:7 — "Son of man, I have made you a watchman for the house of Israel" | עֹפֶה (tsopheh, tso-FEH) — watchman / lookout | The watchman's post is assigned by God, not self-selected; the location of the post is irrelevant to its validity | | Hebrews 7:15–17 — "in the likeness of Melchizedek there arises another priest… according to the power of an endless life" | Μελχισέδεκ (Melchizedek, mel-kih-SEH-dek) — king of righteousness / priest without genealogy | Christ's priesthood is not Levitical but eternal — He atones without needing atonement for Himself | | Revelation 5:5–6 — "Behold, the Lion of the tribe of Judah… And I looked, and behold, in the midst of the throne… stood a Lamb as though it had been slain" | ἀρνίον (arnion, ar-NEE-on) — a little lamb / the slaughtered one | The Lion and the Lamb are not sequential identities but simultaneous dimensions of one eternal Person | | Philippians 2:7 — "but made Himself of no reputation, taking the form of a bondservant" | κένωσις (kenōsis, keh-NO-sis) — self-emptying / voluntary laying aside of divine prerogative | The Incarnation was the voluntary limitation of omnipotence in order to accomplish redemption from within human flesh | | Job 38:4 — "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?" | אַיֵּה (ayyeh, ah-YEH) — Where?
