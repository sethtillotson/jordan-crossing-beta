# 07-28 Reflection: Grief, Marriage, and Finding God in Ordinary Moments **Title:** 07-28 at 22:31 — The Weight We Carry While Life Keeps Moving **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listen back to this conversation with my wife, I am struck by how tenderness and dread, laughter and lament, all crowded into a few short minutes. We spoke of a loved one gravely broken — seventeen fractures, a wounded spine, an uncertain future — and in nearly the same breath we spoke of coffee, sour soup, and the punishing heat of a smoke-veiled sun. Something in me recognizes that this is exactly how sorrow arrives in a real life: not in solemn isolation, but braided into the ordinary. **Why This Matters:** This matters because discipleship is not lived in a chapel where all distractions fall silent, but in the marketplace, the parking lot, the hot walk home while carrying grief and a child in the womb at once. If I only know how to meet God in curated stillness, I will not know how to meet Him when the news is bad, the body is tired, and the errands still demand attention. The Spirit is teaching me that faithfulness is forged precisely in these mingled moments, where love for the suffering and love for the living must both be honored without either being abandoned. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation Read naturally, this is simply a couple managing logistics under stress. A friend is critically injured far away; travel is costly and dangerous; a pregnant wife is anxious, overheated, and irritable; a husband weighs whether visiting two people at once justifies the gas and the risk. The worldly mind would file this under coping — gallows humor, the coffee craving, the sour-soup longing all functioning as pressure valves that keep two people from being overwhelmed. It is a reasonable, even compassionate reading, and it is not wrong as far as it goes. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind can only see survival where the Spirit sees a sacrament. It notices the deflection of humor but not the mercy hidden in it; it registers the argument about the drive but misses the covenant tension underneath — the ancient pull between guarding the household and honoring the wounded. Left to itself, human reasoning treats grief as a problem to be minimized and love as a cost to be calculated. It cannot perceive that the very ordinariness of this exchange is where God is quietly present, nor that a decision about a five-hour drive is really a question about what kind of person the Kingdom is forming me to be. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with this, the first thing that arrests me is my wife’s cry — “Adi is so unwell… seventeen fractures and his spine is affected. He might not be able to move again.” There is no theology in that sentence, only wound. And yet Scripture never asks us to spiritualize away such a cry. Jesus Himself, standing at Lazarus’ tomb though He knew resurrection was coming, still “groaned in the spirit and was troubled” and “wept” (John 11:33, 35). The Spirit is pressing on me that lament is not a lapse of faith; it is often faith’s first honest breath. I notice how quickly we moved from the tragedy to the coffee, and my instinct was almost to be embarrassed by that pivot. But I am seeing now that this is not shallowness — it is the frailty of the earthen vessel God chose to fill. Paul says we hold treasure “in earthen vessels, that the excellency of the power may be of God and not of us” (2 Corinthians 4:7). The mingling of grief and mundane craving is not a failure of holiness; it is the very texture of the clay God delights to dwell in. Something in me softens when I hear us laughing about the coffee that once wrecked us both, the coffee we now cannot live without. Even in dread, we reached for shared memory, for the small liturgy of a marriage. The Preacher wrote, “Two are better than one… for if they fall, one will lift up his companion” (Ecclesiastes 4:9–10). I hear in our banter the sound of two people lifting each other, not with grand words but with the ordinary grace of being known. Then comes the tension I cannot ignore — the drive. My wife pleaded, “Just stay right now… it’s not worth it.” And I answered, almost as prophecy, “It’s never going to be worth it… The world isn’t getting any better… but you know what’s worth it? Friends and family.” As I revisit my own words, I feel the Spirit confirming something deeper than I intended in the moment. The calculus of the world will always tell us to wait for a better time to love. That time never comes. I keep returning to that phrase — never worth it — because it exposes a lie that governs so much of modern life: the belief that presence must be justified by return on investment. But the Kingdom runs on a different economy. The Samaritan did not weigh the cost of the inn against the stranger’s worthiness; he simply “had compassion” — the Greek σπλαγχνίζομαι (splanchnizomai, splangkh-NID-zoh-my), to be moved in the inward parts, a gut-level mercy (Luke 10:33). Love that waits for a favorable exchange rate is not yet love. And yet the Spirit will not let me make my wife the villain of caution. Her fear is not faithlessness; she carries a child, and her protectiveness is its own form of love. Here two goods press against each other — the call to visit the broken and the call to guard the vulnerable within our own tent. This is the real weight of discernment. Wisdom is not choosing love over safety or safety over love; it is holding both before the Lord until He gives the ordering. I notice, too, how the heat and the smoke keep intruding — the sun that hides and returns, the wildfire haze that makes the day deceptive. My wife said it perfectly without knowing it: “one second there’s shade, and then the next second there’s the sun again.” I am seeing that this is a parable of the whole conversation, of the whole season. The light is unreliable to our senses right now, veiled by the smoke of a world on fire. James reminds me that with the Father “there is no variation or shadow of turning” (James 1:17). Even when my perception flickers, His light does not. The water, too, preaches to me. I grabbed the purified when she wanted the spring — and she said the purified “is making me more thirsty.” How like the soul’s longing. There is a water that is technically clean but cannot satisfy, and there is living water welling up. Jesus told the woman at the well, “whoever drinks of the water that I shall give him will never thirst” (John 4:14). In the middle of complaining about bottled water, we were unknowingly confessing the deepest human ache — a thirst nothing filtered or purchased can quench. Even the sour soup she craved speaks. In pregnancy, the body reaches for sharp, tart, living flavors, for what awakens the tongue. I hear in it a hunger for something real amid a numbing day. And I think of how the Kingdom often comes not as sweetness but as something bracing — the salt that has not lost its savor, the sharp word that cuts to the dividing of soul and spirit (Hebrews 4:12). God is not always comfort; sometimes He is the sour that revives. What the Spirit is ultimately unveiling is that this entire exchange — bad news, coffee, argument, heat, water, soup — is holy ground I nearly walked past. “Surely the LORD is in this place, and I did not know it,” said Jacob (Genesis 28:16). I did not know it either. I thought it was just a phone call. But God was in the mingling, teaching me to love the wounded, cherish the wife, discern the drive, and thirst for the only water that satisfies — all at once, all in the ordinary. ## Implications and Challenges I offer these gently, first to my own heart. None of this is condemnation; it is invitation. **The idol of the “right time.”** Much of Western Christianity has absorbed a quiet gospel of convenience — we will serve, visit, give, and go when it is affordable and safe. But if love is only extended when it is efficient, it may not be love at all. The challenge is to ask whether I am waiting for a worthiness in circumstances that the Kingdom never promised. **Sanitizing grief.** We are often uncomfortable when sorrow and normal life collide, as if laughing while grieving is hypocrisy. Yet Scripture holds space for “a time to weep, and a time to laugh” (Ecclesiastes 3:4) — sometimes in the same hour. The challenge is to stop policing the shape of lament and to let it be as human as God made it. **Treating presence as transaction.** When we calculate gas, risk, and payoff before showing up for the broken, we reveal how deeply the market has colonized our love. The challenge is to recover the reckless mercy of the Samaritan, who counted no cost. **Mistaking caution for weakness — or love for recklessness.** My wife’s protectiveness is not faithlessness, and my desire to go is not automatically obedience. The challenge is humility: neither spouse holds the whole picture, and both must be laid before the Lord. **Drinking purified water.** So many of our comforts are technically clean but leave us thirstier — entertainment, consumption, curated spirituality. The challenge is to stop settling for what filters out impurity but cannot give life, and to return to the Living Water. I end this list as I began it: with grace. These are not failures to be ashamed of but doorways the Spirit is opening. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity is learning to meet God in the mingled hour — to grieve and laugh, to fear and trust, to plan errands and intercede, without splitting my life into “spiritual” and “ordinary.” It is letting the flickering light of a smoke-veiled day drive me back to the Father in whom there is no shadow of turning. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience here looks like refusing the tyranny of “worth it.” It calls me to weigh the drive not by gas and danger alone but by love, and then to submit even that decision to the Lord and to my wife in mutual honor, seeking the wisdom that holds compassion and prudence together rather than sacrificing one. **For Prophetic Posture:** This trains me to watch for God in the unremarkable — to discern the parable hidden in bottled water and sour soup, to recognize holy ground in a parking-lot phone call. The prophetic posture is not only foreseeing great things; it is seeing the present rightly, naming God’s nearness in the very moment I was tempted to rush past it. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come as Your servant and Your son, standing between heaven and earth to make agreement with Your will. I thank You that You dwell in earthen vessels and are not ashamed of the mingled clay of our days. I lift up Adi, broken and afraid — You who bind up the brokenhearted, be nearer to him than his own breath, and let Your resurrection life reach even his wounded spine according to Your mercy. Guard my wife and the child she carries; give her cool shade in the heat and living water for her thirst. Grant us wisdom about the road ahead — let neither fear nor bravado decide, but only Your Spirit. Teach me to love without waiting for it to be worth it, to grieve without shame, to laugh without guilt, and to find You in every ordinary moment I once walked past. To You be all glory, in the name of Jesus Christ come in the flesh, amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |---------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------| | John 11:33, 35 | ἐμβριμάομαι (embrimaomai, em-bri-MAH-oh-my) — to groan deeply | Jesus’ lament sanctifies honest grief even where faith is fully present. | | 2 Corinthians 4:7 | ὀστράκινος (ostrakinos, os-TRAH-kee-nos) — made of clay/earthen | God’s power is displayed through, not despite, our fragility. | | Luke 10:33 | σπλαγχνίζομαι (splanchnizomai, splangkh-NID-zoh-my) — to be moved in the inward parts | Kingdom love is gut-level mercy, uncalculated by cost. | | John 4:14 | ὕδωρ ζῶν (hydōr zōn, HOO-dor ZONE) — living water | Only Christ’s gift quenches the thirst no purchased water can satisfy. | | James 1:17 | παραλλαγή (parallagē, pah-rah-lah-GAY) — variation/change | The Father’s light does not flicker even when our perception is veiled. | | Ecclesiastes 4:9–10 | — | Companionship is God’s provision for lifting one another in the fall. | | Genesis 28:16 | — | God is often present in the very place we fail to recognize as holy. |
