# 08-12 Call Reflection: Intercession for a Family in Medical Crisis and Grief **Title:** 08-12 at 15:56 — When the Lungs Fail and the Fever Lingers: Interceding in the Valley **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I hung up the phone with Tasha, I sat in a strange silence — the kind that follows when you have just been handed a weight that is not your own to carry alone. A trach in Andy’s throat, sedation to quiet his agitation, a fever no one can explain, and beneath it all, Diamond gone to lymphoma while Andy lies unaware. The Spirit pressed something into me: this is what intercession looks like when it is not tidy, when suffering compounds and there is nothing to fix — only Someone to bring it all to. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ was never designed to endure suffering in isolation, nor was intercession ever meant to be a polished performance of religious language. It matters because in this brief call, I witnessed the raw architecture of what it means to stand in the gap for a family under siege — a husband tethered to machines, a wife absorbing grief she cannot yet share, and a beloved companion lost. To learn how to pray in seasons like this is to learn the priesthood of Christ Himself, who “ever lives to make intercession” (Hebrews 7:25). If we cannot love and lift one another in the valley, our faith is merely theory. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation Viewed through natural reasoning, this is simply a sequence of unfortunate medical and emotional events. A man’s respiratory system is failing, requiring surgical intervention and chemical sedation to manage his distress; an unexplained fever suggests possible infection; a pet’s death from cancer adds emotional strain to an already-taxed caregiver. The compassionate secular response would be to offer sympathy, perhaps practical help, and to acknowledge Tasha’s resilience — “You’re doing good besides that” — as an admirable coping mechanism. Prayer, in this frame, functions as emotional support: comforting, perhaps, but ultimately a gesture that soothes the one praying more than it changes anything measurable. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind, for all its sober assessment, stops at the surface of the visible. It can chart oxygen saturation and fever curves, yet it cannot perceive the unseen war being waged over a sedated man’s body, nor the ministry of a wife’s quiet strength as something Spirit-sustained rather than merely psychological. It hears “I have prayer groups all over the world praying for you” as a kind gesture, unable to grasp that the prayers of the saints rise as incense before the throne (Revelation 5:8) and that heaven bends toward the cry of the afflicted. The flesh sees a hospital room; the Spirit sees a battleground and a mercy seat. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I listened to my sister describe Andy’s failing lungs, something in me recognized the profound symbolism the Spirit weaves through the very organ that is failing. It is the breath — the נְשָׁמָה (neshamah, neh-shah-MAH) — the breath of life that God breathed into Adam’s nostrils, making him a living soul (Genesis 2:7). When a man’s lungs will not work with him, when a trach must be cut into the throat simply to keep breath moving, I am reminded how utterly our very respiration is a moment-by-moment gift. We do not sustain ourselves. “In Him we live and move and have our being” (Acts 17:28). I notice that the throat and the breath are also the seat of speech, and Tasha said something that arrested me: “I wasn’t able to tell him nothing.” Andy cannot yet receive the news of Diamond’s death because he cannot yet bear it. There is a mercy here that mirrors the Lord’s own dealing with us — Jesus told His disciples, “I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now” (John 16:12). The Spirit withholds not from cruelty but from tenderness, waiting until we are able to hear. Tasha’s instinct to protect her husband from further stress is itself an echo of divine patience. As I sit with this, the fever that “they’re not quite sure what it is from” speaks to me of the hidden things — the afflictions whose source remains veiled to the physicians. Scripture knows such mysteries. Job sat covered in boils with no diagnosis that could satisfy his friends’ theories, and the answer, when it came, was not an explanation but the presence of God Himself. Sometimes the Lord does not tell us the origin of the fever; He simply says, “I am with you.” Our task is not always to know the cause but to bring the sufferer to the One who holds all causes in His hand. I am seeing that this call carried a compounding of griefs — the husband in sedation, the fever unexplained, and Diamond lost to lymphoma, cremated in solitude while Andy sleeps unaware. Tasha bears these layered sorrows, and yet she says, “I am doing good besides that.” I do not hear denial in this; I hear the strange grace given to those who walk through valleys. The psalmist did not say he felt no fear in the valley of the shadow of death, but that he would “fear no evil, for You are with me” (Psalm 23:4). The rod and the staff — instruments of both correction and rescue — are what comfort in the dark. Something deepens in me here regarding the ministry of presence over the ministry of answers. I offered Tasha no solutions. I could not heal Andy’s lungs or explain his fever or restore Diamond. What I could offer was this: “I’ve been praying for you guys.” The Spirit is pressing on me that intercession is not weakness or last resort — it is the highest priestly calling. When Aaron stood between the living and the dead with his censer, the plague was stayed (Numbers 16:48). The one who prays stands in that same gap, between heaven’s mercy and earth’s affliction. I marvel that I was able to tell her, “I have prayer groups all over the world praying for you.” This is the mystery of the one Body — that a family in a hospital room is upheld by saints they will never meet, scattered across nations, joined in the same Spirit. This is the communion of saints made tangible. Paul wrote of being “present in spirit” with the Corinthians though absent in body (1 Corinthians 5:3), and the prayers of the global Church weave an unseen net beneath the falling. Distance is nothing to the God who fills all things. The Spirit is also showing me the significance of my counsel to withhold the news of Diamond. “No, I wouldn’t until he gets out of there. Don’t cause any more stress.” There is a wisdom of seasons here — an understanding that grief has its appointed time. Ecclesiastes declares “a time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance” (Ecclesiastes 3:4). To force mourning upon a man fighting for breath would be to burden him beyond what love permits. Even sorrow must be stewarded in its proper hour. And I cannot escape the tenderness woven through the whole exchange — how Tasha, in the midst of her own crushing week, thought of Andy’s grief before her own, saying of Diamond, “I wasn’t able to tell him nothing.” Her sorrow is doubled: she grieves Diamond alone, and she carries the future grief Andy will feel when he wakes. This is cruciform love, the love that “bears all things” (1 Corinthians 13:7), the love that suffers for the sake of another. I saw Christ in her, even in a phone call cut short by another incoming call. For the call ended abruptly — “can I call you back? Jules called me” — and even that ordinary interruption reminded me that life continues to press its demands upon the grieving, that the valley does not pause to let us catch our breath. Finally, I am seeing that the very brevity and messiness of this conversation is itself a testimony. Real intercession does not happen only in cathedrals or extended prayer vigils. It happens in the interrupted phone call, the two-minute check-in, the “I’ve been praying for you” spoken before someone has to run. The Lord receives these fragments and makes them whole. Our God is not too grand for a quick call between two burdened saints; He inhabits it. ## Implications and Challenges I offer these reflections gently, aware that we are all learning to love better in seasons of trial. - **We often mistake fixing for loving.** The modern instinct — especially in a culture obsessed with solutions and control — is to feel useless when we cannot solve a problem. Yet this call reveals that faithful presence and steadfast prayer are not the consolation prize when we cannot fix things; they are the substance of Christlike love itself. - **We rush the grieving toward premature “victory.”** Shallow teaching sometimes pressures the suffering to declare healing before they have been permitted to lament. But Scripture makes room for the unexplained fever, the unanswered question, the withheld news. There is holiness in saying, “We’re not quite sure,” and still trusting God. - **We privatize suffering that was meant to be shared.** Western individualism tempts us to bear our burdens alone and to be embarrassed by our need. Yet the reality of prayer groups across the world upholding one family exposes how false that isolation is. We belong to one another. - **We underestimate the ministry of the interrupted moment.** We may think meaningful ministry requires uninterrupted time and eloquent words. But the Spirit works through the brief, the ordinary, the abbreviated call. Do not despise the small window of grace given to speak love. - **We forget that timing is an act of love.** Withholding hard news from a man who cannot yet bear it is not dishonesty — it is discernment. Love knows not only *what* is true but *when* the truth can be received. May we grow in the grace to simply stand with one another, censer in hand, in the gap. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This changes how I measure faithfulness. Maturity is not the ability to resolve every crisis but the willingness to remain present, prayerful, and tender when there is nothing to fix. I am learning to sit in the valley without demanding the exit. **For Kingdom Obedience:** This calls forth active, specific intercession — not vague well-wishing but the deliberate lifting up of Andy’s lungs, his unexplained fever, Tasha’s compounded grief, and the eventual gentle telling of Diamond’s passing. It calls me to follow through on “I’ve been praying for you” until the answer comes. **For Prophetic Posture:** This trains me to watch and wait with the afflicted, to discern the seasons of grief, to withhold and to speak as the Spirit directs, and to testify that the God who gives breath has not abandoned the one whose breath is failing. It teaches me to abide in the gap between heaven and earth as a faithful servant. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before Your mercy seat as one of Your servants, joining my voice with the saints scattered across the earth who are lifting this family before You. You who breathed life into Adam’s nostrils, breathe now into Andy’s failing lungs; let the very breath You gave be sustained by Your hand. Search out the hidden source of the fever that the physicians cannot name, for nothing is veiled from You. Uphold Tasha in her doubled grief — the loss she carries alone and the sorrow she shields her husband from — and let her quiet strength be revealed as Your Spirit’s own sustaining. Grant wisdom for the appointed hour when hard news must be spoken, and let it be received in a season of peace and not of stress. I thank You that You ever live to make intercession, and that You have made us a kingdom of priests to stand in the gap. Into Your faithful hands I release this family, trusting that You are already present in that hospital room, breathing peace where machines labor. In the name of Jesus Christ, come in the flesh, risen and reigning, I pray.
