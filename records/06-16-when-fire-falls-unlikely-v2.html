# 06-16 Call Reflection: Faith, Entrepreneurship & Covenant Brotherhood **Title:** 06-16 at 08:58 — When the Fire Falls on Unlikely Ground **Recorded:** 2026-06-16 08:58:55 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother on this call, something in me recognized a moment that was far larger than its surface appearance — two men talking shop, talking numbers, talking about a sales call seven minutes away. But underneath all of that, I was watching something holy move. The fire my brother was describing was not the fire of human ambition alone, and I knew it even as the words were flying. Something arrests me here: God was in this exchange, and neither of us was pretending otherwise. **Why This Matters:** It matters because the Body of Christ is in desperate need of a theology of *ordinary holy moments* — the kind where the Spirit shows up inside a business call, inside a conversation about conversion rates, inside the admission that you are still catching yourself with cigarettes, inside the first stirring of a calling returning after eight months of silence. If we only look for God in sanctuaries and formal prayer times, we will miss the vast majority of the terrain where He is actually working. Discipleship is not a Sunday posture — it is a continuous recognition of the One who inhabits all of life, and this call, as brief and scattered and profane as parts of it sounded, was a living testimony to that truth. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind would look at this conversation and see exactly what it appears to be on the surface: two friends hyped up before a sales call, celebrating a good conversion metric, encouraging each other, talking about business momentum and pricing strategy. A psychologist might describe it as social reinforcement — two people in a mutually motivating relationship feeding each other’s confidence before high-stakes performance moments. A marketing analyst would zoom in on the numbers: 482 views, \$10.37 per view at a \$5,000 close, and call it a strong funnel. The reference to smoking, to getting one’s life together, to the arc of five days — the natural mind files these as background noise, personal color, emotional texture. Nothing more. The financial momentum is the story, and the rest is just conversation. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind cannot perceive the thin places — the moments where heaven and earth press close together inside the utterly mundane. It has no category for what it actually means when a conversion rate “defies every natural marketing metric,” because it has no theology of divine favor resting on a man who is still imperfect, still mid-journey, still catching himself in old habits and yet ablaze with something that cannot be explained by effort alone. The natural mind would call the numbers lucky. It would call the fire enthusiasm. It would call the five-day arc a good week. It cannot see a book of remembrance being written in real time by the finger of God, because it does not believe that God writes in such places. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sat with this call after it ended, what rose in me first was not theological analysis — it was simple wonder. My brother said, *“I am on fire,”* and I believe him, not because of the sales metrics but because of the *fruit*. The numbers were extraordinary, yes — but what struck me more was the confession sitting right next to the triumph: *“I’m still catching myself smoking and shit.”* In most conversations, that admission would be buried or avoided. Men in the middle of winning don’t typically stop to name where they’re still losing. And yet there it was, offered openly, without defense. That is not a small thing. That is the signature of a heart that is genuinely being worked on by Someone greater than itself. This brought me immediately to the nature of repentance — μετάνοια (metanoia, meh-TAH-noy-ah) — which in its truest New Testament form does not mean a single dramatic reversal but rather a *continuous reorientation of mind and direction*. The English word “repentance” has been so compressed by revivalist usage that it often conjures the image of a moment — an altar call, a tearful prayer, a decisive break. But metanoia (meh-TAH-noy-ah) is a word of ongoing motion. It is a mind perpetually turning toward God, perpetually being renewed, perpetually catching itself and redirecting. When my brother said, “I’m catching myself,” he was describing μετάνοια (meh-TAH-noy-ah) in its most honest and living form. That itself is the work. That itself is evidence of the Spirit’s hand. I said something in the call that I want to return to and press deeper: repentance does not happen all at once. It is a lifelong journey. This is not a concession to moral laxity — it is a statement of how sanctification actually operates. Paul himself wrote to the church at Philippi with absolute confidence about God’s ongoing work: *“being confident of this very thing, that He who has begun a good work in you will complete it until the day of Jesus Christ”* (Philippians 1:6, NKJV). The word “complete” there carries the sense of fully carrying out, fully accomplishing — but notice that it describes something *in motion*, something not yet finished, something that will reach its fullness only at the day of Christ. My brother is in the middle of that arc. So am I. And neither of us is disqualified by the middle. What I also noticed — and this is important — is that the fire was not contingent on perfect behavior. The anointing was not withheld because of the cigarettes. The conversion rate was not adjusted downward because of ongoing struggle. This mirrors one of the most disorienting truths in all of Scripture: that God’s gifts and calling are *irrevocable* (Romans 11:29, NKJV). χαρίσματα (charismata, kah-REEZ-mah-tah) — the grace-gifts — are not merit-based allocations that God withholds until the recipient cleans up every area of life. They are expressions of His covenant faithfulness, flowing from His character rather than our performance. This is what makes grace scandalous to the religious mind and liberating to the honest heart. Then there was the moment I shared about having titled my own recent meditation “Thank You, My Friend” — and what I said about it carries a theological weight I want to unfold carefully here. I described feeling like an invitation into the upper room, and I tried to distinguish between God’s universal love and His particular fondness for certain hearts. This distinction is real and it is scriptural, though it must be handled with care. God is no respecter of persons in the sense that His covenant is available to all who will come (Acts 10:34). And yet the Scriptures testify repeatedly to a peculiar intimacy that certain souls are drawn into — Moses, who spoke with God *face to face as a man speaks with his friend* (Exodus 33:11); David, called *a man after God’s own heart* (1 Samuel 13:14); John, who called himself the disciple whom Jesus loved. The Hebrew word חֵן (chen, KHEN) — favor, grace, delight — captures something of this: God delights in particular people in ways that go beyond mere universal benevolence. It is not favoritism in the worldly sense. It is the mystery of divine intimacy. When Jesus said to His disciples in that upper room, *“No longer do I call you servants, for a servant does not know what his master is doing; but I have called you friends”* (John 15:15, NKJV), He was issuing an invitation into a category of knowing that the servant relationship cannot produce. A servant obeys without understanding. A friend is let into the *why*, into the counsel, into the heart. And when I titled that meditation “Thank You, My Friend,” I was responding to exactly that — the sense that in the last five days, I had been let into something of the divine counsel. Not elevated above my brothers. Not granted special status that separates. But drawn *closer into understanding* in a season that demanded it. The upper room is not a place of pride. It is a place of breaking bread and receiving truth that is too heavy to carry alone. The reference to a *book of remembrance* is one I want to honor deeply. This phrase appears in Malachi 3:16: *“Then those who feared the LORD spoke to one another, and the LORD listened and heard them; so a book of remembrance was written before Him for those who fear the LORD and who meditate on His name.”* The context of Malachi 3 is remarkable — it is written against a backdrop of cynicism, of people saying that serving God is useless, that the arrogant prosper and the wicked go unchallenged. And into that cynicism, God says: I am keeping records of the conversations of those who fear Me. Every word spoken in reverence. Every exchange between faithful friends. Every moment of honest testimony. None of it is lost. The call I just had with my brother — with all of its profanity, its excitement, its fumbling toward holiness — I believe it was heard. I believe it was noted. Not because we were perfectly composed, but because we were genuinely seeking, genuinely grateful, genuinely in awe of what God was doing. There is something else in this call that the Spirit will not let me pass over: the return of calling. I said that today, for the first time in what felt like eight months, I felt the call to come back to entrepreneurship. The way I said it was quiet, almost offhand, sandwiched between my brother’s business details. But I have learned that the moments we speak the most significant things most quietly are often the moments the Spirit marks most carefully. Eight months is not nothing. Eight months of silence from a particular calling is a season — a season of hiddenness, of preparation, of what the desert fathers called *apophasis*: the stripping away of what is not essential so that what remains is purely of God. Elijah sat under the juniper tree. Moses spent forty years in Midian. Paul went into Arabia. The return of a calling after a season of apparent dormancy is not a coincidence. It is a resurrection pattern. And I noticed the grace in my brother’s response: *“Well the good news is I’m already on it.”* He did not make me feel late. He did not make me feel like I had missed something unrecoverable. He simply said: we are covered, we are moving, come and join what God is already doing. This is the right posture among covenant brothers — not shame for the seasons of hiddenness but joy at the re-emergence. The Body of Christ functions this way when it is healthy: those who are carrying the weight do so with gladness, and those who are stepping back into their place are received without condemnation. The final image I keep returning to is the number itself — 482 views, \$10.37 per view, closing at \$5,000. I ran those numbers through an AI tool and stared at what came back, because no natural marketing framework could explain that ratio. I said it defies every known metric. And I believe what is happening underneath that math is something the ancient Israelites would have recognized immediately: divine multiplication. It is the logic of five loaves and two fish. It is the logic of the widow’s oil that did not run out. It is the logic of a jar of flour and a jug of oil that sustained a prophet and a widow through a famine. God has never been impressed by adequate resources. He is most visible when the resources should not be sufficient but somehow are. My brother’s numbers are a testimony — not just to good sales skill, though that is real — but to the multiplication principle of the Kingdom, where what is consecrated and offered in faith does not behave according to natural law. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation raises is the one most uncomfortable for religious culture: the question of *where God shows up*. There is a persistent and unexamined assumption in much of modern Christianity that the sacred and the secular occupy separate zones — that God is present in worship services and prayer meetings, and that business conversations, sales calls, and commercial activity are spiritually neutral at best, corrupting at worst. This call dismantles that assumption. The fire my brother described was burning in the middle of a seven-minute window before a business call. The book of remembrance I described was being written across five days of ordinary life. If we maintain a sacred/secular divide in our actual lived theology, we will miss the vast majority of where God is moving — and we will produce disciples who are holy on Sundays and orphaned the rest of the week. The second challenge is the modern Church’s truncated theology of repentance. If μετάνοια (meh-TAH-noy-ah) is reduced to a one-time event at an altar rather than a continuous reorientation of life, we produce two unhealthy extremes: those who believe they must be fully sanctified before God can use them, and those who use grace as a license to remain comfortably unchanged. My brother’s honesty — *“I’m still catching myself”* — is not spiritual weakness. It is spiritual health. The man who cannot name where he is still being worked on is the man who has stopped paying attention to the Spirit’s movement in his own interior life. Honest self-awareness before God is not shame. It is the beginning of ongoing metanoia (meh-TAH-noy-ah). The third challenge is to those who interpret seasons of hiddenness as divine abandonment or personal failure. The eight months of silence I described around entrepreneurship are a real experience — and they are not unique to me. Many in the Body of Christ are in seasons right now where a calling has gone quiet, where a door that was once open seems closed, where the fire that once burned with obvious intensity has reduced to something harder to name. The Scripture does not call these seasons punishment. It calls them wilderness — and the wilderness in Scripture is consistently a place of preparation, of intimacy, of stripping down to what is essential. Deuteronomy 8:2 frames the wilderness explicitly: *“And you shall remember that the LORD your God led you all the way these forty years in the wilderness, to humble you and test you, to know what was in your heart.”* The wilderness reveals what is in the heart. It is not the end of the story. The fourth challenge is to the prosperity mindset that would take the extraordinary numbers in my brother’s business as evidence of personal blessing alone. The Kingdom multiplication principle is real — but it is not a formula for personal accumulation. It is always, in Scripture, connected to purpose, to mission, to the feeding of others, to the advancement of something larger than the individual. The widow’s oil was for sustaining a prophet and preserving life through famine. The loaves and fish were for the feeding of thousands who had nothing. The financial breakthrough my brother is experiencing carries a stewardship question embedded in it: *what is this multiplication for?* That question is not a guilt trip. It is the question every person walking in unusual favor must hold with open hands. The fifth challenge is to covenant brotherhood itself. What I experienced in that call — a brother who celebrated without competing, who carried the weight without resentment, who received my re-emerging calling with joy rather than critique — is rarer than it should be among men who claim to follow Christ. The enemy knows that the isolation of gifted men is one of his most effective strategies. Two men walking in mutual accountability, genuine transparency, and covenant joy are a dangerous spiritual combination. The Church needs more of these conversations — not polished, not perfectly composed, but genuinely honest before God and one another. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This meditation calls us to expand our theology of location — to genuinely believe that God inhabits our ordinary moments as fully as He inhabits our extraordinary ones. Spiritual maturity is not the ability to sound holy in religious settings. It is the ability to recognize the holy in the mundane and respond with reverence. It also calls us to hold repentance as a living posture rather than a past event — to be people who are perpetually catching ourselves and perpetually turning back, without shame and without complacency. **For Kingdom Obedience:** If you are in a season of hiddenness around a calling, the obedience is not to manufacture momentum but to remain faithful in the small and to be ready when the stirring returns. The return of calling after a long silence is a signal to step back in — not with frantic catching-up energy, but with the calm confidence of one who knows that God’s timing is not late. And if you are in a season of unusual multiplication and favor, the obedience is to steward it with open hands and an open question: *what is this for, Lord?* **For Prophetic Posture:** Prophetic people keep books of remembrance — not for self-aggrandizement, but because testimony is one of the primary weapons of the Kingdom. Revelation 12:11 names it clearly: *“they overcame him by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony.”* What God has done in the last five days, the last eight months, the last season of your life is not private information meant only for your comfort. It is ammunition for the warfare of faith. Record it. Speak it. Let the saints hear it and be strengthened. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You with gratitude that overflows into wonder — because You are not waiting for us to be finished before You move on our behalf. You showed up in a seven-minute window before a sales call. You wrote into the record a conversation between two imperfect men who love You and are still becoming. You multiplied what should not have multiplied, and You let the numbers bear witness to Your faithfulness where only Your faithfulness can explain the math. I thank You for the gift of covenant brotherhood — for men who carry the flame when another is in a season of quiet, and who receive the returning without judgment. I bring before You every beloved brother and sister who is in a season of hiddenness right now, who is catching themselves in old places, who is wondering if the calling has passed them by — and I declare over them in agreement with Your Word that what You have begun, You will complete, that Your gifts and calling are without repentance, and that the wilderness You have led them into is not abandonment but preparation. Let the book of remembrance continue to be written. Let the fire that cannot be explained by natural metrics burn for Your glory and for the multiplication of Your Kingdom purposes. Let the upper room invitation — *friend, not servant* — settle into every heart that has been striving to earn what You have already freely given. We are not late. We are not disqualified. We are right where You said we would be. May all of it — every call, every number, every admission of still-catching-ourselves, every returning stirring — be to the praise of Your name.
