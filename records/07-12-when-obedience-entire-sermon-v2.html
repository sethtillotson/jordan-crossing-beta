# 07-12 Call Reflection: Obedience, Prophetic Hunger, and Church Discernment **Title:** 07-12 at 14:06 — When Obedience Is the Entire Sermon **Recorded:** 2026-07-12 14:06:42 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation Something stopped me mid-conversation today — not a theological argument, not a vision, not even a particularly dramatic moment, but a quiet word from the Holy Spirit after I had already left the building. I had gone to a church this morning at His direction, sat through a service I didn’t feel fed by, and walked out wondering why He sent me there at all. And then, on the way to the grocery store to buy a watermelon, He told me: *I sent you there so that you would obey.* That was it. That was the whole point. The act of going *was* the sermon. **Why This Matters:** In a season where so many believers are measuring every spiritual experience by what they received, what they felt, how high the worship went, or whether the message was relevant — the Lord is still training some of His servants in the most ancient school of all: the school of simple obedience. If we can only follow when the destination is exciting, the fire is obvious, and the reward is immediate, we are not yet walking by faith. We are walking by appetite. What I encountered this morning — both in the church and in the word the Spirit spoke afterward — is a direct confrontation with how deeply the modern Christian mind has been shaped by consumerism rather than covenant. This matters for every believer who is hungry for something real, because the hunger itself must be submitted before it can be sanctified. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, my morning looks unremarkable at best and somewhat disappointing at worst. I visited a church that seemed theologically sound but culturally misaligned with my background. The sermon was basic. The worship didn’t stir me. I didn’t connect with the congregation over shared cultural touchstones like football or American social life. I left early. By any measurable standard of spiritual consumer satisfaction, the visit was a miss — and the rational response would be to simply mark that church off the list and keep searching for a better fit, a more engaging preacher, a community whose cultural vocabulary matches mine. Even the conversation with my brother afterward had that natural undercurrent: we are both evaluating institutions, assessing ROI, deciding what is worth investing in and what needs to be let go. The natural mind applies those same metrics to spiritual community — what am I getting out of this, is this worth my Sunday morning, does this serve my growth? **The Limitation:** But the carnal mind, however sincere it may be, cannot perceive the architecture of divine pedagogy. It sees a dull church visit; the Spirit sees a training ground for surrender. It sees an absence of resonance; the Spirit sees the beginning of a hunger that will outlast every comfortable substitution. The wisdom of God does not always announce itself with fire and confirmation — sometimes it hides itself inside an act of simple obedience that looks, from the outside, like nothing at all. There is a dimension of the Kingdom that can only be entered through the narrow gate of *doing what He said because He said it* — and that gate does not widen to accommodate our preferences or our expectations of what the experience should feel like. The natural mind will always mistake the container for the content, and when the container is institutional and familiar and slightly boring, it will conclude there is nothing in it. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I am sitting with the simplicity of what happened this morning, and I find it genuinely arresting. The Holy Spirit directed me to a specific church — not the larger one I was also considering in Moorhead, but this one. I obeyed. And then I spent most of the service quietly puzzled, waiting for a reason, asking Him inwardly why He had sent me there, and receiving no answer during the service itself. It wasn’t until I was already in the car, already moving toward the ordinary errand of picking up a watermelon for my wife, that the word came: *I sent you there so that you would obey.* I keep returning to the timing of that. He waited until I had already left, already finished the obedience, before He explained it. That sequencing is not incidental. This is the pattern of Abraham leaving Ur. Hebrews 11:8 says he “went out, not knowing where he was going.” The destination was not revealed before the departure. The confirmation came *through* the movement, not before it. And what Scripture is actually celebrating in that verse is not Abraham’s theological understanding — it is his unquestioning motion in response to a voice. The writer of Hebrews does not say Abraham went out because he understood. He went out because he believed. There is a profound difference between those two things, and the modern Western church has largely collapsed that distinction, teaching people to expect understanding before obedience rather than understanding through it. I also notice something significant in the way the Spirit confirmed the assignment through Ephesians. I had fallen asleep the night before listening to a four-hour reading of that letter — not studying it, not preparing for anything, simply resting in it — and then the sermon this morning was drawn from the opening chapters of Ephesians. The Spirit was layering. He was building a quiet confirmation that was only visible in retrospect. This is how He often works with those He is training prophetically: He plants a seed in the night season, and the next day He points to the blossom without announcing it. The confirmation is for the one with eyes tuned to watch for it. The book of Ephesians itself is not incidental here. Paul opens that letter from prison — from a place of institutional confinement, paradoxically — and writes some of the most expansive, liberated theology in the entire canon. He writes about being “blessed with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ” (Ephesians 1:3) from a Roman cell. That letter is not produced by comfort. It is produced by a man who has learned, as he writes in Philippians 4:11, to be content in whatever state he is in, which is a learned discipline — the Greek there is μεμάθηκα (memathēka, meh-MAH-thay-kah), meaning *I have been taught through experience*. Contentment is not inherited. It is schooled into us through exactly the kinds of unremarkable, unglamorous obedience moments I walked through this morning. What I witnessed in that church — and I want to hold this carefully because I have genuine love for those brothers and sisters — was the fruit of institutionalism. The word that keeps rising in me for what I observed is the Greek κατάρτισις (katartisis, kah-TAR-tee-sis), which means the equipping or complete furnishing of the saints — the very thing Paul describes in Ephesians 4:12 as the purpose of the fivefold ministry. What I saw was a form of equipping that was programmatic rather than pneumatic. There was a prayer corner. There were candles. There were sticky notes for heavy burdens. There was a commissioning center. All of these things have their place in the life of a congregation, and I hold no contempt for any of them. But the Spirit was showing me the difference between a structure that *contains* ministry and a living body through which ministry *flows*. One is institutional; the other is incarnational. What I am hungry for is a prophetic ministry — not performance prophecy, not the showmanship of calling out names and zip codes — but the genuine operation of the Spirit of prophecy that can stand in a room full of people and hear what God is saying *specifically*, not merely generally. The prophetic dimension I looked for in that service felt constricted, as though the Spirit were present but the room had not been arranged to receive what He wanted to do. I am not saying He was absent. I am saying He was not unconstrained. And there is a difference. The Scripture says in Amos 3:7 that “the Lord God does nothing unless He reveals His secret to His servants the prophets” — and where the prophetic voice is suppressed or domesticated, the congregation is left with good information about God but not necessarily a living encounter with Him. The conversation with my brother Samuel touched on this same dynamic in a different register. He spoke about letting go of a team member who was not yet independent enough to carry his own weight in the work — someone who needed to be paid for every small task, who couldn’t yet be trusted to run unsupervised. And my brother said something that struck me: *I don’t need a multiple-man team to get to where I’m going right now.* I heard the Spirit whispering an ecclesiastical parallel: there is a season before the multiplication in which the Lord is teaching the leader to move lean, to move in dependence on Him rather than on systems, to prove the ministry before He expands it. That same principle governs the life of a church planter. You do not begin by assembling the team. You begin by hearing the voice. The twelve came *after* Jesus had already been about the Father’s business for thirty years. There is also something holy in the small provision I gave my wife today — the watermelon, the simple act of care in the midst of a busy afternoon — and I do not want to pass over it without naming it. Samuel’s Lord provided for him when he was stranded and out of fuel. My Lord directed me to go to a specific church and then revealed the purpose on the way to pick up groceries. The divine is present in the mundane. This is the testimony of Scripture from beginning to end — burning bushes beside ordinary flocks, angels at evening meals, resurrection announced to a woman coming to perform a burial. The sacred does not stand apart from the ordinary. It inhabits it. I find myself more hungry now than I was before I walked into that church. That hunger is itself a gift. Before this morning, I was searching abstractly. Now I am searching specifically. I know what I am not looking for. I know what I *am* looking for. I want to be planted in a community where the Spirit is not merely invited as a guest to the program but governs the program entirely — where the preacher can stand up and hear from the Lord about specific people in the room, where κατάρτισις (kah-TAR-tee-sis) is not managed but manifest. And if such a community does not exist in proximity to where I am living, then the conversation I have been having for months — the prophetic word from multiple voices telling me about my own ministry, my own calling to plant and gather — takes on fresh weight. I am not in a hurry to declare what that means. I am not ready to plant anything today. But I am no longer dismissing the pattern. When the Spirit sends a person to church after church and they find in each one the same institutional ceiling, that is not evidence that the church is broken beyond use — it may be evidence that the person is being made into something the existing structures were not built to contain. Moses did not find his commissioning inside Pharaoh’s palace or even inside the Hebrew camp. He found it in a wilderness, beside a bush that burned without being consumed. There are times when the absence of fit is itself the preparation for calling. ## Implications and Challenges The instinct to measure a church visit by what *I received* is deeply embedded in contemporary Christian culture, and this morning’s experience challenges that at the root. The consumer posture — even when dressed in spiritual vocabulary — approaches Sunday morning as a transaction. I come with needs; the church meets them; the exchange is successful or unsuccessful. But the Lord’s word to me today reframes the entire question. He did not send me there to be fed. He sent me there to obey. Until we are willing to show up in places that cost us our comfort and return us nothing visible, we have not yet graduated from spiritual consumerism into covenantal discipleship. There is a subtle pride that can masquerade as prophetic discernment. I must name this honestly: the feeling that I could give a better sermon than what was preached, that I already know the Greek words they are introducing, that the congregation’s cultural touchstones feel foreign to me — these observations may be accurate in some measure, but they carry a spiritual danger. Discernment that ends in comparison rather than intercession has drifted from the Spirit’s purpose. What the Lord gives us sight to see in a congregation, He gives us so that we can pray — not so that we can position ourselves above. The modern church’s discomfort with the prophetic is not simply a matter of theological disagreement. It is, in many cases, a trained institutional reflex — a management of the Spirit designed to protect the program from disruption. But the New Testament church was, from its very birth, a disrupted institution. Pentecost looked like chaos to those on the outside. The early gatherings described in 1 Corinthians 14 were apparently so unpredictable that Paul had to give instructions on how to order the prophetic flow — not eliminate it, but order it. The problem the contemporary church has largely solved by simply removing the prophetic from the service is not a problem Paul solved the same way. He said, “do not forbid to speak in tongues” and “do not despise prophecies” (1 Thessalonians 5:20), and he said it *precisely because the temptation to do both was already present in his day*. My brother Samuel’s testimony about his own church situation — holding genuine appreciation for his congregation while recognizing the distance between where he is spiritually and where the preaching lands — is a shared tension among those who are growing quickly under the direct tutelage of the Spirit. This is not a reason for arrogance. It is a reason for humility and patience. The brother who has been given much is responsible for much — including the responsibility of remaining submitted, teachable, and present in community even when community is imperfect. The danger for the prophetically gifted is isolation dressed as discernment. The answer is not to leave every church the moment it fails to meet us where we are, but to remain long enough to ask what the Lord is building in us through the friction. And yet — and I hold both of these things at once — there is a legitimate season of *searching*. Not every institutional church is the Lord’s answer for every believer. There is a specificity to spiritual community that the Scriptures honor. Paul left behind cities where the ground was not yet ready. He shook the dust. He moved. The Spirit led him *away* from Asia and toward Macedonia in Acts 16, redirecting the mission not because Asia was evil but because the kairos was elsewhere. I believe the Lord may be doing something similar in my searching right now — not shaking me away from the Body of Christ, but positioning me toward the specific expression of it that He has prepared for this season. The word from my brother that has stayed with me: *If it’s meant to be, it will be — if not, then you should start one.* I am not receiving that as pressure. I am receiving it as prophetic trajectory, already confirmed by multiple witnesses across multiple months. The Lord does not waste the hunger He creates. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Obedience before understanding is not a beginner’s discipline — it is the mark of mature faith. The believer who will only move when the destination is clear, the reason is revealed, and the reward is visible has not yet learned to walk with God in the way the patriarchs walked. Maturity means I can follow the voice into an unremarkable room and trust that if the Spirit sent me, the purpose is real even when it is hidden from me until I am already walking away. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Where the Spirit says *go*, I go. Where He says *wait*, I wait. And when I arrive somewhere that does not immediately satisfy, I do not immediately interpret discomfort as misdirection. I sit, I watch, I pray, and I ask Him what He is building. If a church is not where He is planting me, that does not mean He did not send me there — it may mean He sent me there to show me what I am hungry for, so that I pursue it with greater intentionality and less passivity. **For Prophetic Posture:** Those who carry prophetic sensitivity must be especially careful not to let the gift become a lens for judgment. The prophetic mantle trains us to see — but what we do with what we see reveals whether the gift is maturing in love or calcifying into superiority. I am being trained to see the gaps in the modern church not so that I can document them from a distance, but so that I am prepared to steward something that fills them. That stewardship begins with intercession. It matures toward planting. And it is always, always grounded in the humility of one who knows that he himself is still being formed. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as a son still under Your patient, deliberate training — grateful that You speak, grateful that You direct, and grateful that You sometimes withhold the explanation until I have already finished the obedience. What You are doing in this season is not small, even when it looks unremarkable from the outside. You are tuning my ear to Your voice. You are showing me the difference between a church that knows about You and a community that moves with You. You are whittling me down to the thing I am actually called to carry, and You are doing it gently — through a watermelon errand, through a sticky-note prayer wall, through a four-hour Ephesians recording in the night, through a friend’s voice crackling over a phone call while he’s tired and short on sleep and full of faith anyway. I receive all of it as formation. I trust You with the hunger You have lit in me. I trust You with the ministry You have been speaking over me through many voices. I ask that You would continue to lead — specifically, carefully, and without ambiguity — toward the community You have prepared, or toward the courage to plant what is not yet there. May every act of obedience, no matter how unglamorous, be acceptable in Your sight as worship. May the hunger never become pride. May the discernment never become contempt. And may everything You are building in this season be built to last, rooted in the apostolic foundation, alive in the prophetic current, and saturated in the love that covers the whole Body. We agree with what You are doing, Lord — between heaven and earth, in the seen and the unseen, in the Church You are purifying and the servants You are raising. To You be all glory.
