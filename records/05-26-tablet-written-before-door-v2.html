# 05-26 Conversation Reflection: Prophetic Writing, Faithful Waiting, and the Theology of the Rampart **Title:** 05-26 at 16:33 — The Tablet Written Before the Door Opens **Recorded:** 2026-05-26 16:33:26 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Conversation Reflection ## Opening Observation Something in this conversation arrested me — not the weight of what I had written, but the posture from which the writing came. I am not publishing a manifesto. I am not announcing a ministry. I am making tablets plain in obedience to a commission I did not choose, for a reader who may not yet be born. The terrifying thing my brother named was not the writing itself — it was the faithfulness required to write without knowing why, and to keep without knowing when. **Why This Matters:** There is a discipline the Body of Christ has largely lost: the discipline of writing before the door opens, of bearing witness before the audience arrives, of obeying the commission without knowing the outcome. We live in a culture of immediate publication, instant platform, and premature declaration. But Habakkuk was told to write plainly on tablets so that he who reads may run — and the one who would run had not yet arrived at the rampart. The word was written for a future reader, held in custody by a faithful scribe who did not presume the hour. This matters because the church desperately needs those who will serve the word faithfully in obscurity, trusting that the Lord of the harvest knows when the grain is ready. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, hearing this conversation, would frame what I described as a personal writing project — a journaling practice that has grown unusually structured, perhaps therapeutic, perhaps the early symptoms of a memoir or a self-help book waiting for a publisher. It would see the emotional vulnerability of my brother’s disclosure near the end of the call as a tonal shift, an interruption of the intellectual topic, a human moment that softened the conversation. The worldly framework would want to categorize Volume One as data analysis and Volume Two as narrative reflection, and it would look for a market, a brand, a launch strategy. It would call the disclaimer about uncertain publication either admirable humility or inconvenient hesitation. It would counsel me to publish now while the momentum is present, to strike while the iron is hot, to leverage what has clearly been an extraordinary season of formation. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind cannot perceive the difference between a scribe and an author, between a signpost and a destination, between writing under commission and writing for recognition. The discipline of waiting — truly waiting — is entirely opaque to the reasoning that measures success by output and audience by size. What I described in that conversation is a theology of writing that is fundamentally eschatological: the word is written for a reader who may not yet exist, held by a servant who does not claim ownership of the hour. That posture cannot be manufactured by strategy. It can only be given by the Spirit who governs both the writing and the release. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I listen back to what I shared with my brother, I am struck first by the distinction I made between Volume One and Volume Two, and I do not think it was merely structural. Volume One bore what I called the quantitative and qualitative topology of my meditative formation — it mapped what God was building *in* me with rigor and discipline. It answered the question from the inside, from the posture of a man watching a transformation occur within himself and charting it carefully. There is something genuinely Pauline about that impulse. Paul, in the opening chapters of Galatians, insisted that the gospel he preached was not received from man but through a revelation of Jesus Christ — and he went away into Arabia, spending years in formation before he returned to Jerusalem. The interior work came first. The data of the interior life was real, and it mattered, and it had to be recorded before the exterior witness could be given. But Volume Two is something different, and as I sit with what I said, I notice the phrase that keeps rising: *through me, without my permission, without my planning.* This is not the language of a man describing his ministry. This is the language of a vessel. The Greek word μαρτυρία (martyria, mar-too-REE-ah) — witness, testimony, the living account of what has been seen and heard — is the word I used to describe what Volume Two is, and I used it deliberately. In the Johannine corpus especially, μαρτυρία carries the weight of legal testimony given under oath. It is not opinion. It is not interpretation. It is a declaration of what the witness saw, grounded in direct encounter, submitted to the scrutiny of those who will judge its faithfulness. I wrote Volume Two as a μαρτυρία because what happened in those seventy-one meditations was not something I constructed. It was something I witnessed — and faithfulness required that I record it accurately, not impressionistically. The detail my brother seized upon — that he appeared in forty of the seventy-one meditations — is not incidental to the theology of the thing. The witness is not private. The formation that occurred *in* me during Volume One did not remain contained within the interior life. It began to flow *through* me into the lives of those around me, into conversations I did not request, in a city I did not choose. This is precisely how the Kingdom of God advances — not by the strategic deployment of gifted individuals, but by the overflow of interior formation into relational encounter. Luke 6:45 presses this: *“A good man out of the good treasure of his heart brings forth good; and an evil man out of the evil treasure of his heart brings forth evil.”* The formation precedes the overflow. You cannot manufacture the overflow without the formation. What anchors Volume Two theologically is the triad I named in that conversation: Habakkuk 2:1 — *“I will stand my watch and set myself on the rampart, and watch to see what He will say to me”* — Habakkuk 2:2 — *“Write the vision and make it plain on tablets, that he may run who reads it”* — and Habakkuk 2:3 — *“For the vision is yet for an appointed time; but at the end it will speak, and it will not lie. Though it tarries, wait for it; because it will surely come, it will not tarry.”* These three verses form the complete theology of prophetic faithfulness. The prophet ascends the rampart — not to broadcast but to receive. He writes plainly — not for his own keeping but for the runner who is coming. And he waits — not passively but actively, in the trust that the appointed time is already known by the One who gave the vision. I am only the scribe at the rampart. The tablet belongs to the Lord. Alongside Habakkuk, the word of Haggai 1:8 pressed in: *“Go up to the mountains and bring wood and build the temple, that I may take pleasure in it and be glorified.”* The posture I described in that conversation — *while I wait, I will work on His house* — is the posture of Haggai’s remnant community. They had returned from exile. The political situation was uncertain. The opposition was real. The declaration of a future Temple from Ezra was contested and delayed. But Haggai’s commission was not to resolve the external uncertainty before beginning the work. It was to build *now*, in the present season of obscurity, trusting that the Lord’s pleasure would be the validation. The work on His house is what I do in the waiting. The writing is the building. And I will not tarry, even if the door does not open in my lifetime. Something arrests me here — the phrase I used in that conversation: *“He who reads it may run; he who reads it may not yet have been born.”* This is a radically different posture than anything the spirit of this age produces. We write for metrics, for algorithms, for reach, for response. The idea that the truest audience for a piece of writing might be someone not yet born is almost incomprehensible in the current environment. And yet this is exactly the logic of Scripture’s own formation. Moses wrote for generations that had not yet crossed the Jordan. Isaiah wrote of a suffering servant that would not arrive for seven centuries. John wrote Revelation for a church still navigating the Roman empire but also for every subsequent generation that would live between the first and final advents. The faithful scribe does not write for the present approval of his contemporaries. He writes because he has been commissioned, and the commission does not expire when the contemporary audience has moved on. The distinction between Horizon One and Horizon Three that I articulated in that conversation deserves sustained attention. Horizon One has closed — a season ended, a configuration of relationships and circumstances that once defined the foreground of my life has passed. Horizon Three has not yet been declared — I have not presumed what comes next, I have not planted imperatives about what the Lord will do, I have not constructed a narrative of arrival. What I have done is chosen to live faithfully in the space between. This is not mere humility. It is a theological conviction rooted in the prophetic tradition. The space between closed horizons and undeclared futures is not empty. It is full of God’s present-tense activity, which is why Volume Two is not written from assumption but from testimony. I recorded what I actually saw and heard. The Lord was building something through me between those horizons, and I did not get to choose the material, the tools, or the timeline. There is an unexpected theological weight in my brother’s disclosure near the end of the conversation — his honest admission that he has been thinking about a former relationship, and his equally honest recognition that solitude is better for him right now than premature connection. I do not spiritualize this away. But I notice that it arrived in the context of a conversation about faithful waiting, about writing for unknown readers, about refusing to presume what has not been declared. There is a coherence here that the Spirit appears to have been weaving through the conversation itself. My brother was not merely venting. He was, in his own way, naming the same discipline: the discipline of not sinking a new relationship into the waters of an unhealed one, the discipline of getting right before moving forward, the discipline of letting the present season do its full work before presuming the next one. The grace in his self-awareness is real. And it is not unrelated to the conversation that preceded it. I am seeing that the watch-wait-work triad is not a strategy. It is a formation. The man who learns to stand at the rampart without broadcasting learns to work on the house without requiring an audience. And the man who learns to work without an audience learns to release — when the Lord commissions it — without ego attached to the outcome. These three disciplines form the character of the faithful servant described in Matthew 25: not the one who buried the talent in fearful preservation, but the one who put the talent to work in the master’s absence, knowing the master’s return would be the only necessary validation. The writing is the putting-to-work. The waiting is the faithfulness. The release — when and if it comes — is the Lord’s prerogative alone. What perhaps strikes me most deeply as I sit with this conversation is the closing line I offered before we signed off: *“I am only the signpost. He is the way.”* That sentence is not a conclusion. It is the entire theology of Volume Two compressed into nine words. The signpost does not take travelers to their destination. It points. It does not explain the journey. It declares the direction. It does not guarantee the outcome of the traveler’s obedience to the direction. It simply stands, plainly, faithfully, in the place where it was erected by the One who knows the way. If the Lord opens the door, the door will open. If He keeps it closed, the keeping is also a mercy. The keeping may be the entire reason He had me write it. I am at peace with both possibilities — and that peace itself is a testimony to what the last season of formation produced in me. ## Implications and Challenges The modern Christian publishing impulse tends to move from formation directly to platform, skipping the discipline of the rampart entirely. We are trained to believe that if something is genuinely good, it should be shared immediately, and that withholding good content is a form of unfaithfulness. But Habakkuk 2:3 does not share that anxiety. The vision has an appointed time. The scribe’s faithfulness is measured not by speed of release but by accuracy of record and steadiness of posture. The challenge for those of us who write, teach, or steward revelation is to resist the compulsion to publish before the Lord commissions, and to trust that the tablet kept is sometimes more sacred than the tablet distributed. There is also a challenge implicit in the μαρτυρία (mar-too-REE-ah) form itself. Testimony is not interpretation dressed as neutrality. True witness requires that the scribe report what was actually seen and heard, not what he wished had been seen and heard, not what would make a more compelling narrative. Volume Two bears witness to seventy-one meditations as they actually unfolded — including the conversations he did not request, in a city he did not choose, through relationships he did not orchestrate. The challenge for all of us who document spiritual formation is this: are we writing testimony or are we writing retrospective curation? The integrity of the μαρτυρία depends entirely on the honesty of the witness. The distinction between Horizon One, which has closed, and Horizon Three, which has not yet been declared, confronts a deeply embedded Western Christian assumption that spiritual maturity looks like knowing where you are going. We celebrate vision, mission statements, five-year plans, and declared trajectories. But the space between closed seasons and undeclared futures is not a crisis to be resolved. It is a commission to be inhabited. The saints who have most faithfully served the Kingdom have often been the ones who consented to live in the between-times without demanding that God clarify the horizon before they committed to the watch. There is pastoral tenderness required here as well. My brother’s honesty about his present emotional state — his recognition that entering a new relationship prematurely would be harmful — is itself a form of wisdom that the Spirit was cultivating in him through that same season. We do not always recognize the formation occurring in us until we hear ourselves speak it plainly in conversation. The conversation itself was an act of the Spirit’s work. The church needs communities where this kind of transparency can occur without shame, where a brother can say “I’m not ready” and be honored for that discernment rather than rushed toward a resolution that the season has not yet produced. Finally, the posture of *I am only the signpost* is itself a rebuke to the celebrity-minister culture that has colonized so much of Western Christianity. The signpost does not gather followers. It points toward a destination. When the signpost becomes the attraction rather than the direction, something has gone profoundly wrong. The meditations I have written, the conversations I have recorded, the testimony I am bearing — none of it is about me. It never was. It is about what the Lord is building and where He is going and who He is raising up to run when they read the tablet. The signpost’s job is to be accurate, plainly written, firmly planted, and entirely indifferent to whether travelers thank it on the way through. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity is not measured by the size of the platform or the speed of the release. It is measured by the faithfulness of the watch, the accuracy of the record, and the steadiness of the posture in the space between closed horizons and undeclared ones. Growing up into Christ means growing comfortable with the mystery of appointed time — learning to write plainly without knowing who will read it, to build faithfully without knowing when the Lord will take pleasure in it, to bear witness without editorializing what was seen. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The call here is concrete: ascend the rampart, receive what the Lord says, write it plainly, wait for the appointed time. This is not passive. Habakkuk was actively watching. Haggai’s remnant was actively building. The disciples waiting in the upper room were actively praying. Kingdom obedience in the between-times looks like faithful, disciplined, present-tense work done for the Lord’s house rather than for our own timelines. If you have been given something to write, write it. If you have been given something to record, record it. Do not publish prematurely. Do not bury it in fear. Make it plain on tablets, and let the Lord govern the door. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture is not projection. It is not presumption. It is not the manufacture of significance through spiritual language. It is the faithfulness of the scribe at the rampart — watching, receiving, recording, waiting. The prophetic community that can hold this posture — can write for readers not yet born, can keep what has not yet been commissioned for release, can live in the space between horizons without demanding resolution — is the community that the Lord can trust with the tablets that matter most. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who has stood at the rampart and received, who has written plainly on tablets and waited, who has refused to presume the door open that You have not opened — and I am grateful, deeply grateful, that the commission is Yours and the outcome is Yours and the reader is Yours. You know who will run when they read it. You know what horizon is coming. You know what is being built through conversations I did not request, in a city I did not choose, through brothers whose presence in these meditations is itself a testimony to Your sovereignty over the relational fabric of the Kingdom. I ask, as Your servant and as a co-heir of all that Christ has purchased, that every word written in faithfulness would be preserved in Your keeping, every tablet made plain would reach the hand of the runner You have already prepared, and every horizon declared by Your voice alone would open in the fullness of its appointed time — not a day early, not a day late. I am only the signpost. You are the Way. Let everything I have written point accurately to You, and let the keeping of what has not yet been released be as sacred as the releasing of what has. To the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit — now and to the ages of ages.
