# 07-27 Reflection: Deliverance, Mercy, and the Streets Where Thomas Walks # Title: 07-27 at 19:20 — Deliverance, Mercy, and the Streets Where Thomas Walks **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I sat with a long night’s worth of conversation with my brother — a wild story of reckless drunkenness and a stranger’s erratic mercy, a 3 a.m. anxiety attack met by an unnamed helpline worker’s steady voice, and finally a late-night call with Brother Samuel where confession, grace, and intercession for a homeless man named Thomas intertwined into something holy. Three moments, one thread: the Lord’s providence moving through chaos, crisis, and compassion alike, often through vessels that never once spoke His name. **Why This Matters:** This matters because so much of the Christian life is not lived in dramatic moral collapse or mountaintop clarity, but in these gray, chaotic, half-comic, half-desperate nights where discernment is dulled, anxiety spikes, and love for the broken costs us something real. My brother is not a stranger to the faith — he walks alongside Nigerian Christian friends, knows better, yet found himself carried by a stranger’s unstable arms one hour and steadied by a stranger’s calm voice the next. Samuel and I, in turn, found ourselves confessing compromise and interceding for a man neither of us can save by our own strength. Watching over one another in these moments — with grace, not judgment — is part of what it means to be members of one body, bearing one another’s burdens and speaking truth in love before harm arrives, or naming God’s hidden mercy after it has already passed. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural standpoint, this whole night reads as a string of loosely connected episodes: a wild story young men tell each other with adrenaline still in their voices, a textbook anxiety attack managed through basic clinical psychoeducation, and two friends processing stress, guilt, and compassion fatigue over a homeless man’s addiction. The natural mind interprets the reckless car ride as adventure, the helpline call as sound behavioral science doing its job, and the prayers over Thomas as well-intentioned but ultimately powerless words spoken into a phone past midnight. It reads survival as proof of harmlessness, coping tools as merely mechanical, and compassion for the unreachable as sentimentality or a boundary problem — codependency, perhaps, or ordinary burnout dressed in spiritual language. **The Limitation:** What the natural mind cannot easily perceive is how thin the line was between comedy and catastrophe in that car, how a body’s cry for breath at 3 a.m. was quietly overlapping with the Spirit’s own ancient pattern of ministering to the exhausted, and how Thomas’s bondage is not merely psychological but spiritual — a soul in chains choosing, again and again, not to be free. The flesh measures a night by whether it ended in laughter, a crisis by whether the technique worked, and compassion by whether it changed the outcome. It cannot see that stepping into a stranger’s car under the influence, or dialing a helpline in a panic, or aching over a man on the street are all threads in the same tapestry — one where providence, not wisdom, was the only thing standing between recklessness and tragedy, and where the invisible war being waged over a man’s will can only be perceived by those with eyes trained to see beyond the visible transaction. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I listened to my brother recount the first part of his night, I noticed how much of it hinged on getting into a car driven by someone clearly unfit to drive it — intoxicated, “coked up,” erratic, yet strangely, insistently generous. There is a strange mercy operating even here, an unearned kindness extended by someone in no position to be trusted, and I found myself struck by how often the Lord’s providence covers us in the very places we should not have gone. Scripture speaks plainly about the vessel we are called to steward. Paul writes, “Or do you not know that your body is the temple of the Holy Spirit who is in you, whom you have from God, and you are not your own? For you were bought at a price; therefore glorify God in your body and in your spirit, which are God’s” (1 Corinthians 6:19–20). The night my brother described was not evil in intention — it was reckless in practice, a temple carried carelessly through streets thick with danger, entrusted for an hour to a driver who did not even trust himself with a straight line. I noticed too the imagery of the stranger insisting, “I take your home, I take your home” — a phrase repeated almost like liturgy, garbled but persistent, one that could have belonged to someone with far darker intentions. Scripture warns believers to “walk circumspectly, not as fools but as wise, redeeming the time, because the days are evil” (Ephesians 5:15–16). The word translated “circumspectly” is ἀκριβῶς (akribōs, ah-kree-BOSE) — exactly, carefully — carrying the sense of walking with precision, watching one’s steps as one who knows the ground may not be safe. My brother’s night reveals what it looks like when that watchfulness is set aside for the sake of a story worth telling later, even as the presence of his Nigerian Christian friend MJ — strong, joyful, sober that night — stood as a quiet signpost pointing toward another way of living, even if unheeded in the moment. There is a deeper irony the Spirit surfaced as I meditated further: the drunken stranger, in his own disordered way, showed hospitality — he refused payment, tried to help, showed an almost childlike desire for connection (“You’re my best friend, man”). Even in brokenness, the image of God flickers. Yet I cannot ignore that generosity flowing from intoxication and instability is not a foundation to build a life upon. Proverbs warns, “Wine is a mocker, strong drink a brawler, and whoever is led astray by it is not wise” (Proverbs 20:1). This is not condemnation of my brother, but illumination — the entire night was built on impaired judgment stacked upon impaired judgment, and only the sovereign kindness of God kept it from becoming tragic rather than comedic. James asks the sobering question, “What is your life? It is even a vapor that appears for a little time and then vanishes away” (James 4:14). The laughter in my brother’s voice as he told this story was real and good — but beneath it, the Spirit invited me to recognize how fragile that laughter’s continued existence actually was. As the conversation continued, the tone shifted — my brother told me about the drinks, the reckless ride, and then, in the same breath, about the terror of an anxiety attack that drove him to dial a medical helpline in the middle of the night, where an unnamed woman walked him through it with patience and truth. Scripture never asks us to compartmentalize testimony this way; it invites the whole story into the light. “But if we walk in the light as He is in the light, we have fellowship with one another” (1 John 1:7). I am struck by how God is not limited to using vessels who know they are being used — Elijah, exhausted and despairing under the broom tree, was not first given a rebuke but a messenger with bread and water and the simple instruction to rest, eat, and walk (1 Kings 19:4–7). The pattern is strikingly similar: physical intervention before spiritual instruction. My brother received his own “arise and eat” through a stranger’s voice on a phone line — a modern echo of that ancient mercy. I find it worth noting that the Hebrew word behind “spirit” in that passage, רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — Spirit, breath, wind — carries a resonance with the very crisis my brother described. An anxiety attack is, physiologically, a crisis of breath — shallow, racing, out of control. And what did the helpline worker prescribe? Movement, air, walking outside. I am not saying a walk outside is a spiritual discipline in the technical sense, but I am seeing that the body’s cry for breath and rhythm is not separate from the deeper cry of the soul for the Spirit’s steadying presence, and that God can meet both at once — just as His רוּחַ (ROO-akh) breathed life into Adam (Genesis 2:7) and later filled the dry bones of Ezekiel’s valley until they lived (Ezekiel 37:9–10). I also noticed the strange juxtaposition of the night’s two halves — celebration of “earned” income spent freely with friends, and anxiety born, by his own admission, from an endless stream of sales calls and adrenaline never released. There is a subtle warning tucked inside his own joy: the very grind producing his provision is also the mechanism wearing down his nervous system to the point of crisis at 3 a.m. Ecclesiastes names this pattern plainly: “There is one alone, without companion… yet there is no end to all his labor… For whom do I toil and deprive myself of good?” (Ecclesiastes 4:8). The blessing of provision and the burden of striving can grow from the same root, and only the Lord’s wisdom can show a man how to labor without being consumed. Something arrests me here — my brother said he was hoping the Lord would do more than he ever could for the woman who helped him, asking me to bless her in prayer since he couldn’t thank her directly. This is priestly instinct rising up even in his weakest hour, echoing Job praying for his friends even amid his own affliction (Job 42:10). Later that same night, in a separate but connected conversation, I found myself on the phone with Brother Samuel — a call that began in ordinary places, waiting seasons, marriage, discipline, and ended somewhere holy, praying over a homeless man neither of us can save by our own strength. I confessed feeling old and slow, full of compromise, anticipating fatherhood in a season that felt like gestation without visible fruit. Paul’s words came alive to me here: “My little children, for whom I labor in birth again until Christ is formed in you” (Galatians 4:19) — real work happening in darkness, unseen, until the appointed time. I was not wrong to feel the absence of visible progress; I was only wrong to interpret absence as abandonment. Samuel’s own hard-won wisdom — “don’t judge your faith by how you feel today” — echoed Habakkuk’s posture exactly: “though the fig tree may not blossom… yet I will rejoice in the LORD” (Habakkuk 3:17–18). The Spirit then pressed something deeper into our exchange — the recognition that “we are all Judas.” I want to sit with this rather than soften it. This was not an excuse for sin but an honest reckoning with the flesh’s capacity for betrayal even within genuine love for Christ. Peter denied Jesus three times; Judas betrayed Him for silver. The difference was not the presence of failure but the response to it. This is why I have taken to praying “God keep you” instead of merely “God bless you” — because Jesus’s own prayer for Peter in Luke 22:32 was not “may you never fall” but “I have prayed for you, that your faith should not fail.” The Greek word behind “faith” here is πίστις (pistis, PIS-tis) — not mere intellectual belief but persevering trust, a fidelity that survives failure. My own prayer, “keep me,” is not asking God to prevent my flesh from stumbling — it is asking Him to preserve the πίστις (PIS-tis) underneath the stumbling, the way He preserved Peter’s. Samuel’s insight that our relationship with God is “fifty-fifty, but you give a hundred percent for your fifty” needed correction, and tracing it now I see it more clearly: it is not that God does fifty percent and we contribute an equal fifty as partners in a shared project. It is that God does everything, and our part is trust that positions us to receive what is already entirely His gift — the same mystery Paul unpacks in Philippians 2:12–13, “work out your own salvation… for it is God who works in you both to will and to do.” Our labor is real, but it is response, not contribution. This led naturally into Amos 3:7 — “Surely the Lord God does nothing, unless He reveals His secret to His servants the prophets” — not primarily about prediction but about partnership, the same principle behind the Lord’s Prayer itself: “Your kingdom come, Your will be done on earth as it is in heaven” (Matthew 6:10), a prayer assuming heaven’s will requires earthly agreement to be enacted in time. This is the hinge on which the whole conversation turned toward Thomas. Samuel’s grief over a homeless man he barely knows is not sentimentality — it is the fragrance of a heart aligned with God’s own heart for the broken, in contrast to the “noise of your songs” Amos condemns when ritual outpaces justice and mercy (Amos 5:23–24). I recall Jesus weeping over Jerusalem — “how often I wanted to gather your children together… but you were not willing!” (Matthew 23:37) — and I see now that this is the exact shape of Samuel’s grief over Thomas. It is not guilt, as Samuel rightly discerned; it is compassion refused by a will that has not yet surrendered. Grief and sovereignty are not opposites. Samuel named the tension of Thomas’s freedom precisely: “he has to choose to be free… and he’s not choosing” — the same mystery Scripture holds concerning Israel’s hardened heart in Romans 9–11, a hardening that is real and culpable yet held within a larger redemptive purpose that does not erase the love extended toward it. The discernment about enabling versus helping — Samuel’s recognition that continued help without accountability might harm Thomas’s soul more than his body — echoes Paul’s instruction that “if anyone will not work, neither shall he eat” (2 Thessalonians 3:10), not as cruelty but as acknowledgment that love sometimes requires boundaries that protect the dignity and agency of the one being loved. My counsel to “shake the dust off” if discernment shows the effort isn’t received recalls Jesus’s own instruction to the seventy in Luke 10:10–11 — not abandonment, but recognition that some doors, for a time, remain shut. I noticed finally the mercy embedded in Samuel’s testimony of once being homeless and sober, while I confessed having been most financially successful while active in addiction. Neither poverty nor plenty determines the condition of the heart before God; only surrender does — the same confession Paul makes of himself: “of whom I am chief” (1 Timothy 1:15), a mercy that qualifies him to extend mercy precisely because he never forgot what he was rescued from. Sitting with all three movements of this night together — the reckless ride, the anxious call, the intercession for Thomas — I see one Father moving through a stranger’s erratic kindness, a helpline worker’s calm instruction, and a friend’s grief over the unreachable. None of these vessels named Christ explicitly in the moment, yet none of them were outside His reach. “And we know that all things work together for good to those who love God, to those who are the called according to His purpose” (Romans 8:28) — not because the night itself, or the anxiety, or Thomas’s bondage were good, but because even here, the Spirit was at work drawing all of us toward deeper watchfulness, deeper trust, and deeper love for the broken. ## Implications and Challenges This night challenges a common thread in modern culture — including at times within circles that name Christ — where recklessness is repackaged as adventure and near-misses become entertaining anecdotes rather than sobering warnings; there is grace for laughter, but grace does not mean the absence of discernment, and survival is not the same as wisdom. It challenges the assumption that God only ministers through explicitly Christian channels — pastors, quoted Scripture, prayer meetings — when in truth He is sovereign enough to send relief through a secular helpline worker who never mentioned His name, or through a stranger’s incoherent generosity. We must not despise the instruments He chooses, nor treat practical, physiological coping tools as somehow less spiritual than “just praying it away” — God made bodies with nervous systems, and honoring how He designed them is not a lack of faith. It challenges the tendency to weaponize “we are all Judas” as an excuse for ongoing compromise rather than an honest starting point for repentance; naming the flesh’s capacity for betrayal must lead toward Peter’s tears, not Judas’s despair or presumption. It confronts a subtle works-based framing — the “fifty-fifty” language — reminding us that even our trust is sustained by grace, lest we quietly slide into thinking God’s provision is contingent on our performance rather than our surrender. It challenges comfortable Christians to examine whether our compassion for the broken, like Samuel’s for Thomas, is genuine grief like Christ’s over Jerusalem, or a safe, sanitized version that never risks proximity, inconvenience, or heartbreak — while also gently correcting a codependent model of mercy, showing that boundaries rooted in discernment are not a failure of love but sometimes its most mature expression. And yet, all of this correction is offered with grace: my brother is loved, his friendship with MJ is precious, his honesty in sharing both his recklessness and his fear is itself an opening for deeper trust; Samuel’s grief over Thomas is precious too, and none of the roughness of our theology mid-conversation diminishes what the Spirit was already doing in real time. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This calls me to grow in discerning between celebrating survival and cultivating wisdom, to stop measuring God’s faithfulness by daily emotional temperature, and to root confidence in His unchanging character — trusting hidden formation is as real as visible fruit, and holding joy for God’s mercy alongside honest concern for the choices that required it. **For Kingdom Obedience:** This calls for sober-mindedness as an act of worship — treating our bodies, minds, and nights as belonging to God — and for loving the Thomases in our path with open hands, offering real help, real accountability, and real boundaries, while releasing the outcome to God rather than carrying the weight of another’s will as though it were ours to control. It also calls me to keep praying for the unnamed instruments of mercy God places in the paths of those I love. **For Prophetic Posture:** This trains me to listen well before speaking, to receive a brother’s testimony with warmth before offering correction, to watch for God’s hand in unlikely places — helplines, strangers, late-night confessions — and to hold intercession and discernment together, praying boldly for the bound and broken while remaining sober about when to stay, when to withdraw, and when to shake the dust from my feet. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as a co-heir with Christ, grateful for the mercy that covered my brother on a night that could have ended so differently, grateful for the unnamed woman who steadied his racing heart at three in the morning, and grateful for the late-night honesty between Samuel and me that turned into intercession for a man named Thomas whom only You can fully reach. Thank You that You are not confined to church walls or Christian vocabulary, that You move through strangers, helplines, and imperfect friends to accomplish Your keeping work. I lift up my brother’s new season, his sobriety, his growing wisdom; I lift up Samuel’s steadfastness and his grief for the broken; and I lift up Thomas, asking that You loose what is bound in him, soften what is hardened, and let him know Your love whether on the street or at the gates of eternity. Keep us all steadfast, Lord, preserve our πίστις (PIS-tis) beneath our stumbling as You preserved Peter’s, and let our love for one another and for the lost be a fragrance pleasing before Your throne. In Jesus’s name, Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** |
