# 07-14 Call Reflection: Priestly Calling, Present-Tense Obedience, and the Peace Beyond Stoicism **Title:** 07-14 at 12:34 — The Crucible That Forges Priests: Present-Tense Obedience, the Priestly Call, and the Peace That Stoicism Could Never Find **Recorded:** 2026-07-14 12:34:00 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I am sitting with something that emerged not in a church building or a quiet study, but in the middle of a sweltering workday — a lunch-break call that cracked open into genuine, Spirit-moved exchange between brothers. What struck me most was not any single insight, but the compounding revelation that threaded itself through what my brother Samuel shared: that the present moment is the only arena of real obedience, that lust and fleshly thinking are not merely moral failures but acts of desecration against the living temple, and that the peace Marcus Aurelius reached for with his own disciplined mind is precisely the peace that only Christ can give — and that Christ gives freely to those who do not strive for it but simply abide. The heat of the welding station and the heat of spiritual formation turned out to be the same fire. **Why This Matters:** The saints of God are not formed in comfortable conditions. They are formed in crucibles — in workplaces that feel like furnaces, in lunch breaks stolen between clock-outs, in the quiet pressure of deciding whether to approach a man you had a hand in removing from his position, in the discipline of reading Scripture until the Word actually begins reading you. What I heard in Brother Samuel’s voice was the unmistakable sound of a man who is genuinely beginning to understand what it means to be a royal priest in Christ — not a man performing religion, but a man learning to intercede through a window for an enemy without being seen, learning to measure himself only by present-tense obedience rather than the ghosts of past failure or the anxieties of future uncertainty. This matters for every believer because these lessons are not advanced theology reserved for scholars. They are the bread-and-water survival kit for anyone who intends to actually follow Jesus in a world that is actively hostile to His Spirit. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural frame, what Brother Samuel described would be read as a series of fairly ordinary personal development insights: live in the present, don’t compare yourself to others, manage your impulses, handle workplace conflict with emotional intelligence, and appreciate that even non-Christian thinkers like Marcus Aurelius arrived at some universal truths about self-discipline and inner peace. The natural mind would commend Samuel for his maturity in not confronting his former boss at an emotionally charged moment, praise the stoic framework as a practical tool for mental resilience, and perhaps suggest that the difference between Marcus Aurelius and Christianity is mainly one of emphasis rather than kind — both, after all, seem to recommend a kind of inward ordering of the self against the chaos of the world. The natural mind would file these reflections under “personal growth” and move on. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind cannot perceive what the Spirit was actually doing in that conversation, because the natural mind has no category for the priestly. It has categories for the therapeutic, the philosophical, the emotionally regulated — but it does not know what it means for a man to stand at a window and speak blessing over someone who has reason to resent him, invoking not his own goodwill but the living God who alone can reach that man’s heart. The natural mind can produce stoic endurance, but it cannot produce resurrection joy under persecution. It can produce self-mastery, but it cannot produce a holy temple. The difference is not one of degree but of origin — one peace is manufactured from within, and the other is received from above. Until a man understands that distinction in his bones, all the Meditations of Marcus Aurelius will bring him to the edge of the water but never to the drink. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I listened to Brother Samuel describe the lessons that crystallized for him — that he is his own man before God, that the only real platform is now, that measuring himself against others along a timeline is a category error — I recognized something the Spirit has been pressing into me as well. There is a reason Paul writes in Philippians 4:11, *“I have learned, in whatever state I am, to be content.”* The word “learned” matters enormously. The Greek is μεμάθηκα (memathēka, meh-MAH-thay-kah) — a perfect active indicative, meaning this is a completed learning that still carries present consequence. Paul is not saying contentment is instinctive. He is saying it is acquired through process, through the school of actual experience, through seasons of being abased and seasons of abounding. What Brother Samuel articulated — that we live only now, and that our only valid measure is the quality of our present decisions — is not merely a philosophical insight. It is the lived experiential pathway by which μεμάθηκα (meh-MAH-thay-kah) gets written into the body of a disciple. I find myself arrested by the moment Samuel described standing in a corridor, walking back and forth, praying a simple prayer: *Lord, if I am meant to speak to him, free up the conversation.* That is not stoic self-management. That is not emotional regulation. That is priestly intercession dressed in ordinary clothes. The priest in the Old Covenant stood between the holy and the common, between the people and the consuming fire, and made representation on behalf of those who could not represent themselves. What Samuel did at that moment was stand between a wounded man and the justice of God, and invite the Spirit to arbitrate what his flesh could not wisely adjudicate. When I said to him, *“It sounds like you are finally learning what it means to be called a priest in Christ”* — I meant it exactly that precisely. The New Covenant priesthood described in 1 Peter 2:9, *“a royal priesthood, a holy nation”*, is not a title. It is a vocation that gets activated in moments exactly like that corridor. What deepens this further is what Samuel shared about lust — the insight he had encountered that lust is judged more harshly than anger because it is a sin of chosen pleasure, not involuntary pain. When I added to that the dimension Paul gives us in 1 Corinthians 6:18-19 — *“he who commits sexual immorality sins against his own body… your body is the temple of the Holy Spirit”* — something clarified that I want to sit with. The Greek word for temple here is ναός (naos, NAH-oss), which does not refer to the outer courts of Herod’s complex but specifically to the inner sanctuary — the Holy of Holies, where the Shekinah dwelt. When Paul says sexual sin is a sin against the ναός (NAH-oss), he is not speaking metaphorically in the casual sense. He is saying that the very place in a human being where the Spirit of God takes up residence — the innermost chamber of the person — is what is being violated. To choose lust is not merely to sin morally. It is to walk into the Holy of Holies with unclean hands and desecrate the mercy seat. This is what I tried to articulate to Brother Samuel: that when God says *“you are my delight, my love, my everything”* — and I am pressing into the Hebrew concept of חֵפֶץ (chephets, KHEH-fets), which means not merely pleasure but sovereign delight, the kind of delight a craftsman has in the work that fully expresses him — when God speaks that over a human being and that human being turns toward lust instead, what is happening is not simply a failure of willpower. It is the creature saying to the Creator, *“Your delight in me is not enough. I need something now that you have declared I should not take.”* And because that rejection happens in the ναός (NAH-oss), inside the sanctuary itself, it is as intimate a betrayal as any in Scripture. The conversation then turned to the question of familiar spirits and the origin of intrusive, condemning thoughts. When Samuel mentioned that he kept finding himself thinking about Emily — what she was doing, how she was living — and how he had come to recognize that those thoughts were not of the Spirit, I pointed out what I genuinely believe: not all recurring negative thoughts originate in us. Some are our own ego cycling. Some are the Spirit’s conviction, which leads toward repentance and release. But some are whispers from what Scripture calls familiar spirits — spirits that know our personal histories, our wounds, our relational losses, and exploit them with remarkable precision. The fact that those thoughts produced shame, condemnation, and comparison rather than grief that drives to prayer is itself diagnostic. Romans 8:1 declares there is *“no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus”* — and the distinguishing mark of the Spirit’s work is not that it makes us feel worse about ourselves endlessly, but that it calls us upward and forward. What keeps a man circling the grave of an old relationship is not God’s voice. It is a familiar voice dressed in the clothes of conscience. Then came the Marcus Aurelius thread, and this is where I believe the Spirit was doing something particularly layered. Samuel had been reading the *Meditations* — a book written by a man who oversaw the martyrdom of Christians, a pagan emperor who nonetheless in his private writings, apparently never intended for publication, addressed not *gods* but *God* in the singular. Samuel was asking an honest and spiritually discerning question: How does a Christian read this? And the answer the Lord led him toward was exactly the answer I have been sitting with for some time now. God can use anything. He used Pharaoh’s hard heart to reveal His own glory across ten plagues and a parted sea. He used Cyrus, a Persian king who did not know the God of Israel, to decree the rebuilding of Jerusalem — and Isaiah 45:4 records God calling Cyrus by name a century before Cyrus was born. The vessel does not have to be consecrated for God to pour revelation through it. What matters is whether the reader has the Spirit to discern what is gold and what is dross. Marcus Aurelius arrived at something genuinely valuable: the discipline of the present moment, the importance of not being enslaved to passion, the dignity of doing one’s duty without requiring the approval of the crowd. These things echo the wisdom literature of Proverbs, they rhyme with Paul’s call to φρονεῖν (phronein, froh-NANE) — to set the mind on things above. But here is exactly where Aurelius reached the ceiling of what the unaided human mind can achieve: his peace was the peace of a man who had successfully managed his inner world. It was a constructed peace. And what Christ offers is not constructed. It is given. John 14:27 — *“Peace I leave with you, My peace I give to you; not as the world gives do I give to you.”* The contrast Jesus draws is not between peace and chaos, but between two different kinds of peace: one that the world can give through technique, philosophy, or discipline, and one that He gives through the deposit of His own Spirit. The first kind, Aurelius achieved with extraordinary discipline. The second kind, he never found — because it is not achievable. It is only receivable. What Samuel said about this cut cleanly: *“His relationship was with his own mind. That’s where he found the peace. And though he was close, he wasn’t it.”* Yes. That is exactly it. And the danger of a book like the *Meditations* is not that it is evil — it is that it is close enough to the truth to function as a substitute for the truth in the life of a believer who is not yet rooted deeply enough to detect the substitution. The enemy does not always come with obviously false doctrine. Sometimes he comes with genuinely good philosophy that falls one step short of Christ and offers itself as sufficient. When I referenced how I had written about this in a previous meditation — that stoicism teaches defense against the world while Christ in you teaches something entirely different — I was pressing at this exact distinction. Stoicism builds an inner fortress. The Gospel does not build a fortress. It builds a resurrection. You do not fortify yourself against the world from inside the citadel of your own mind. You carry the life of the risen Christ into the world, and that life is indestructible precisely because it has already passed through death. This brought me back to the image that struck me most powerfully in the whole conversation — the image of embracing Pilate as he nails you to the cross. Samuel said it and I felt it land with weight: *“You hug Pilate as he puts you on the cross.”* That is not stoic resignation. That is not Epictetus telling you to distinguish between what is in your power and what is not. That is the Lamb of God going willingly to slaughter, not because He has detached from pain, but because He has entrusted Himself fully to the Father who judges justly (1 Peter 2:23). The saints do not endure persecution by feeling nothing. They endure it by knowing Someone who is worth every cost, and they say alongside the psalmist in Psalm 22 — the psalm Jesus quoted from the cross — *“My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?”* knowing that the psalm ends not in abandonment but in proclamation: *“They will come and declare His righteousness to a people who will be born, that He has done this”* (Psalm 22:31). I think about the welding station in one hundred and five degree heat. The gloves. The mask. The furnace of summer work. I said to Gladys before the call came: *“Just building the endurance in me — for persecution.”* She asked, *“For what?”* I said, *“For persecution.”* And she said, *“Oh no.”* But the truth is that this is precisely how the Spirit works in ordinary life — the slow forging of a person through unglamorous, uncomfortable, daily faithfulness. The Greek word ὑπομονή (hypomonē, hoo-po-moh-NAY) is often translated “patience” or “endurance,” but its root meaning is more active: it is the capacity to remain under a heavy load without abandoning the position. It is not passive waiting. It is active, rooted, purposeful bearing. James 1:3 says the testing of faith works ὑπομονή (hoo-po-moh-NAY) — and James does not frame that as a tragedy. He frames it as the pathway by which the believer becomes τέλειος (teleios, TEH-lay-oss), complete, lacking nothing. The crucible is not the enemy of completeness. It is the method of it. And finally, I want to name what I observed in Brother Samuel’s growth — because it connects to everything. He said: *“You know what the difference is, bro? I started reading.”* Not performing. Not attending services. Not accumulating spiritual credentials. Reading. Sitting with the Word and with books that the Spirit was using, and letting that reading recalibrate his interior world. And what struck me is that this is the ancient path — Psalm 1 describes the blessed man as one whose delight is in the law of the LORD and who meditates on it day and night. The Hebrew word there for meditates is הָגָה (hagah, hah-GAH), which in other contexts is used for the low growl of a lion over its prey or the murmuring of a dove. It is not intellectual analysis. It is intimate, repeated, absorbed rumination — the kind of reading that gets into the body, not just the mind. What Brother Samuel was describing when he said *“I started reading”* was not merely the acquisition of information. It was the beginning of hagah. And that changes everything. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation presses on the modern Western church is our deeply embedded preference for emotional experience over formative practice. We have built entire worship cultures around the feeling of being touched by God in a service, while quietly deprioritizing the daily disciplines — reading, prayer, fasting, intercession — that actually build the kind of interior architecture that can withstand a crucible. Brother Samuel’s breakthrough did not happen in a special service. It happened in a parking lot with a book, followed by a phone call on a lunch break in one hundred and five degree heat. The Spirit does not require our preferred conditions to do His best work. We are the ones who have made conditions a prerequisite. A second challenge sits squarely in the area of lust and sexual sin. The modern church has often reduced the conversation about sexual immorality to a behavioral and psychological framework — addiction cycles, accountability partners, shame reduction — without recovering the full theological weight of what Paul says in 1 Corinthians 6. When we understand that the body is the ναός (NAH-oss) — not the outer courts but the inner sanctuary — and that sexual sin is a unique desecration of that sanctuary, the stakes become deeply personal rather than merely moral. The invitation is not simply to “do better” but to understand that the living God has taken up residency in you, and that what you do with your body you do in the immediate presence of that residency. That is not meant to produce shame. It is meant to produce awe — the kind of awe that makes you want to guard the temple rather than abuse it. Third, the Marcus Aurelius thread issues a quiet warning about the seductive capacity of near-truth. A believer who is not deeply rooted in Scripture is genuinely vulnerable to philosophies that are coherent, beautiful, and functionally wise — but that stop short of Christ. Stoicism is perhaps the most refined example in Western history, but it is not the only one. There are therapeutic frameworks, self-help philosophies, and even certain strands of contemplative spirituality that offer a constructed inner peace that can be mistaken for the gift of God. The way to discern the difference is not to avoid all such material, but to do exactly what Samuel demonstrated: hold everything up against the Lord first, read with discernment active, and keep asking where the source of the peace ultimately resides — in the mind, or in the Person. Fourth, the priestly calling deserves to be reclaimed from abstraction. “Royal priesthood” in 1 Peter 2:9 is not a metaphor for church membership. It is a description of function. A priest stands between. A priest intercedes. A priest approaches what is holy on behalf of those who cannot. Samuel standing at a window praying blessing over a man who could have resented him, rather than approaching in the flesh at the wrong moment, was not simply an emotionally intelligent workplace decision. It was the exercise of a priestly calling in the most ordinary of circumstances. We should cultivate this. Every time we feel the pull to speak when the Spirit says wait, and we respond with intercession instead of impulse, we are functioning in our actual vocation. Fifth and finally, the test that follows the lesson deserves to be honored as a gift. Samuel noted that the Lord tested him on the very principles he had just received — within the same hour. This is not cruelty. This is the method of the Kingdom. Luke 8:18 warns, *“Therefore take heed how you hear.”* The word that is received and not immediately exercised becomes vulnerable to being taken away. The test that arrives immediately after the word is God’s gracious provision for the consolidation of that word into actual character. When the exam arrives the same day as the lesson, it is an act of mercy, not persecution. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This meditation calls us to take seriously the formation that happens in ordinary, uncomfortable, unglamorous circumstances — in heat and weariness and workday pressure. Spiritual maturity is not built in mountaintop moments alone. It is built in corridors, waiting, choosing to intercede rather than react, choosing to read rather than scroll, choosing to bless rather than confront at the wrong moment. The measure of our growth is not the height of our best spiritual experience. It is the quality of our smallest present-tense decision when no one is watching. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Kingdom obedience calls us to recover the priestly function as a lived daily practice — not a title or a Sunday identity, but a standing posture of intercession and representation. When we are grieved in the spirit about someone and we cannot resolve it with words or resources, the invitation is always into prayer. That is the priestly movement. That is what the Gospel makes possible. Wherever we stand — at a window, in a corridor, at a welding station — we carry the office of reconciliation, and we exercise it through prayer when our hands cannot yet act. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation calls us into is the posture of the discerning reader — not merely of Scripture but of all things, holding everything before the Lord first, recognizing that He can speak through any vessel while simultaneously recognizing that not every vessel leads to the fountain of life. We remain in the Word deeply enough that we can receive insight from Aurelius without being seduced by Aurelius. We remain in prayer persistently enough that the familiar spirit’s whisper is recognizable as distinct from the Spirit’s still small voice. We remain in community honestly enough that a lunch-break phone call between brothers can become the occasion for genuine iron sharpening iron — and that sharpening is itself a prophetic act, preparing each of us for what is coming. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I thank You for the crucible — the heat of the welding station, the ache of a lower back after faithful housework, the corridor walked twice while waiting on Your timing, the window through which a man speaks blessing over someone he could not yet face. I thank You that You are not wasteful with suffering, and that every furnace You allow is calibrated to produce gold, not destruction. Lord, I receive what You have been forging in me through ordinary days and uncommon heat — the ὑπομονή (hoo-po-moh-NAY) that James says completes what is lacking, the priestly instinct that reaches toward intercession before it reaches toward impulse, the discernment that can read the wisdom of the world without being claimed by it. I stand in agreement with Your Word that I am the ναός (NAH-oss) — the inner sanctuary — of the Holy Spirit, and I consecrate this body and this life as a living offering, not desecrated by the pleasures that pass away but held in the holiness that endures. I pray for my brother Samuel — Lord, go find the beauty in this man. Find the priest in him that is already there by Your design, and call him forth into the fullness of what You have placed in him. I pray for every brother and sister who reads this — that the peace You give, not as the world gives, would settle into the deepest chamber of who they are, and that the pursuits of the flesh would grow dim in the presence of the living God who calls them delight. You are worthy of every cost, every furnace, every act of surrender. We say yes to the formation. We say yes to the priesthood. We say yes to the present moment, because it is here that You are. In the name of Jesus Christ, risen and returning — Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Philippians 4:11 — “I have learned, in whatever state I am, to be content” | μεμάθηκα (memathēka, meh-MAH-thay-kah) — perfect active indicative of manthanō;
