# 06-18 Call Reflection: Samuel’s Wilderness Season — Divine Formation, Solitude, and Entrepreneurial Tension **Title:** 06-18 at 11:24 — The Wilderness of Solitude and the Shape of a Man Being Built **Recorded:** 2026-06-18 11:24:47 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture / Phone Call) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to this conversation with my brother Samuel, one moment surfaced above the noise of the rest of it and arrested me — the moment he said, almost casually, that he has come to the conclusion of exiling himself from the world and living alone with God. It was buried between talk of pipes and chatbots and ad spend, and yet it landed in me with unusual weight. Something in the Spirit pressed me to sit with that one sentence rather than let it dissolve into the surrounding conversation. **Why This Matters:** The shape of a man being built by God is rarely announced with ceremony. It tends to surface in unguarded moments — between the jokes, between the business talk, between the distractions — as a quiet confession of what is actually happening beneath the surface of a man’s life. What my brother described, the sense that the world is pushing him out rather than pulling him in, and that the only real refuge left is God Himself, is not a crisis of belonging. It is one of the oldest patterns in the scriptural record. If we do not recognize it for what it is, we will try to fix it, soften it, or explain it away. The Body of Christ needs to know how to recognize this pattern when it appears in a brother’s voice — because what looks like social isolation from the outside may, in fact, be the chamber of formation from the inside. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, hearing a young man say he wants to exile himself from the world and live alone with God, will reach almost immediately for one of two diagnoses. Either it will pathologize it — labeling it avoidance, social anxiety, a trauma response, or a coping mechanism dressed in spiritual language — or it will romanticize it, treating it as a phase, a young man’s idealism that will pass once the bills pile up and the relationships demand something of him. The same natural mind, watching the rest of this conversation, would likely focus on the entrepreneurial hustle, the business ambition, the technology project, and the financial back-and-forth as the real story. It would see the comment about exile and God as a throwaway line, perhaps even as a contradiction to the drive and ambition present elsewhere in the exchange. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind cannot perceive what the Spirit does with a man’s brokenness, restlessness, and rejection because it does not have access to the architectural blueprints of God’s formation process. What looks like contradiction — a man simultaneously building a business and confessing he wants to flee the world — is not incoherence to the Spirit. The Spirit recognizes a man in two seasons at once, one foot still in the world of activity and ambition, one foot already leaning toward the threshold of deeper solitude with God. The natural mind sees the gap as hypocrisy or confusion. The Spirit sees it as the very tension that precedes transformation. To flatten that tension into a tidy psychological category is to miss the invitation entirely. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I am sitting with the simplicity and the weight of what Samuel said: God makes it so that the world rejects you, and then the only place you have to go is Him, and then He builds you anew, and then He releases you to show them. I want to stay here because there is more Scripture packed into that sentence than Samuel may have fully realized in the moment he said it. He was not quoting a text. He was living one. The pattern he described is not new. It is the pattern of the wilderness. It is the pattern by which God has formed virtually every servant of significant consequence in the scriptural record. Moses spent forty years in the wilderness of Midian before the burning bush. David was anointed king and then spent years running through caves and wilderness terrain before he ever sat on a throne. Elijah fled to Horeb after Jezebel’s threat, and it was there — in the still small voice — that God met him and reoriented him for his final assignment. John the Baptist was described in Luke 1:80 as being “in the deserts till the day of his manifestation to Israel.” Paul received his gospel not from men but in Arabia, in a season of hidden preparation that he would later describe in Galatians 1:15-17. The wilderness, it seems, is not a detour from the assignment. It is the assignment’s first chapter. What strikes me, however, is where I pushed back in the conversation. Samuel said God makes it so the world rejects you, and I responded: “I don’t think He makes it so the world rejects you — I think the world just rejects.” I said that instinctively, and I want to be honest about what I was doing. I think I was trying to protect the character of God from sounding arbitrary or punishing. But sitting with it more carefully now, I wonder if I was too quick. Because Scripturally, there is a real sense in which God does order the circumstances of His servants’ lives in such a way that the world’s doors close. He doesn’t merely permit it. He orchestrates it. Joseph’s brothers did not simply hate him — they were, without knowing it, instruments of a sovereign journey. The Psalmist of Psalm 105 says plainly that God “sent a man before them — Joseph, who was sold as a slave.” The selling was human sin. The sending was divine sovereignty. Both were true simultaneously. This is the doctrine of divine purposefulness in suffering, and it is not cruel — it is covenantal. The Hebrew word מִדְבָּר (midbar, MID-bar) — wilderness — carries within it the root דָּבַר (davar, dah-VAR), which means to speak or to word. The wilderness in the Hebrew imagination is not merely a place of absence; it is a place where God speaks. What looks like abandonment is, in fact, the most intimate geography of divine communication. The prophet Hosea captured this precisely in Hosea 2:14 — “I will allure her, will bring her into the wilderness, and speak comfort to her.” God does not abandon His people to the wilderness. He brings them there to speak. As I reflect on what Samuel described — feeling the world push him out, finding himself turning toward God as the only remaining option — I want to name what I am seeing with some theological care. There is a difference between retreat driven by despair and retreat driven by divine appointment. The former is a man running from pain. The latter is a man being run toward God. And crucially, these two things can look identical from the outside — and even feel identical from the inside — in the early stages. The distinguishing mark, over time, is fruit. Does the season of solitude produce encounter with God, realignment of identity, and eventual release back toward others with something transformed and clarified? Or does it produce mere avoidance, further contraction, and increasing isolation from accountability and love? The trajectory tells you what kind of wilderness you are in. I notice that Samuel also identified the movement as three-stage: the world rejects, God builds, then God releases to show them. This is not merely a personal experience narrative. This is a theological pattern. In prophetic typology, it is the pattern of death, burial, and resurrection. It is the pattern of the grain of wheat Jesus describes in John 12:24 — “Unless a grain of wheat falls into the ground and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it produces much grain.” The aloneness is not the end. It is the condition required for multiplication. What is buried in the ground is not lost. It is being transformed into something that cannot exist without the burial having occurred first. The Apostle Paul names this trajectory through the language of παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) — fatherly training, discipline, the kind of formation that a father administers to a son he is preparing for inheritance. Hebrews 12:7 says, “If you endure chastening, God deals with you as with sons.” The word “endure” there is important. The Greek ὑπομένω (hypomenō, hoo-po-MEN-oh) does not mean passive suffering. It means to remain under, to stay the course beneath the weight, to not flee the process. What God is doing in the wilderness requires the son to stay, not to escape. The exile Samuel is describing, if it is genuinely Spirit-led, is not an escape from discomfort. It is a staying under the hand of God while the world’s pull loses its grip. What also surfaces for me in Samuel’s phrasing is the word “alone.” He wants to live alone with God. I want to sit with that phrase — alone with God — because it is both beautiful and dangerous if misunderstood. There is a kind of “alone with God” that Scripture celebrates. Moses entered the thick darkness where God was (Exodus 20:21). Elijah heard the still small voice only after the spectacular had passed. David spoke of the Lord as his shepherd in Psalm 23 in language so intimate it is almost embarrassing in its tenderness — “He leads me,” “He restores my soul.” The solitary encounter with the living God is one of the most sacred geometries in all of Scripture. But Scripture also holds that the final destination of the man built in solitude is not continued solitude. It is community. It is the Body. It is the testimony released among brothers and sisters who needed what could only be forged in the hidden place. I think what I want to say to my brother — and to myself — is this: the desire to withdraw from a world that feels increasingly alien to what God is building in you is not a failure of love. It may be a sign of maturation. When a man has been genuinely touched by the living God, the noise, compromise, and performance of the world starts to feel not merely unappealing but genuinely incompatible. That incompatibility is not pride. It is holy dissonance. It is what 2 Corinthians 6:17 addresses when God says, “Come out from among them and be separate.” The called-out ones — the ἐκκλησία (ekklēsia, ek-klay-SEE-ah) — are, by their very nature, people who do not fully belong where they came from. But here is the tension I want to name with care: the business conversation, the TikTok strategy, the AI chatbots, the £3,500 membership tiers — none of that is spiritually neutral in the presence of a man who is also sensing a call to deeper solitude with God. I am not pronouncing judgment on any of it. I do not know what God is or isn’t blessing in Samuel’s entrepreneurial work. But I notice the juxtaposition. The same voice that said, “I want to exile myself from the world and live alone with God” is also building a concierge network, chasing viral reach, and calculating profit margins. That is not an accusation. That is the lived tension of a man in transition — still building in the world’s economy while something deeper in him is being summoned out of it. The Spirit does not always demand that a man leave his marketplace work. He sometimes transforms it into Kingdom work entirely. But He does demand honesty about which master is being served. Jesus said in Matthew 6:24 that a man cannot serve two masters. That word “serve” — δουλεύω (douleuō, doo-LEW-oh) — carries the sense of total subordination, the posture of a slave before an owner. The question is not whether a man can have a business and a prayer life simultaneously. The question is which one shapes the other. Is the solitude with God shaping the business, or is the business consuming the time that should be given to the solitude? I end this section where the Spirit keeps bringing me back — to that buried sentence. The world rejects. God builds. God releases. That is a testimony waiting to be told. And it will only be told truly if my brother endures the building long enough to receive what cannot be rushed. ## Implications and Challenges The pattern of divine withdrawal and reformation is well-attested in Scripture, yet the modern Western church often pathologizes it rather than pastors it. When a brother or sister begins to feel drawn away from social engagement and toward solitude, the instinctive response of many communities is concern, intervention, and the urging of re-engagement. We have developed a reflexive suspicion of aloneness that Scripture does not share. The desert fathers, the wilderness prophets, the apostolic withdrawals — these were not pastoral failures. They were formation seasons. We would do well to learn the difference between isolation born of wounding and solitude born of divine summons, and to create space in our communities for the latter without shame. The easy conflation of entrepreneurial ambition with Kingdom purpose is a genuinely subtle danger, not because building businesses is wrong, but because the language of Kingdom and the language of market share can begin to merge imperceptibly. When a man begins talking about viral reach, exclusive memberships, and profit optimization with the same breath he uses to speak of God’s formation in his life, the discerning ear must ask: is the Kingdom vision shaping the business strategy, or has the business strategy quietly annexed the Kingdom language for its own legitimacy? Neither outcome is inevitable. But the question deserves to be asked honestly and often, especially in the early stages of a man’s formation. The willingness to be rejected by the world is not a passive resignation. It is an active theological stance. Romans 12:2 commands transformation by the renewing of the mind and explicitly frames it as non-conformity to the age. That non-conformity is not cultural eccentricity for its own sake. It is the visible consequence of a mind being genuinely restructured by the Word and Spirit of God. When a man like Samuel says the world rejects him, the Spirit-formed response is not to ask how to re-enter that world’s approval. It is to ask what God is now able to build in the space created by that rejection. The temptation to delay the hidden work of formation until financial stability is secured is one of the oldest and most effective strategies the enemy deploys against servants in transition. “Once the ads are running, once the revenue is consistent, once the system is built — then I will give God the deep attention He is asking for.” The problem is that the season of formation does not typically wait for financial convenience. It arrives when it arrives, and the man who defers the inner work until his outer world is organized will often find that the deferral itself has become the obstacle to the breakthrough he is waiting for. Finally, there is an invitation here to the Body of Christ to take seriously the testimonies buried inside ordinary conversations. Samuel said something of profound theological and personal weight in the middle of talk about pipes and ad budgets. If no one in his life pauses to recognize it, to call it out, to sit with him in it, and to help him understand what God may be saying through the restlessness — the word can pass unacknowledged and the season can pass without the formation it was intended to produce. Being a brother in the Spirit sometimes means hearing the sentence beneath the sentence and having the courage to return to it. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This reflection calls me to take the pattern of divine formation through rejection and wilderness more seriously — not as a theological concept but as a lived reality to be stewarded. Maturity means I no longer flinch at the world’s rejection of what God is building in me, and I no longer rush the hidden season in order to produce visible fruit before its time. It also means I grow in the capacity to recognize when a brother is in this season and to honor it rather than inadvertently pull him back into what God is drawing him out of. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this calls forth is the obedience of patience and honest self-examination. For Samuel, it may be the courage to let the desire for solitude with God be taken seriously rather than treated as a passing mood. For me, it is the ongoing commitment to hold the inner life as primary and to let everything built in the external world flow from what is genuinely being cultivated in the hidden place. Kingdom obedience here looks like prioritizing the building that God is doing rather than only the building that man can see. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation calls forth is the posture of attentive listening — to the Spirit, to brothers in conversation, and to the patterns God embeds in ordinary exchanges. Prophetic ministry is not only the dramatic word spoken to a congregation. It is also the recognition, in a casual phone call, that something of eternal weight just surfaced in a brother’s voice, and the faithfulness to mark it, return to it, and let the Spirit unfold it. To remain in this posture requires a quiet interior — which is precisely what the wilderness of solitude with God is designed to produce. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who has also known the ache of the world’s rejection and the quiet wonder of finding You in the space it left behind. I thank You that You are not distant from my brother Samuel, that You hear him even in the middle of conversations about earthly things, and that You planted that word in his mouth — that You build, and then You release — as a testimony of what You are already doing in him. I ask You, Lord, to honor the longing in him that is genuinely Yours. Guard it from the noise that surrounds it. Protect the formation You have begun from the pressures that would rush it or abandon it. Give him grace to recognize the difference between the wilderness You are calling him into and the isolation that fear would construct. Let the desire to be alone with You deepen into actual encounter, actual formation, actual newness of mind and spirit. And Father, for me — keep me attentive to what You are saying through the ordinary moments, through the unguarded sentences, through the brother on the phone who does not yet fully know the weight of what he just confessed. May we be men who endure the building. May we not flee the פַּדַר (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) of Your hand. And when the season of building is complete, may the release be recognizable as Yours — bearing Your fruit, carrying Your fragrance, and pointing only to Your Name. In Jesus’ Name, Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Hosea 2:14 — “I will allure her, will bring her into the wilderness, and speak comfort to her.” | מִדְבָּר (midbar, MID-bar) — wilderness;
