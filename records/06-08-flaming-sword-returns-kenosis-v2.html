# 06-08 @ 10:04 — Call Reflection: The Flaming Sword Returns: Kenosis, Commission, and the Already-But-Not-Yet of Kingdom Building **Title:** 06-08 at 10:04 — The Flaming Sword Returns: Kenosis, Commission, and the Already-But-Not-Yet of Kingdom Building **Recorded:** 2026-06-08 10:04:02 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to this exchange — broken audio and all — something pressed through the noise that I could not ignore. Beneath the surface talk of sales networks and commission structures, a deeper current was running: the language of obedience windows, empty vessels, flaming swords, and ancestral intercession. The Spirit does not wait for clean audio or convenient settings to speak. What arrested me most was the convergence of the mundane and the sacred happening simultaneously on the same call — and how that convergence itself may be the very word the Spirit is pressing through. **Why This Matters:** There is a discipleship crisis in our generation that is not primarily doctrinal in its symptom — it is perceptual. We have been trained to compartmentalize: the sacred happens in church, the secular happens at work, and rarely do we expect the Spirit to be moving through a garbled phone call between two brothers navigating business strategy and Kingdom revelation in the same breath. Yet this is precisely the texture of the apostolic life as we find it in the canon — tents being sewn while the Gospel is being preached, ships being mended while disciples are being called, prisons becoming prayer rooms. If we cannot perceive the Spirit moving through the fabric of ordinary, interrupted, imperfect moments, we will miss more revelation than we will ever receive in curated settings. Learning to hold both streams — the practical and the prophetic — without collapsing one into the other is a mark of genuine spiritual maturity. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a natural vantage point, what we have here is simply two men catching up — one in a workplace setting with inadequate headphones, the other sharing a business update involving a mutual contact, commission percentages, and a growth strategy built around reinvesting early revenue into advertising. The theological language that surfaces in the second speaker’s portions would likely be read by the natural mind as enthusiastic spiritual metaphor at best, or as an eccentric overlay on otherwise practical conversation at worst. The world tends to evaluate conversations by their signal-to-noise ratio, their actionable outcomes, and their efficiency. A call where the audio keeps cutting out, where topics shift between sales funnels and flaming swords, and where one participant keeps losing the thread would register as unproductive — something to be cleaned up, focused, and made more professional before being taken seriously. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind operates from a fundamental deficit when it encounters the movements of the Spirit: it cannot hold the sacred and the mundane in simultaneous tension without resolving that tension prematurely in favor of one or the other. It either spiritualizes everything into abstraction, disconnecting revelation from the dirt of real life, or it pragmatizes everything into function, stripping the sacred register entirely. What the Spirit was doing in this conversation — weaving a thread of genuine prophetic reflection through the noise of a broken work-call — requires a kind of double-vision that only the renewed mind can sustain. The veil is not removed by intelligence or attentiveness alone; it is removed when the Lord is encountered and the inner eye is trained to see what the outer ear alone will always miss. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It What first catches my attention as I sit with this conversation is the moment my brother — the one I was speaking with — described his prior season of writing as an “obedience window.” He said he was walking in the dark the whole time, not knowing why he was writing, and only now, in the releasing of that work, does the purpose become visible. This is not a peripheral observation. This is the testimony of Abraham leaving Ur not knowing where he was going (Hebrews 11:8), of Noah building an ark before a single raindrop had fallen (Genesis 6), of the disciples being sent out two by two with instructions they did not yet fully understand (Luke 10:1–3). The obedience precedes the revelation. The walk precedes the map. This is consistently how the Spirit operates with those He is genuinely forming. I am struck by the image he reached for: murmurations. The coordinated, breathtaking flight of starlings — thousands of individual birds moving as one organism, no single bird directing the whole, yet the whole moving with a coherence that seems almost impossibly intentional. He was describing what happens when the individual commission is completed and released, and the tandem process begins — a chorus, he called it. There is something deeply pneumatological here. The Greek word for Spirit, πνεῦμα (pneuma, NYOO-mah), means breath or wind — and there is something about wind that is inherently collective and directional without being coercive. The Spirit does not force the murmuration; He breathes through it. Each bird yields, and the whole becomes beautiful. What my brother described — the completing of a corpus, a body of written work given in obedience, now released to an inner circle for testing and witness — carries the shape of what Paul describes in Ephesians 4 as the equipping of the saints. The gifts are not retained; they are poured out. The fivefold ministry does not hoard what it receives from the Head; it distributes. And the moment of release, he noted, created a new emptiness — an availability. “Always empty and then filled,” he said. This is the pattern of kenosis, the Greek κένωσις (kenōsis, keh-NO-sis) — self-emptying — that Paul describes in Philippians 2:7 of Christ Himself, who “made Himself of no reputation, taking the form of a bondservant.” The vessel must be emptied to be filled again. The season of accumulation must give way to the season of release. And in the releasing, the servant discovers that the next horizon is already approaching. Then came the moment that genuinely arrested me, and I do not think it was coincidence: the first person entering the network they were building is a man named Kane — and my brother immediately made the connection to the kenosis meditation he had written, and to the return of the flaming sword. I want to sit with that more carefully. In Genesis 3:24, after the expulsion from Eden, God placed cherubim and a flaming sword at the east of the garden “to guard the way to the tree of life.” That sword was not punishment alone — it was guardianship. It was the seal of a threshold not yet meant to be crossed. The way back to life was not through human effort or religious striving; it was blocked until the appointed time of restoration. And then comes Jesus. In Revelation 1:16, the Risen Christ appears with “a sharp two-edged sword” proceeding from His mouth. In Hebrews 4:12, the Word of God is described as “living and powerful, and sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing even to the division of soul and spirit.” The flaming sword that barred re-entry into the garden has not been destroyed — it has been transformed and placed in the mouth of the Son. The Hebrew word for word in Psalm 33:6 is דָּבָר (davar, dah-VAR) — word, but also event, act, thing. God’s speech is never merely informational. It is constitutive. It makes and unmakes. The Word that guarded the garden is now the Word that opens it — but it still cuts. It still proves. My brother said it plainly: “The Word cuts and tastes and proves.” This is not soft theology. This is the terror and the mercy of encounter with the living God. The Potter and the clay image he reached for from Jeremiah 18 deepens this further. The potter does not discard the marred clay — he reduces it to a lump and remolds it. The going-back-to-formlessness is not failure; it is preparation. There is a divine economy in which the seasons of seeming disorder, of being returned to the lump, are actually the moments of greatest redemptive potential. The Hebrew root for “form” in Genesis 2:7, יָצַר (yatsar, yah-TZAR), is the same word used for the potter’s work in Jeremiah 18. God formed man from the dust, and God the Potter remolds what has been marred. The empty vessel my brother described — that post-release season of fresh availability — is not a valley; it is a lump in the Potter’s hands. And that is a holy place to be. What he then said about his identity and calling deserves reverent handling: “I believe I am building a kingdom. My job is to be an ancestor, the one who stands before God on behalf of the people.” This is intercessory, priestly, generational language — and it echoes the pattern of Abraham, Moses, and the prophetic stream. Moses stood in the gap in Exodus 32:11–14 when God threatened to consume Israel, and his intercession turned the tide of history. The word for “stood” in that passage in the Hebrew implies a deliberate, sustained posture — not a momentary cry but a covenantal stand. When my brother describes his function as standing before God on behalf of others, he is articulating something that the institutional church has largely lost: the understanding that some are called not just to minister to the present congregation but to anchor something in the spirit for the generations that follow. The disciples he referenced from Luke 10 — the seventy sent out, returning with reports that demons were subject to them in the name of Jesus — is a powerful parallel to what he was experiencing in his secret place: words coming unbidden, a sense of being spoken through rather than merely speaking. Jesus’ response to the seventy’s report is instructive: “I saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven” (Luke 10:18). The commissioning of servants in obedience is cosmically consequential. When ordinary people walk in genuine Kingdom authority, something happens in the unseen realm. My brother was not boasting; he was marveling. And that wonder — “I can’t explain it” — is often the most honest theological statement a person can make when they are genuinely in the current of the Spirit. What I keep returning to is the convergence of the name Kane with the kenosis meditation, the flaming sword, and the moment of entering a new network. I do not wish to force typological precision where humility is more fitting. But I notice that the Spirit often marks the entrance into new territory with a sign — sometimes a name, sometimes a word spoken at an unexpected moment, sometimes a break in the audio that forces stillness. Jacob was not permitted to cross the Jabbok until he had wrestled and received both wound and blessing (Genesis 32). The thresholds of Kingdom advance are rarely clean. They are often characterized by poor signal, broken equipment, and interrupted sentences. Yet the word gets through. There is also something worth naming about the fractured nature of this call itself — the repeated audio failures, the struggle to hear, the constant negotiation of whether to continue or disconnect. I find a kind of parabolic quality here. So much of the spiritual life is lived in exactly this condition: partial hearing, broken connection, straining to catch what the Spirit is saying through interference and noise. Paul wrote in 1 Corinthians 13:12, “For now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face.” The dim mirror is not an excuse for passivity — it is the current condition of the pilgrim. We press through the static because the voice on the other end is worth the effort. And sometimes the Word that breaks through the worst audio quality is the one that carries the most weight. Finally, I am sitting with the unnamed sister mentioned only briefly — described as part of the inner circle to whom the corpus was released for immediate testing and witness. Her presence matters theologically even in its brevity. The testimony of the Spirit is never solitary. In Deuteronomy 19:15, every matter is established by two or three witnesses. The apostolic pattern in Acts consistently involves the community as the confirming body of prophetic revelation. The corpus was not released to the public first — it was released to the immediate circle of trust. This is wisdom. What is given in the secret place is tested first among the faithful before it is handed to the wider Body. That ordering is not timidity; it is stewardship. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation presses upon us is the tendency to separate our vocational and spiritual lives so completely that we cannot recognize the Spirit moving in the middle of a business conversation. The men on this call were simultaneously discussing commission structures and speaking prophetically about kenosis and flaming swords. This is not confusion — this is integration. The modern Western Christian has often been taught to clock in and clock out spiritually, reserving the sacred register for Sunday mornings. But the Kingdom does not observe those boundaries. We must develop the ear to hear the prophetic frequency even when it is embedded in the noise of the ordinary workday. A second challenge concerns our relationship with obedience windows — those seasons of calling that demand faithfulness before clarity. Many believers today abandon assignments before their completion because the purpose has not been revealed ahead of time. The consumer Christianity of our era promises clear outcomes and visible metrics before demanding investment. But the pattern of Scripture runs in exactly the opposite direction: obey first, understand later. If we refuse to walk in the dark until the path is lit, we will never arrive at the place where the light finally breaks. Third, the kenotic pattern — emptying, releasing, waiting to be refilled — directly confronts the hoarding posture that afflicts both individual believers and institutional ministries. There is a profound fear in many quarters about releasing what has been accumulated: the teaching archive, the theological formation, the spiritual insight. But what is not released cannot become multiplied bread. The five loaves and two fish had to be broken and given away before they became enough for five thousand. The season of release is not loss — it is the precondition of multiplication. Fourth, the intercessory and ancestral calling my brother described challenges our deeply individualistic spirituality. Most Western Christians are trained to think about their faith in terms of personal salvation and personal sanctification. The idea that one is called to be an ancestor — to stand in the gap generationally, to build something whose harvest one may never personally eat — is genuinely foreign to the therapeutic, self-centered spiritual culture we swim in. But Abraham did not see the fulfillment of the promise. Neither did Moses enter the land. Neither did David build the temple. Generational faithfulness is its own form of greatness, and we must recover it. Fifth, the image of the murmuration invites us to reconsider our models of leadership and Kingdom coordination. Much of what passes for spiritual community in our day is still organized around a single directional voice — one teacher, one pastor, one platform. But murmurations have no central director. They operate through proximity, responsiveness, and shared attunement to the same Spirit-wind. The question for the Body of Christ is whether we are close enough to one another, and yielded enough to the same Spirit, for that kind of coherent, Spirit-led movement to emerge among us. Sixth and finally — the broken audio itself. I want to say this gently but clearly: there is a temptation to wait for perfect conditions before we speak what the Spirit is pressing. Better equipment, a quieter space, a more prepared audience. But the canon does not record the Spirit waiting for ideal acoustic conditions. He spoke through a burning bush, through a still small voice after wind and earthquake and fire, through a donkey, through a vision in the middle of the night. Our job is not to produce clean conditions for revelation. Our job is to press through the interference and keep speaking. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This call is an invitation to develop what I would call *integrated perception* — the capacity to hold the sacred and the practical in simultaneous, non-competing awareness. Spiritual maturity is not the ability to retreat into the sacred and ignore the mundane; it is the ability to see the sacred *in* the mundane without collapsing the distinction between them. Growing in this perception means training ourselves to notice when the Spirit is threading a word through an ordinary conversation, a name, a coincidence, a broken connection — and learning to receive it without either dismissing it as noise or over-interpreting it as elaborate prophecy. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience window is not a metaphor — it is a recurring structural reality in the life of those whom God is forming. Kingdom obedience calls us to complete what we were assigned in the dark, before the purpose is visible, before the audience is assembled, before the fruit is measurable. It calls us to release what we have accumulated at the appointed time, trusting that the emptying is not ending but preparation. And it calls us to hold our networks, our platforms, our relationships, and our resources as stewardships — channels through which Kingdom advance flows — rather than assets to be protected for personal security. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation calls forth is one of attentive, expectant availability — not the performance of spiritual gifts on demand, but the quiet readiness of a servant in the household who keeps the lamp trimmed and the oil full. It means stewarding what is spoken in the secret place with reverence before releasing it to the wider circle. It means standing in the gap for others without needing credit or recognition for the intercession. And it means accepting that some of what we are building, we are building for the ones who come after us — learning to find joy and faithfulness in being an ancestor rather than insisting on being the generation that sees the full harvest. ## A Closing Prayer Father, we come before You as those who have heard Your voice through broken audio and interrupted sentences, through obedience walked in the dark and words spoken in the secret place with no recorder running, and we thank You — because You are not diminished by our limitations, and Your Word is not weakened by our noise. We stand in agreement with what You are doing in the lives of Your servants: the completing of commissions given in faithfulness, the releasing of what was accumulated in the hidden season, the emptying that precedes the next filling. Lord, we ask that the κένωσις (keh-NO-sis) that marked Your Son — the willingness to pour out, to be emptied, to take the form of a servant — would mark those You are forming for generational Kingdom work. Guard the thresholds. Let the flaming Word do its cutting and proving work. Establish the ancestral intercessors in their stations. Bring the murmurations into coherent Kingdom movement, each yielded member close enough to the others and attuned enough to Your Spirit-wind to move as one. Let the obedience windows remain open in us even when we cannot yet see the purpose, and let the releasing of what You have given us become the bread that multiplies for the thousands we will never personally meet. You are worthy of the harvest, Lord. We agree with Your will between the heavens and the earth.
