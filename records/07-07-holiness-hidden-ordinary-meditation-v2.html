# 07-07 Personal Meditation: Holiness in Ordinary Marriage Moments **Title:** 07-07 at 14:52 — The Holiness Hidden in the Ordinary: A Meditation on the Sacred Threshold of Home **Recorded:** 2026-07-07 14:52:36 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Personal Meditation ## Opening Observation There is something quietly arresting about this brief exchange — a husband and wife, a hot dog, a movie, a workshop, a return time of two-thirty. What the world would dismiss as trivial domestic noise, I find myself unable to simply pass over. Something in the Spirit will not let me treat the covenant threshold of home as spiritually insignificant. The ordinary rhythms of married life — checking in, saying goodbye, saying *love you* — these are not interruptions to sacred living. They may be among its most faithful expressions. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ has often been trained to look for God in the spectacular — in the pulpit moment, the corporate altar call, the prophetic declaration over thousands. Yet Scripture does not restrict the presence and will of God to public stages. The covenant of marriage, established before the fall of man and sanctified anew in Christ, is itself a living theological statement about the nature of God’s love for His people. When a husband says *I will come home quickly* because he has an unexpected day off and his wife’s voice carries a quiet *yay* of gladness — something holy is happening. How we treat that holiness, whether we learn to see it or habitually overlook it, shapes the kind of disciples, spouses, and witnesses we become. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation A secular observer listening to this exchange would hear nothing more than background noise — two working adults coordinating logistics, the low-bandwidth communication of a busy married couple managing schedules and minor comforts. The hot dog is just food, the movie is just entertainment, the workshop is just employment, and the two-thirty return is just time management. The natural mind, shaped by a culture that measures significance through scale, novelty, and emotional intensity, would file this entire recording under *unremarkable* and move on. In a world that celebrates the dramatic and commodifies intimacy, a quiet mid-morning check-in between spouses generates no cultural capital and inspires no viral moment. It is, by every worldly metric, forgettable. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind is precisely calibrated to miss what the Spirit prizes most. It cannot perceive that faithfulness is built not in the grand gesture but in the thousand small ones — that the fabric of a covenant is woven from exchanges exactly like this one. The capacity to recognize the sacred embedded in the routine requires a kind of spiritual sight that must be cultivated through abiding. A mind formed only by worldly metrics will spend its entire life waiting for significance to arrive while holiness is quietly folding laundry in the next room. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with this short recording — this small window into an ordinary morning — I am struck by how much grace is compressed into how few words. My wife is resting. I am heading back to the workshop. We are watching movies, eating hot dogs, navigating OB calls and unexpected days off. And yet underneath all of it, there is a covenant structure quietly holding. There are two people who still say *love you* at the end of a logistical exchange, still ask *what time are you coming home*, still register a small joy when an unexpected free afternoon opens up. That is not nothing. In the Kingdom, that may be everything. The Song of Solomon — often spiritualized to the point of abstraction in the Western evangelical tradition — opens with the raw voice of longing: *“Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth, for your love is better than wine”* (Song of Solomon 1:2). What is preserved in that ancient book is not idealized romance but *embodied covenant desire* — the genuine pleasure of presence, the ache of absence, the relief of return. I hear an echo of that in this exchange. The *yay* when I say I’m coming home at two-thirty is not an insignificant emotional blip. It is a small sound of what Solomon’s bride felt when she heard her beloved’s voice at the door. Scripture frames marriage as something far larger than a social arrangement. In Ephesians 5:31–32, Paul quotes Genesis 2 and then immediately says *“This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church.”* The Hebrew word for *mystery* that Paul is reaching toward in the Greek is μυστήριον (mystērion, moos-TAY-ree-on) — a sacred secret, something hidden that has now been disclosed. The marriage covenant was always meant to be a living icon of the relationship between Christ and His Bride. Which means that how a husband and wife speak to each other, how they anticipate each other’s homecoming, how they hold each other’s weariness with gentleness — all of it participates in a theological reality that is simultaneously domestic and cosmic. I notice in this exchange something I want to name carefully: *tenderness under tiredness*. My wife says she is tired, that she is just laying down, and I hear myself say *okay, you just rest*. There is something of the Shepherd in that small permission. Psalm 23 does not describe rest as laziness or spiritual failure — *“He makes me lie down in green pastures”* (Psalm 23:2). The Shepherd initiates the lying down. He creates conditions for it. When a husband learns to create conditions for his wife’s rest rather than demanding performance from her exhaustion, he is participating in a pattern that originates in the character of God Himself. This is not sentimentality. This is covenant theology lived out in real time. The Spirit is pressing something else here — the quiet discipline of *checking in*. We live in an age of radical individualism where privacy and autonomy are elevated to near-sacred status even within marriages. Two people can share a roof, a bed, a last name, and never truly check on each other. But there is something deeply covenantal about the habit of keeping account of where the other person is — not as surveillance, but as shepherding care. *What time are you coming home?* is a small act of remaining oriented toward one another. Proverbs 27:23 says, *“Be diligent to know the state of your flocks, and attend to your herds.”* The marital household is among the first flocks entrusted to a man’s stewardship. Knowing where your wife is, when she is resting, when she needs you to come home — this is not micromanagement. It is the practice of a watchful shepherd-husband. There is a Hebrew word that carries enormous weight in the Old Testament covenant context: שָׁלוֹם (shalom, shah-LOHM) — completeness, wholeness, well-being, the state of nothing missing and nothing broken. True shalom is not merely the absence of conflict; it is the active presence of flourishing. I look at this exchange and I see two people laboring, however imperfectly and simply, toward שָׁלוֹם (shah-LOHM) with one another. The check-in call itself is a shalom-building act — I want to know you are okay, I want you to know when I am coming, I want us to remain connected across the distance of a morning apart. What strikes me now is the way this recording captures something about the incarnation. When God chose to dwell among us, He did not begin His earthly ministry with a great public address. He spent approximately thirty years in a home in Nazareth — eating, working, presumably having ordinary conversations with Mary, with Joseph, with neighbors. The Eternal Word subjected Himself to the rhythms of domestic life. Luke 2:51 simply says *“He went down with them and came to Nazareth, and was subject to them.”* The Greek word for *subject* here is ὑποτάσσω (hypotassō, hoo-poh-TAHS-so) — to arrange oneself under, to submit to order. The Son of God practiced ὑποτάσσω (hoo-poh-TAHS-so) in a household before He ever preached in a synagogue. This sanctifies the domestic. It does not relegate it to secondary importance. The moment in this exchange that I return to most is the closing — *“Bye, love you. Bye, love you.”* So unremarkable on the surface. And yet I want to hold it up to the light, because the habit of speaking love as a regular, reflexive closing to an ordinary conversation is a spiritual discipline. Love spoken habitually — not performatively, not theatrically, but simply and consistently — is what builds the kind of covenant security that sustains two people through seasons far harder than a busy morning. 1 Corinthians 13 does not describe love as a feeling that arrives uninvited; it describes love as a pattern of behavior that must be practiced, chosen, and embodied. *“Love suffers long and is kind”* (1 Corinthians 13:4). The patience and kindness described there are not dramatic. They are dailiness. They are *bye, love you* on an ordinary Monday. I find myself confronted here by a question the Spirit seems to be asking beneath the surface of this simple recording: *Am I building the kind of marriage that becomes a sanctuary?* Not a stage, not a performance, not a projection of spiritual aspiration — but a true covenant dwelling where two people are genuinely safe, genuinely known, genuinely tended. The tabernacle in the wilderness was not the grandest structure in the ancient Near East. It was a tent. But it was *ordered* — every cubit measured, every piece of furniture placed with intentionality, every threshold acknowledged as holy. The marriage home can be that kind of space. Not perfect, not pristine, but *ordered toward God’s presence* by the daily practice of faithfulness in small things. One final thread I want to trace: the joy of anticipated return. *“I will come home quickly.”* There is something eschatological hidden in that sentence. The whole of Christian life is structured around awaiting a homecoming — the return of the Bridegroom, the reunion of Christ and His Bride, the restoration of all things. Revelation 22:20 closes the canon with the cry: *“Even so, come, Lord Jesus!”* And the Bridegroom’s answer: *“Surely I am coming quickly.”* When a husband says to his wife *I am coming home quickly* and her heart registers gladness, they are — perhaps without knowing it — enacting in miniature the longing and the promise that structures all of redemptive history. Every homecoming in a covenant household is a small sermon about the Great Return. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation surfaces is the tendency to compartmentalize the sacred and the domestic — to believe that spiritual life happens *over there*, in the prayer closet or the sanctuary, while ordinary life happens *over here*, in the kitchen and the car. Scripture resists this division at every turn. Deuteronomy 6:7 instructs that the commands of God are to be spoken of *“when you sit in your house, when you walk by the way, when you lie down, and when you rise up.”* The household was always intended to be a place of ongoing theological formation. We must resist the drift toward a spirituality that abandons the home as unworthy of theological attention. A second challenge is the cultural pressure to treat verbal affection as excess or weakness — to see *love you* as a rote phrase drained of meaning through repetition. The Kingdom inverts this. Repetition in love is faithfulness, not emptiness. The angels cry *“Holy, holy, holy”* without ceasing (Revelation 4:8), and their repeated declaration does not diminish in meaning — it accumulates in weight. The habit of regular, spoken affection in marriage is a spiritual practice, not a social nicety. Third, this exchange gently confronts the modern Western Christian tendency to evaluate a spouse’s worth by productivity rather than presence. My wife was resting. Tired. Laying down in the morning. And the right response — the covenant response — was *okay, you just rest*. In a culture that quietly idolizes output, learning to honor a spouse’s need for rest without attaching shame or urgency to it is a counter-cultural and deeply Christ-shaped act. Fourth, there is an implicit challenge here about the ministry of attentiveness. Asking *what time are you coming home* is an act of pastoral care within the covenant. Modern life conspires to make spouses strangers — two individuals sharing logistics but not truly shepherding one another. The discipline of genuine attentiveness — *where are you, how are you, when will I see you* — is a form of love that must be intentionally protected against the erosion of busyness. Finally, this meditation challenges any reading of the Song of Solomon or Ephesians 5 that abstracts the theology of marriage so completely that it loses its grounding in embodied, everyday covenant life. The mystery of Christ and the church is not meant to float above actual marriages — it is meant to be visible *through* them, in the very texture of how husbands and wives speak to each other, rest near each other, and return to each other. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity is not only measured by what we do in public — it is measured by what we do when no one is watching except our spouse and the Lord. The capacity to bring the same reverence, patience, and love to an ordinary mid-morning phone call as we would bring to a prayer meeting is a mark of deep formation. Let us ask the Spirit to sanctify the dailiness of covenant life, training our eyes to see the holy in the habitual. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Kingdom obedience includes the obedience of coming home — of being present, of honoring commitments made to the person we are covenanted with before God. It includes the obedience of rest — releasing a tired spouse from the pressure to perform or produce. And it includes the obedience of love spoken plainly and consistently, not because it is felt in a peak moment, but because covenant love is a vow, not a feeling. **For Prophetic Posture:** Those who would speak prophetically into the world about the love of God must first learn to embody it in their homes. The marriage covenant is itself a prophetic act — a declaration before heaven and earth that covenantal love is real, that faithfulness endures, that return is promised. Let us guard that witness fiercely, not only in doctrine but in the small moments that no platform will ever capture. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as a co-heir of Your Kingdom, standing between heaven and earth as one of Your servants, giving voice to what Your Spirit has pressed through something as simple as a morning phone call. I thank You that You are the God who is present in the ordinary — who sanctified the household, who came and dwelt among us in the dailiness of Nazareth before the miracle at Cana, who calls the marriage covenant a great mystery pointing to Your own heart for Your people. Lord, I ask that You make me faithful in the small things — faithful in the check-in call, faithful in the permission to rest, faithful in the *love you* at the end of an unremarkable exchange. Let the covenant of my marriage be a living sanctuary, a dwelling ordered toward Your presence, a prophetic witness to a watching world that the Bridegroom is coming and that covenant love endures. Guard what has been given to us. Sanctify every threshold, every goodbye, every homecoming. May the Great Return of the King find us faithful in exactly these things — not only in the grand declarations, but in the quiet, steady, dailiness of loving well. To You be all glory, all honor, and all thanksgiving — now and into the ages of ages.
