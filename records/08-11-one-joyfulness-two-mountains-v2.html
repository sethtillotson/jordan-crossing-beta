# 08-11 Call Reflection: Old Man and New Man in Brotherly Correction **Title:** 08-11 at 15:40 — One Joyfulness on Two Mountains **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I called my brother Samuel while cleaning the car in the heat, and what began as banter about weather and grocery stores kept sliding, again and again, into the deep places. The Spirit kept surfacing one thread through all our wandering: the difference between the old man and the new man, and how love that corrects is the very hand that helps another die and rise. I am seeing that a phone call about caramel ice cream and Paris flights carried, underneath it all, a wrestling over identity, attraction, marriage, and the peril and gift of prophetic tandem. **Why This Matters:** This matters because so much of our discipleship happens exactly here — not in a pulpit but in a sweating garage, phone pressed to the ear, half-distracted, half-tender, moving between jokes and confessions. The Body of Christ is knit together in these unremarkable exchanges where one brother says to another, “I need you to become the new man,” and the other receives it. If we only look for God in the sanctified moments, we will miss how He weaves transformation through the ordinary. Endurance, holiness, and love are forged in the small faithfulness of brothers who tell each other the truth. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation Read naturally, this was two young men catching up: one navigating a new leadership role and a fresh crush on a Christian coworker in Paris, the other exhausted by a hot factory and a pregnant wife with a list of chores. The talk of blocking a former friend, of “making Emily and Chloe grieve the man you will become,” reads as ordinary self-improvement psychology — a man cutting off old attachments to reinvent himself. The mention of a woman met on social media with who I share “revelations” could be interpreted by a suspicious mind as an emotional affair dressed in spiritual language, and the whole conversation as two friends validating each other’s ambitions. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind, watching from outside, can only ever tally the surface data — the crush, the blocked contact, the shared prayers — without perceiving the invisible war being fought inside each choice. It cannot see that Samuel’s cutting off of Chloe is not mere self-help but a small crucifixion, nor that my careful guarding of the connection with sister Katie is not repression but vigilance against a documented spiritual snare. The flesh reads attraction and calls it opportunity; it reads correction and calls it control. Only the Spirit discerns that some fires must be starved not because the warmth is evil, but because the house is not ours to burn. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I listened to Samuel describe stepping into his new work — working full hours now, solving a new problem every five minutes, realizing “no one knows this business better than me” — I heard him name something true and dangerous in the same breath: “a gift and a curse.” The Spirit pressed on me how often mastery becomes a mountain of pride the moment it becomes a mountain of competence. Samuel caught it himself, speaking of grown men with mortgages thanking him. There is a fear of the Lord that must accompany every gift, lest the gift become the god. I noticed how naturally our conversation turned to the seasons — winter, summer, harvest, the dead time. My brother said the older he gets, the more he loves winter, and something in me wanted to sanctify that instinct. Scripture keeps its own seasons: “To everything there is a season, a time for every purpose under heaven” (Ecclesiastes 3:1). We spoke of preparing for winter by chopping wood, preparing for harvest in summer, and I am seeing that the wisdom of aging is precisely this — learning that no season is to be resented, because each one is preparing you for the next. The sluggard who will not plow because of the cold shall beg in harvest and have nothing (Proverbs 20:4). When Samuel said “your God is my God” and I heard my own words echo back — that odd possessive, *my* Jesus — the Spirit arrested me on the beauty of covenantal belonging. This is Ruth’s confession: “Your people shall be my people, and your God, my God” (Ruth 1:16). The possessive is not presumption; it is relation. To say *my* God is to stand inside the covenant where the Almighty is not a distant deity but the One who has bound Himself to me by the blood of His Son. Then came the phrase that lingered longest: “one joyfulness.” Samuel spoke of finding commonality with Amelia “within the spirit and within ourselves.” I hear in this a longing for oneness, and I do not doubt its sweetness. Yet I also hear the Spirit’s caution woven through my own later confession, because oneness in the spirit between a man and a woman is a holy fire that must be tended with trembling. For I told my brother of the strange gift given between sister Katie and me — what the canon might call the pattern of two prophets on two mountains, receiving the same revelation from two sides, “one exterior, one interior.” This is not unlike Peter on the rooftop and Cornelius in his house, each given half of one vision, converging by the Spirit (Acts 10). But I confessed the trial that rides alongside the gift: such tandem revelation, born of God and not of us, can be hijacked by the emotions into elation that destroys marriages and diverts saints from the path. I had to pray in the Spirit — προσευχόμενος ἐν πνεύματι (proseuchomenos en pneumati, pros-yoo-KHO-men-os en NYOO-mah-tee) — praying in the Spirit, laboring so that no triggered affection would mistake God’s fire for my own. The Spirit reveals here the hard doctrine of the two men: “that you put off, concerning your former conduct, the old man which grows corrupt according to the deceitful lusts… and that you put on the new man which was created according to God” (Ephesians 4:22–24). The old man is παλαιὸς ἄνθρωπος (palaios anthrōpos, pah-lai-OS AN-thro-pos) — the former self, the man crucified with Christ. When Samuel said, “Seth doesn’t need old Sam, Seth needs new Sam,” he was preaching the gospel to himself. The blocking of Chloe was not cruelty; it was a man refusing to resurrect what God had already put to death. I was struck by the counsel his friend gave him: “You need to make Emily and Chloe grieve the man you are going to become.” At first this sounds harsh, but the Spirit shows me its deeper truth — the new man is so radically other that the old attachments can only mourn him, because he is no longer available to them. This is the cost of being crucified with Christ: “I have been crucified with Christ; it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me” (Galatians 2:20). The people who loved the old you must sometimes grieve, because the man they knew is in the tomb. Then Samuel spoke the thing that humbled me most. He said that when I correct him, the first thing he hears is “anger, annoyance, death, and asshole” — but when he sits with it, the meaning comes. Something arrests me here about the mechanics of loving correction. My part is only to speak it with grace; the landing of it in the heart is the Holy Spirit’s work entirely. “Faithful are the wounds of a friend, but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful” (Proverbs 27:6). I do not have to force the wound to heal; I only have to make it faithful. And he answered with the covenant of brotherly love, invoking that we love “as Christ loved the church.” I am seeing that this is the whole architecture of true fellowship — that a brother’s correction, when it flows from ἀγάπη (agapē, ah-GAH-pay) — covenantal, self-giving love — is received not as jealousy but as the very care of Christ. “As iron sharpens iron, so a man sharpens the countenance of his friend” (Proverbs 27:17). Iron sharpening iron makes sparks and noise; it is not gentle, but it is love. Both of us, almost reflexively, passed the glory upward. He honored me; I redirected to “Jesus, the originator of my transformation”; and he said, “as will I.” The Spirit reveals in this the safeguard against the whole conversation curdling into mutual flattery. When a man says “I am honored you see me that way, but I pass the glory to Jesus,” he is refusing to become an idol to his brother. We are only ever pointing past ourselves to the Author and Finisher of faith (Hebrews 12:2). What lands on me finally is how seamlessly the holy and the mundane braided together — Paris flights, sea salt caramel, a pregnant wife blowing up the phone, and in the same breath, prophets on mountains and old men put to death. The Spirit is not embarrassed by our ordinariness. He walks through the garage and the grocery store. He speaks in the space between the ice cream aisle and the confession of temptation. This is the incarnational shape of grace — the Word made flesh dwelling among the ordinary details of two brothers’ Tuesday. So as I hold this whole rambling call — the sweat, the caramel, the harvests, the confessions — I see that the Spirit was quietly building the same house in two men at once: guard your heart, crucify the old man, submit every affection to the Lord, correct in love, receive correction humbly, and give all glory to Christ. It was never really about the ice cream. ## Implications and Challenges I want to begin with grace, because this whole exchange was itself an act of grace between brothers, and the Lord was clearly present in it. **First,** the modern Christian man often has no one who will tell him the hard word. Samuel’s willingness to hear “anger, annoyance, death, and asshole” and *stay* until the meaning arrived is rare. We have traded faithful wounds for the shallow affirmation of a culture that calls all correction “toxic.” The challenge: do I have a brother who can wound me faithfully, and can I sit with the wound long enough to let the Spirit interpret it? **Second,** the tandem revelation with sister Katie must be held with sober trembling, and I say this to examine my own heart as much as anyone’s. A genuine gift of God can become the doorway of the enemy when spiritual intimacy outpaces spiritual accountability. The safeguard is not secrecy but exposure — bringing it into the light of brothers, spouses, and unceasing prayer, as I sought to do even in this call. **Third,** the counsel to “make them grieve the man you will become” is powerful but must remain tethered to Christ, not to self-reinvention. The world preaches becoming your “best self”; the gospel preaches dying so Christ may live. The new man is not a better version of the old man — he is a resurrected man. Let us not baptize ambition and call it sanctification. **Fourth,** competence is a subtle idol. Samuel naming “no one knows this business better than me” as both gift and curse is spiritually mature. The Western church often crowns capability and calls it calling. Mastery without the fear of the Lord becomes a throne. **Fifth,** we must not despise the ordinary. Some believers wait for mountaintop encounters while God is speaking in the garage, the grocery store, the sweat-soaked rag. The challenge is to keep the ears of the spirit open even when the mouth is talking about caramel. And I end here with grace: the Lord met two flawed, joking, sweating brothers exactly as they were, and He was not far from either of us. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity is learning to receive correction past the first flush of anger, and learning to give it past the fear of being disliked. It is holding gifts — competence, revelation, attraction, connection — with open, trembling hands, refusing to let any good thing become a god. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience here looks like the blocking of Chloe, the guarding of the heart in the Katie connection, the redirecting of glory to Jesus. It is the daily, unglamorous work of putting off the old man and putting on the new, of starving fires that would burn down houses that are not ours. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophet on the mountain must remember there is another prophet on another mountain, and that no revelation is safe until it is submitted, prayed over, and held in the fear of the Lord. To watch, wait, and yield means bringing even our sweetest spiritual gifts under the discipline of accountability and unceasing prayer, so the fire that came from God is not stolen by the flesh. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I thank You that You are not a God confined to sanctuaries, but the One who walks through garages and grocery aisles, who speaks through the banter and the confession of brothers. As Your servant and co-heir in Christ, I make agreement with Your will over Samuel and over me: put the old man fully to death, and raise up the new man created in righteousness. Guard the gift You have given between the mountains, and let no fire from heaven be counterfeited by the flesh. Teach us to wound one another faithfully and to receive the wounds until Your Spirit interprets them. I lay every crush, every competence, every revelation, every marriage upon Your altar, asking that You keep us humble, keep us accountable, and keep us Yours. Let all the glory return to Jesus, the Originator and Finisher of every transformation. With thanksgiving, in the name above every name, amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |---------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Ephesians 4:22–24 | παλαιὸς ἄνθρωπος (palaios anthrōpos, pah-lai-OS AN-thro-pos) — old man / former self | The believer actively puts off the corrupt former self and puts on the new man created according to God; sanctification is death and resurrection, not mere improvement. | | Ruth 1:16 | אֱלֹהַיִךְ אֱלֹהָי (Elohayikh Elohai, el-oh-HA-yikh el-oh-HAI) — your God my God | Covenantal belonging makes the possessive “my God” an act of relation, not presumption. | | Galatians 2:20 | συνεσταύρωμαι (synestaurōmai, soo-nes-tow-RO-mai) — I have been crucified with | Union with Christ’s death; the old life is genuinely ended so Christ may live in us. | | Proverbs 27:6 | פֶּצַע (petsa, PEH-tsah) — wound | The faithful wounds of a friend are healing; loving correction is superior to flattering deceit. | | 1 Corinthians 14:15 | προσευχόμενος ἐν πνεύματι (proseuchomenos en pneumati, pros-yoo-KHO-men-os en NYOO-mah-tee) — praying in the Spirit | Spirit-empowered prayer as the guarding discipline over temptation and spiritual gifts. | | Ecclesiastes 3:1 | עֵת (et, ayt) — season / appointed time | Every season under heaven carries divine purpose and preparation. | | 1 John 4 / general | ἀγάπη (agapē, ah-GAH-pay) — covenantal love | Self-giving love is the ground of faithful correction, received not as jealousy but as Christlike care. | ---
