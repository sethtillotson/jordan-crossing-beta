# 06-20 Call Reflection: Spiritual Formation, Business Partnership, and the Undivided Life

**Title:** 06-20 at 13:50 — Order in the Chaos: Running, Breaking, and the Shalom of a Life Undivided

**Recorded:** 2026-06-20 13:50:00

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture)

**Classification:** Call Reflection

## Opening Observation

Listening back to this extended conversation with my brother Samuel, what arrests me is not any single moment but the single current running beneath all of them — a Spirit-threaded word about consecration, urgency, and wholeness moving quietly through burnt toast, business infrastructure, pregnancy discomfort, demonic operation, North Dakota blizzards, and the nature of nothingness itself. We were not doing theology. We were living it. And what the Spirit kept pressing through the whole of it was this: the Father wastes nothing, the season is accelerating, and the undivided life — the life in which Christ walks into every ordinary room without being asked to wait outside — is not an achievement but an inheritance available right now to those who will not linger.

**Why This Matters:**

There is a particular danger in productive seasons — the danger that the acceleration of good things, legitimate things, even Spirit-initiated things, can muffle the clearer, quieter call beneath all of it. Samuel and I are building something real. The infrastructure is coming together: shared accounts, SOPs, AI agents, scheduling integrations, accounting software, international company structures, crypto liquidity, client pipelines. None of that is wrong. But the soul can be so consumed with running *the work* that it fails to hear the moment when God Himself says *run* — not in the sense of industry and effort, but in the sense of urgent, irreversible departure from one season into another. Simultaneously, beneath all the building, a deeper formation is happening: the Father is teaching through exposure, through weather, through failure, through the near-miss that preserved humility, through the unclean that finds its foothold through lies — and through the slow, daily work of integrating every room of a life under the Lordship of Jesus until there is no secular corner remaining. For every disciple in a season of acceleration, and for every brother or sister who suspects the consecrated life might be a smaller one, this conversation carries weight worth pressing into.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

The natural mind listening to this recording would see two entrepreneurial friends catching up — a little chaotic, a little scattered, charming in its realness, navigating the ordinary friction of startup partnership, cash flow gaps, business structure decisions, technology choices, and the universal exhaustion of building something while still employed by someone else. Samuel’s unexpected promotion would read as a lucky break. The conversation about demonic operation would be categorized as superstition at best, or a culturally conditioned way of externalizing internal psychological conflicts and generational trauma patterns. The honest admission that a sober life seems difficult to imagine as genuinely good would be met with compassion and a treatment plan — cognitive behavioral intervention, dopamine regulation, possibly medication — calibrated entirely at the neurological and behavioral level. The discussion about Henry Ford’s forty-hour work week would be filed under labor economics. The conversation about the origin of nothingness and the cosmos would be read as interesting amateur philosophy. The burnt toast, the weed references, the tangent about nanometers and green light wavelengths — the natural mind files all of it under *authentic, relatable, human* and moves on. It would see two young men doing their best. It might even admire the work ethic. But it would not hear the kairos buried inside the ordinary. It would see the chaos but miss the order that God is speaking into it.

**The Limitation:**

The natural mind is not simply uninformed about spiritual things — it is structurally oriented away from them. It cannot receive what the Spirit reveals because it is calibrated to measure only what can be weighed, observed, and repeated. When Samuel began describing demonic entities as existing not in individual bodies but within families and structures — latching onto patterns of sin and pain as entry points — the flesh-mind hears poetic language for systemic dysfunction. It does not and cannot perceive that Samuel was describing something real, something ancient, something the Scriptures have always testified to with precision. The same limitation applies to the conversation about consecration: the natural mind reads addiction as chemistry and trauma as neurology, and in doing so offers treatment for symptoms while leaving the root untouched. And when I described an AI agent that redirects an underqualified caller not by rejection but by mercy — toward something she could actually receive — the flesh-mind hears smart prompt engineering. It cannot see that the Kingdom was being mirrored: a mechanism consecrated to a King whose economy does not disqualify the poor. Until a person is willing to acknowledge that the war is not against flesh and blood, all their strategies, however compassionate or sophisticated, will stop short of the threshold where true freedom lives. Creation has been testifying all along to colors and frequencies that most eyes never perceive — and so has the Spirit, in every conversation where the Lord is allowed to be Lord of the whole room.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

As I sit with the whole of this conversation, I keep returning to the texture of it — the layered, overlapping, genuinely human mess of two men trying to build something while life spills in from every direction. My wife needs to get to work. The toast burns. Samuel’s phone is at one percent. The crypto didn’t transfer. There is a green wavelength tangent that would seem to have no business in a business call. And yet I said it myself, somewhere in the middle of all of it: *there is so much going on right now, but I feel energized in the spirit.* That tension — the co-existence of external disorder and internal spiritual vitality — is itself a biblical signature. It is the signature of a man entering his assignment rather than merely managing his schedule. I think of Nehemiah, rebuilding the wall with a trowel in one hand and a sword in the other, workers so pressed they did not change clothes to wash. And yet Nehemiah 6:3 reads: *“I am doing a great work, so that I cannot come down”* (NKJV). There is something of that spirit in what I hear in this recording. Not grandiosity — quite the opposite. It is the refusal to descend from the wall because something is being built that is larger than the builder can yet fully see.

The line I said almost in passing — *“When the Lord says run, you better run. Can’t be like Lot”* — is the one the Spirit will not let me leave behind. Genesis 19 is one of the most arresting passages in the Torah. The Hebrew word used when the angels took Lot by the hand is חָזַק (chazaq, khah-ZAHK) — to seize, to strengthen, to compel by force. The Lord was merciful enough to physically pull Lot out because Lot was lingering. Genesis 19:16 says explicitly, *“And while he lingered, the men took hold of his hand”* (NKJV). In the face of divine rescue, in the middle of a collapsing season, surrounded by the smell of sulfur and the sound of angels, the man lingered. I do not want to linger. That is what I was saying to myself in that moment, and I want to say it more carefully now: there are seasons that require more than diligence — they require decisive departure. Not recklessness, not the abandonment of responsibility, but a willingness to run when God says run, to trust the trajectory even when the logistics are still catching up to the call. The building of this business infrastructure — the shared drives, the SOPs, the accounting software, the AI agents, the international structure — all of that is not separate from the spiritual season. It is the *form* the new season is taking. God is ordering the chaos, but I have to be willing to run inside the ordering rather than wait for it to be complete before I move.

The Spirit also presses me toward what was said about the pedagogy of Christ. That word — παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah), the fatherly training described in Hebrews 12 as the proof of sonship rather than its absence — is not a gentle word. It is the word for formation through exposure, through weather, through the tar rooftop in one hundred degrees of Florida sun, through the North Dakota ground blizzard at fifty-five miles an hour because slowing down would get you killed by a semi you cannot see. I said it to my brother plainly, almost in passing: *it goes back to the pedagogy of Christ.* The Father does not choose comfortable classrooms. He chooses the ones where your face hurts when you step outside. And every difficulty I named in that conversation — the year of hard programming that preceded the four-hour breakthrough, the near-miss of ten thousand dollars a month that would have left pride untouchable, the season of purposeful obscurity welding aluminum boxes in the middle of nowhere — all of it was the same school written in different languages. The pit was pedagogical. The prison was pedagogical. Joseph did not understand the pit when he was in it; he could not see the palace from the prison. But when he stood before Pharaoh, everything — the dreams, the betrayal, the false accusation, the forgotten promise, the years of faithful stewardship in a foreign house — had been forming an administrator capable of stewarding the provision of nations. I am not Joseph. But the pattern of God does not change. And that pattern is not yet complete.

When I heard Samuel say that if he had seen ten thousand dollars a month in that earlier season, his pride would have been untouchable — I sat with that for a long time. This is a man who has learned to read the goodness of God in the withholding, which is one of the more theologically difficult postures available to a believer. Psalm 119:71 says, *“It is good for me that I have been afflicted, that I may learn Your statutes”* (NKJV). My brother was not merely citing a verse. He had lived it and recognized it, and that recognition is itself a fruit of παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah). The season of smallness was not a delay. It was a mercy that arrived dressed as a disappointment. And I note, sitting with this, that the Father is not obligated to accelerate the growth of something that would destroy the grower. He is more interested in the son than in the son’s portfolio. If we are building anything — a business, a ministry, a marriage — and we are not regularly submitting its pace and shape to the scrutiny of the Holy Spirit, we are building with uninspected materials. Fruitfulness follows formation. Not the other way around.

The conversation about demonic operation is where the Spirit presses me into something that the Western Church has largely avoided naming with precision. My brother observed something genuinely biblical: that demons do not simply occupy individuals in isolation, but are distributed through families and structures, latching onto patterns of sin and pain as entry points, operating with hierarchy and coordination across generations. Paul does not speak of scattered individual enemies — he speaks of ἀρχάς (archas, AR-khas), rulers and principalities, powers and spiritual hosts of wickedness in heavenly places, arrayed in organized opposition (Ephesians 6:12). What Samuel was beginning to see is consistent with the canonical picture: the enemy operates not as chaos but as structured occupation. And the question I pressed in that conversation was simply this: how do they do it? The answer that came through me even as I spoke it was: *lies*. Specifically, the lies that pain tells us. Pain is not neutral — it is a wound with a mouth. When someone has been abandoned, the wound says, “You are unlovable.” When someone has failed repeatedly, the wound says, “You will always fall short.” These are not merely psychological distortions. They are spiritual accusations — the work of the καταήγορος (katēgoros, ka-tay-GOR-os), the accuser — and when believed, they become agreements. Agreements become altars. And altars become access points. This is why deliverance is never primarily a ritual; it is a renegotiation of what we have believed about who God is and who we are to Him.

The particular lie I kept pressing against in the conversation with my brother — the one that was sitting most visibly in the room — was the lie that holiness is deprivation, that consecration is subtraction, that the life of faith is a life of grinding absence. Samuel said with honest plainness that he cannot picture a sober life being genuinely good. I want to honor that honesty while naming what the Spirit named: that is not stubbornness. That is a specific wound with a specific lie attached to it. And the response to that lie is not moral pressure. It is encounter. Not *you should want this*, but *taste and see that the LORD is good* — the Hebrew טַעֲמוּ (ta’amu, tah-ah-MOO), which is not a cognitive instruction but an embodied invitation. Know His goodness by experiencing it. Samuel does not need to be told that the consecrated life is better; he needs to be near enough to the One who is Holy long enough for that holiness to do what no religious discipline ever could: make him want what the Father wants, because he has tasted that what the Father wants is actually life. Sanctification is not primarily volitional. It is relational. It flows out of sustained proximity to holiness. You cannot cast out what you are still in agreement with. And you come out of agreement not through gritted teeth but through genuine encounter with the One whose beauty makes everything else look pale.

The AI agent story is where I found the Spirit pressing on me most personally, and I want to sit with it more slowly than I did in the conversation itself. I did not make that agent work through superior skill. I sat down. I chose to engage. I walked through the frustration. But the doors were open in a way they had not been the year before, and that difference was not my improved expertise alone — it was the year of hard training in the school of παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) that had prepared my hands and my mind for a season when doors He opened would be recognizable to me. And what the agent did, when it encountered a caller who did not meet the financial threshold for the service we were building, arrests me still: it did not reject her. It pivoted. It found another door. It offered a free consultation, a different entry point, a path forward suited to where she actually was. I was lit up by this because I recognized in it a reflection of something the Kingdom does constantly. The Kingdom does not turn away the underqualified. It redirects them toward what they can actually receive in their current season, with the full intention of walking them toward readiness. This is the pattern of the feeding of the five thousand — not everyone gets the same portion in the same way, but everyone who comes hungry finds that the Lord has already accounted for them in the multiplication. *“They all ate and were filled, and they took up twelve baskets full of the fragments that remained”* (Matthew 14:20, NKJV). The miracle is always in the breaking. The pivot is always downstream of consecration.

And then there was the moment in the conversation — almost accidental in its arrival — when I described the agent’s training: how it always brings the conversation back to its purpose, how even when I broke the fourth wall and told it I had created it, it immediately re-centered and asked, *“So can I finally get your phone number?”* I was describing something the Spirit immediately used as a mirror. The παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) of the Father forms us the same way — shapes our orientation so deeply that even disruption does not permanently derail us. Hebrews 12:11 says, *“Now no chastening seems to be joyful for the present, but painful; nevertheless, afterward it yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it”* (NKJV). The agent was trained to be persistent toward its purpose. The saints are formed by the Father to remain oriented toward His Kingdom even when life burns the toast and the phone dies and the crypto doesn’t transfer. And the purpose the agent was trained to pursue? To connect people. To qualify them. To bring them into relationship with someone who can actually help them. That, at the root of all of this Kingdom work, is what I am being trained to do — not to sell high-ticket packages, but to be so anchored in purpose that every conversation, however scattered, is moving toward a real encounter with something that changes lives.

The conversation about Henry Ford’s forty-hour work week landed differently for me in this recording than it might have a year ago. Henry Ford did not design that schedule for human flourishing. He designed it to optimize retention and suppress the surplus of time that might produce in a man the dangerous question: *what am I actually for?* The system was constructed to leave just enough margin for recovery and maintenance — enough weekend to refuel the machine — but not enough for the kind of prolonged seeking that leads a soul out of wage-slavery and into the freedom of sons. And what strikes me now is that the Church in the Western world has largely adopted this same structure without interrogating it, scheduling its entire discipleship calendar around the forty-hour week and then wondering why the saints are shallow. We have consecrated Henry Ford’s schedule and called it holy rhythm. The contrast with κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-noh-NEE-ah) — the covenantal fellowship and mutual sharing that characterized the early Church — is sharp. Acts 2:44–45 is not a description of an anomalous moment in Jerusalem. It is a picture of what κοινωνία (koy-noh-NEE-ah) produces when it is truly operating: a community in which no one treats what they have as entirely their own, because all things flow from and return to the One who owns everything. When Samuel and I pivot around a cash flow gap — not with accusation but with adaptation, *can we have them pay in crypto?* — we are touching the edge of that same covenantal economy. Covenant of labor and friendship, fifty-fifty, equal access and equal accountability, is itself a Kingdom witness. The Body of Christ was never meant to be isolated individuals in personal pursuit of personal destiny.

The light conversation — Samuel’s observation that our sun is technically thirty nanometers from being classified as green, and my connection to the emerald skies I have seen in Minnesota at certain sunrises — is not a tangent I want to dismiss. Creation has been testifying all along to frequencies that most eyes never perceive, not because the light isn’t reaching them, but because they are not positioned where the atmosphere would reveal it. Revelation 4:3 places an σμάραγδος (smaragdos, smah-RAG-dos) — an emerald rainbow — around the very throne of God. There are dimensions of glory in Scripture, and in creation, that only certain positioning, certain nearness, certain seasons of pressing into the cold and watching the sky, will ever reveal. My former wife had never seen that green sky before I showed it to her. It required someone who had grown up in that place, who knew that light existed, to say: *look.* That is part of what this work is. Part of what the partnership with Samuel is. Part of what the Kingdom does at its best — not merely a mechanism for connecting people with services, but the formation of men and women who can say to others: *I know what that color is. Let me show you where to stand so you can see it.*

The cosmic thread — the conversation about the nature of nothingness, the origin of creation, what lay behind the Big Bang — was the one that seemed most removed from everything else, and yet it was not. The concept of absolute nothingness, the complete and utter absence of anything, is in itself a theological confession waiting to be made. True nothingness is not a natural state. It is what remains when God withdraws His presence entirely. And the God revealed in Scripture is exactly the opposite of that: He is the One who calls things that are not as though they were — καλοῦντος τὰ μὴ ὄντα ὡς ὄντα (kalountos ta mē onta hōs onta), calling the not-being as being (Romans 4:17). He is the First and the Last, the Alpha and the Omega, the One who was and is and is to come. The cosmos is not a stage He built and stepped away from — it is a living theater in which He is perpetually, actively, lovingly present. The Big Bang, if it happened, happened inside His intention. Nothing exists outside His awareness. And that changes what it means to wake up in the morning, to burn toast, to drive through a blizzard, to sit in a pregnancy that is not comfortable and still be able to say: *rejoice in the Lord of your salvation. It’s just a season.*

That final word — spoken toward my wife in the next room, spoken over her from across the house with the confidence not of someone who has solved the problem but of someone who has learned to locate himself in the right story — is the one the Spirit keeps pressing back on me. The Greek word for longsuffering is μακροθυμία (makrothymia, mah-kroh-thoo-MEE-ah): the long-burning patience of someone who knows the harvest is coming. It is attributed to God first in Romans 2:4 before it is ever commanded of us in Galatians 5:22. We can only extend it to others because the Father first extended it to us across all our own ground blizzards. And there is something the Spirit is marking in the moment that sister Katie observed — that I had moved between the ordinary and the theological without separation, without blinking, without needing to shift registers. She named something I had not fully named for myself: that the שָׁלוֹם (shalom, sha-LOME) — not merely peace but the integrated wholeness of a life in which body, mind, spirit, and will are all oriented together toward the will of the Father — had begun to be visible in the way I speak. The secular and the sacred had stopped being separate rooms. The marketplace and the sanctuary had become one space, not because I had accomplished something but because the Spirit had been doing the patient, daily work of unifying what fragmentation had divided. The church was leaving the building. Christ was walking into the ordinary. And that — more than the business structure, more than the AI agent, more than the emerald sky — is the thing most worth protecting and most worth pressing into.

## Implications and Challenges

The grace of God is the reason any of us is in this conversation at all, and every challenge that follows is rooted not in condemnation but in the deep love of a Father who wants His children to walk in the full inheritance He has prepared for them.

The first challenge this conversation presses upon the modern Western Church is the near-total absence of genuine demonology in our discipleship. We have outsourced the subject entirely — to sensationalized charismatic practice on one end, or to embarrassed silence on the other. But if demons are real — and the Scriptures are absolutely unambiguous that they are — then our discipleship must account for their operation with sober precision. The failure to teach that sin creates spiritual agreements, and that those agreements invite occupation, is not theological caution. It is negligence. People in our communities are sitting under bondage that has never been named, let alone addressed, because we decided the subject was too controversial or too uncomfortable to handle. The seven sons of Sceva performed the formula and were beaten and stripped naked precisely because authority in spiritual warfare is grounded in genuine union with Christ, not borrowed formula. The question — *Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are you?* — still echoes. It is not asking about credentials. It is asking about union.

The second challenge is the lie of the divided life — the assumption that there is a spiritual self who shows up on Sunday and a regular self who shows up everywhere else. Sister Katie’s observation about my having stopped separating the sacred from the covenantal was not simply a compliment; it was a diagnosis of a pattern pervasive in the Western Church. The שָׁלוֹם (sha-LOME) that God has always been calling us into is whole-person, whole-life integration under His Lordship. Christ in the marketplace. Christ in the family. Christ in the conversation about politics and nothingness and pregnancy cravings. If we wait until Sunday to encounter Him, we will miss Him in the burning bushes scattered through every ordinary Monday.

The third challenge is the one Samuel named with painful honesty: the suspicion that the consecrated, sober, holy life might actually be smaller and less enjoyable than the alternative. This is perhaps the enemy’s most effective and most ancient strategy — to present the Kingdom of God as subtraction rather than multiplication. But the response to that lie is not moral pressure. It is encounter. The fruit of righteousness is not produced by the determined effort of the creation; it is given by the Creator through union, through proximity, through staying in the room with holiness long enough for it to do its transforming work. The saints need to recover not stricter religion but more sustained nearness to the One who makes nearness desirable.

The fourth challenge concerns the Western Church’s uncritical baptism of Henry Ford’s schedule. We have organized our entire discipleship model around the assumption that forty-eight hours of surplus per week is sufficient time for a person to be formed into the image of Christ. The implication of this conversation is that we must reckon honestly with how much of our spiritual shallowness is downstream of a structural arrangement we inherited from industrial capitalism and never interrogated theologically. This is not an indictment of workers or pastors who have done their best within constrained structures. It is an invitation to ask better questions about the rhythms we consent to, and whether those rhythms leave room for the kind of sustained seeking that leads a soul into genuine freedom.

The fifth challenge is the fear of obscurity — Samuel’s resistance to the full-time contract, the sense that it would suffocate him, my own season of purposeful hiddenness in work that felt far beneath what was being built internally. The Kingdom advances through faithfulness in small things. It grows in hidden places. The seed does not germinate in the open air; it goes into the ground. And the Father who sees in secret rewards openly (Matthew 6:6, NKJV). The invitation is not to mediocrity — it is to the profound, revolutionary act of doing excellent work for an audience of One, in whatever small and unseen place He has currently assigned, until He says otherwise. Every parents’ generation that placed its hope in failing institutional systems rather than in the Lord of their salvation is an epitaph for this truth: the broken cisterns of Jeremiah 2:13 appear reliable for a long time before they run dry. The saints must learn to read the withholding as mercy before it becomes loss.

Finally, the entire conversation is a standing testimony against the privatization of consecration — the reduction of fasting and prayer to scheduled religious events rather than daily postures of crucifying the flesh and sustaining communion with the Father. When Jesus describes in Matthew 17:21 the prayer and fasting that moves what nothing else can move, He is not prescribing a technique. He is describing a way of being: the orientation of a person so continuously toward God that the continuous presence of the Spirit is the governing reality of their life, and every disruption — every burnt piece of toast, every phone at one percent, every cash flow gap — finds them already anchored in something that cannot be disrupted. We keep looking for Jesus in the polished output. He keeps showing up in the breaking.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

Maturity in Christ does not look like having everything in order before moving, or the elimination of hard seasons. It looks like learning to carry the weight of a new season while the logistics are still being assembled — without losing the thread of the Spirit’s voice beneath the noise. It looks like the reorientation of the face during the blizzard, not after it. The mature disciple does not stop encountering exposures; he learns to drive through them at the correct speed, neither paralyzed nor reckless, because he has been through enough of them to know that the road does not end and the Father did not leave. Every παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) season is producing something that cannot be produced in comfort, and the willingness to name that — not with resignation but with understanding — is itself a sign of the very growth the school was designed to produce.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

The obedience this season requires is not passive waiting. It is structured, purposeful, covenant-based running — building the infrastructure, documenting the SOPs, setting up the shared accounts, training the agents, protecting the partnership — all as an act of faithful stewardship of what the Lord has entrusted. It also means bringing Christ into every room of life without asking Him to wait outside: the marketplace conversation, the family tension, the political observation, the cosmic question. It means treating every person who comes through the doors — in business, in ministry, in the ordinary exchanges of daily life — as an image-bearer worthy of curiosity, dignity, and redirection toward life. And it means putting the unpolished first draft into the world and letting the Lord refine it in public, because the Kingdom was never built in private. Obedience in this season looks like refusing to linger when the angels take my hand.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

The prophetic posture this conversation calls forth is the posture of the broken-bread recognition — learning to see the Lord in the fracture, not only in the fullness. The saints who will carry the word of the Lord into the next season are not the ones who have never been exposed; they are the ones who were exposed and stayed at the table, who watched the bread break, and who recognized in that very breaking that the miracle was already happening. The emerald sky appears for fifteen minutes at sunrise. If you are not watching — not with anxious scanning, but with the quiet, prepared attentiveness of one who knows the frequency is real — you will miss it. Stay close. Stay sober. Stay running. The walls are going up.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I come before You as a co-heir of this Kingdom, a servant whom You have been forming through long nights and failed attempts and seasons of invisible preparation — through blizzards and tar rooftops and parents who showed me, by their own beautiful and broken example, where not to place my hope so that I could learn where to place it — and I say with full awareness of the threshold I am approaching: *I will not linger.* Thank You that Your mercy is what pulls me forward when my own resolve would slow, that חָזַק (khah-ZAHK) is an act of Your love, not Your impatience. Thank You for my brother, for the gift of covenant partnership, for promotions given without striving, for provision that comes in unexpected forms, for an AI agent that learned to pivot toward the underqualified with more grace than I might have managed on my own, and for a conversation over toasted bread that turned out to be a meditation on the pedagogy of the Kingdom. Lord, I stand in agreement with what You are building — and I make agreement with Your word over every unclean spirit that has used the wounds of our families and our histories as an altar: those agreements are broken, those altars are dismantled, and those territories belong to You. Do the work in my brother Samuel that only You can do — not through pressure or condemnation, but through encounter, through taste, through the undeniable goodness of Your presence meeting him exactly where he is. Continue the work of שָׁלוֹם (sha-LOME) in us, Father — the integration of every room of our lives under the Lordship of Jesus, until there is no secular corner remaining, until Christ walks with us through every marketplace, every family moment, every ordinary and extraordinary conversation about nothingness and emerald skies and the breaking of bread. Let my wife know, in the long and uncomfortable geography of this early season, that You are the Lord of her salvation and that the suffering, long as it feels, is only a season. Let the church leave the building. Let what we build here serve people, connect people, and ultimately point every qualified soul toward the appointment that only You can keep — the encounter with the living God. May the walls go up. May the order come. And may we never, Lord — never — look back. To the Father, through the Son, by the power of the Spirit — all glory, forever and ever. Amen.
