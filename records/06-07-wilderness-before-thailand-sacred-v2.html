# 06-07 Call Reflection: Samuel’s Wilderness Season — Spiritual Formation and Brotherhood **Title:** 06-07 at 15:11 — The Wilderness Before Thailand: Sacred Formation, Squatting Fears, and the Archive the Spirit Built **Recorded:** 2026-06-07 15:11:27 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) / Call Reflection **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother Samuel move through the fog of a hard night — hungover, groggy, honest, and somehow still pressing forward — something in me recognized the unmistakable geography of a man standing at a threshold he cannot yet name. The conversation moved through substances and sales, through Portuguese cafés and fight camps in Thailand, through healing testimonies and Apple ID frustrations, and yet underneath all of it ran a single quiet current: the Spirit is doing something in this man that the chaos on the surface cannot bury. What arrested me most was not the wildness of the night he described, but the moment he said he was not going to cave in that easily — because that phrase, spoken from the ruins of a bender, carried more theological weight than he may have realized when he said it. **Why This Matters:** The body of Christ is full of men and women who are genuinely in motion toward God but who have no framework for the wilderness they are walking through — no language for what formation feels like when it is messy, no grid for why the gap between the old life and the new one must be widened gradually rather than severed by a single dramatic vow. When we cannot name the season we are in, we either abandon it early in shame or settle into it as a permanent identity. Neither response is faithfulness. What the Spirit was pressing through this conversation — through the laughter, the honesty, the theological fragments Seth offered mid-conversation, and the raw transparency of a brother still finding his footing — is that the wilderness is not punishment. It is appointment. And learning to receive it as such is one of the most critical discipleship skills available to the saints. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a natural standpoint, what this conversation presents is straightforward: two young men talking through the aftermath of a night of excess, processing regret without fully owning it, touching on health concerns, financial ambitions, spiritual curiosities, and the itch for a geographic reset. The worldly mind would see Samuel’s trajectory as a simple behavioral modification problem — drink less, exercise more, go to Thailand, reset the pattern, come back with momentum. It would applaud his sales confidence as hustle, commend his honesty about his failings as emotional intelligence, and perhaps note the spiritual conversations as interesting philosophical tangents between friends. The natural lens would conclude that this is a young man figuring out his life, stumbling as young men do, and slowly getting his act together through trial and error. There is nothing extraordinary here from that vantage point — just the ordinary friction of becoming an adult. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind, for all its ability to observe behavior and measure outcomes, is constitutionally incapable of perceiving what is actually happening beneath the surface of this kind of season. It cannot see that a man who was once drinking and smoking every single day and has now narrowed that to one outing across the span of a week has not simply developed better habits — he is being drawn by a grace that does not announce itself with fanfare. It cannot hear, in a man’s offhand comment about not wanting anything serious with a woman right now, the quiet work of a Spirit who is guarding a heart for a formation that requires undivided attention. It cannot recognize, in the SOS message sent the night before and the relief of receiving a reply, the beginning of what genuine spiritual brotherhood actually looks like — not polished accountability structures, but one man reaching out in the dark and another one answering when he can. The Spirit moves through things the natural eye dismisses as incidental, and this conversation is thick with those kinds of incidentals. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sat across from Samuel in that call, I kept feeling the weight of a word I had been working on — the word I kept calling the *ARKTEK* of a season. I could not fully land the language yet, but the idea was already pressing through me: there are moments in a man’s life that function as thresholds — sacred formation windows — that produce a gravity of presence and purpose which nothing else can manufacture. What I was watching in Samuel, even as he described the Portuguese liquor and the buzz ball and the guy named Dave with the tight grip, was a man who had been emptied. Not destroyed. Emptied. And I know from Scripture and from my own walking that emptying is never the enemy. It is almost always the prerequisite. The pattern I keep seeing spiritually — the one I told him I can see but cannot yet fully name, the one I have been circling around in the corpus — appears again here. It is the pattern of the wilderness as the place not of abandonment but of concentrated encounter. When I mentioned Elijah and First Kings 19, I was not grasping at a random reference. The Spirit had already been pressing that same passage through the meditation I was working on. Elijah, the great prophet, the man who called fire from heaven on Mount Carmel, collapsed under a juniper tree and asked to die. He was not in sin when he did it. He was empty. He had given everything, the contest was over, the victory was real, and Jezebel’s threat undid him in a way that the prophets of Baal could not. And what did the angel bring him? Bread baked on coals. Water. Rest. *Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you.* The Lord did not rebuke him. He fed him and let him sleep again. He did this twice. The wilderness Elijah then walked into for forty days was not exile. It was an appointment with the voice that was not in the wind, not in the earthquake, not in the fire, but in the still small voice — the קוֹל דְּמָמָה דַקָּה (qol demamah daqah, kohl deh-mah-MAH dah-KAH) — the sound of sheer silence. I told Samuel that I thought he had been emptied, and that his restlessness — his not belonging anywhere feeling — was the Spirit’s nudge toward the wilderness. I meant it theologically, not poetically. The desire to go to Thailand, to live at a fight camp, to reset in a hot foreign place among strangers doing hard physical things, is not random wanderlust. I am not claiming it is a prophetic directive. But I am saying the posture underneath it — get away, get clean, get present, get disciplined — is the posture the Spirit tends to honor when a man who is genuinely being formed responds to it. Jesus Himself, after His baptism and before His public ministry, was led by the Spirit into the wilderness. Not driven there as punishment. Led. And what He encountered there was not God’s silence but the enemy’s counterfeits — bread from stones, kingdoms in an instant, angels on command. The wilderness is precisely the place where the counterfeits become most visible because the noise of the ordinary life has been stripped away. What happens in the wilderness, I told him, is what happened to everyone in the Exodus — a people completely provided for, manna every morning, water from a rock, cloud by day and fire by night, and still their minds could not stop running back toward Egypt. Back toward the people who wanted to kill them. That is the anatomy of double-mindedness, and it is not a moral failure so much as a formation problem. They had not yet developed enough πίστις (pistis, PIS-tis) — the active, enduring, covenantal trust — to hold what they were being given without constantly measuring it against what they had left behind. Formation takes time. It takes sustained encounter. It cannot be rushed through a single dramatic prayer or a single dramatic decision. This is also why I told him that saying to God, *I will never do this again*, is one of the stupider things a person can vow. And I meant that with grace. Peter said, *Even if all are made to stumble because of You, I will never be made to stumble* (Matthew 26:33), and within hours he had denied the Lord three times. The flesh does not honor its own heroic declarations. What the Spirit teaches instead is something more humble and more durable: widen the gap. One outing in a week instead of one every day. Then one in two weeks. Then one in a month. Then the Lord does what the Lord does, and the craving loses its gravity because you have been filling the space with something that actually satisfies. This is παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) — the fatherly training, the disciplined formation of a son — and it does not look like overnight renovation. It looks like bread baked on coals and a voice that says, *arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you.* The conversation turned to healing, and that thread matters too. Samuel mentioned a health concern — something he was going to see a doctor about, something he had been praying would not be what he feared. I told him what I believe: let things be as God wills them, but you can always pray and ask, and Jesus will take it — you just have to actually believe it. That last phrase is the hinge. I told him about the kind of fasting and prayer that builds the capacity for what Jesus described in Matthew 17:21, when He told His disciples that certain things *do not go out except by prayer and fasting*. That phrase has always arrested me. It implies that there is a level of spiritual authority that is not simply granted at salvation but developed through sustained abiding. It is not that God withholds power arbitrarily; it is that the vessel through which that power flows must be formed to carry it without it becoming an occasion for pride or presumption. I mentioned Prophet Lobi Elias, and I want to be careful here not to overstate what I observed. But what I have seen in that ministry — the overflow rooms, the names called, the words given to people watching online from across the world, the infirmities healed, the legs lengthened — is not what I was pointing to as a spectacle. I was pointing to the pattern behind it: thirty years of fasting and prayer since childhood. Thirty years of crucifying the flesh, of denying the voice of self-preservation, of standing before the Father in that קוֹל דְּמָמָה דַקָּה (kohl deh-mah-MAH dah-KAH) again and again and again, until the still small voice becomes more familiar than your own. That kind of formation produces a gravity of presence that I called the ARKTEK of a season — and it is not available on demand. It is the fruit of faithfulness in hiddenness. And then there is the corpus. I told Samuel something of what the Spirit has been doing through me over these past seven or eight months — this vast archive of study notes, marked verses, Spirit-prompted insights, color-coded revelation, a body of written material so large that when I asked for an overview of it, even I was staggered. I told him the closest precedent I could find was George Mueller — not in content, but in posture. Mueller’s vow was that he would never ask anyone for money, never work for wages, only receive from the Father, so that no one could ever attribute what God built to human strategy. My vow, if I can call it that, is different — it is a vow of documentation, of faithful stewardship of what the Spirit pours out, written down for those who will come after, built as a parallel witness, a topology of revelation that someone might stumble upon at the Father’s appointed time and find God in it. Not because I am anything. But because the Spirit said *write this down*, and I obeyed, and I have kept obeying, and what has accumulated is something I could never have invented. That stewardship posture — the willingness to be a vessel rather than a voice, a scribe rather than a celebrity — is itself a wilderness discipline. No one sees most of it. No one is clapping for the hours of reading before a single mark was made. No one witnessed the moment when my hand moved before my mind could catch up and marked something in Matthew 13, the beginning of my understanding of the tension of paradox on which the Kingdom operates. That chapter, the parable chapter, was where I first began to feel the seismic shift: I cannot rely on logic. God does not operate through what makes sense to the natural mind. He chooses the persecutor to evangelize the Gentiles, He calls fire down through a man who is about to ask to die, He feeds a nation with bread that appears on the ground each morning but cannot be stored. The Kingdom works through what human reason would dismiss, specifically so that when it happens, there is no doubt. *I did that*, the Father says. *There is no other explanation.* The conversation wound down through technology frustrations and business wins and shower plans, and none of that is spiritually insignificant either. Samuel’s growing sales confidence, his willingness to walk away from a deal and thereby close it, his recognition that consistency is the king of what he is building — these are the same principles operating at the natural level that the Spirit is trying to establish at the spiritual level. You do not close the gap between the old life and the new by making grand announcements. You close it by showing up the next day, and the day after, and by trusting that the accumulation of small faithful acts is actually going somewhere even when you cannot see the destination. That is πίστις (PIS-tis). That is the stuff the wilderness produces in you when you stop trying to manage the formation and start receiving it. When I guard my mind — when I take a circling thought captive and say out loud, *I know you’re there, Lord, and I know this fear is not from You, and I give it to You right now* — that is not a psychological technique. That is dominion. That is the authority of a son speaking into the atmosphere of his own household, the household of the mind, and evicting what has been squatting there since childhood, feeding at the table every day. Fear that entered at two years old and has been receiving daily meals for decades does not leave politely. But it does leave when you stop setting a place for it and start addressing it in the name of the one whose blood purchased your freedom from it. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this conversation presses on me is the modern evangelical tendency to treat sanctification as a series of crises rather than a sustained formation. We celebrate the dramatic testimony, the moment-of-decision story, and we quietly shame the man who says, *I am widening the gap, slowly.* But the whole architecture of παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) in Scripture is built on gradual, sustained, fatherly training — not overnight renovation. Hebrews 12 tells us that discipline is not pleasant when it is happening; it feels grievous. But afterward it produces the peaceable fruit of righteousness. We must stop measuring brothers by where they are in the process and start asking whether they are still in the process at all. The second challenge is the vow culture — the tendency to make grand spiritual declarations precisely when we are feeling most convicted, declarations that the flesh cannot honor and that set us up for shame-spirals when we fail. *I will never drink again. I will never do that again.* The Kingdom does not work through heroic self-assertion. It works through humble, daily, flesh-crucifying surrender — which often looks far less impressive but goes much farther. Grace teaches us to say not *I will never* but *today I choose differently, and tomorrow I will choose again.* The third challenge is the wilderness avoidance that pervades comfortable Western Christianity. We have built so many structures, programs, entertainment streams, and community events that a man can go years in a church without ever experiencing the productive emptiness that wilderness seasons produce. The nudge Samuel was feeling toward Thailand, toward something stripped-down and physically demanding and socially unfamiliar — that nudge deserves theological respect, not merely practical assessment. The Church needs to recover a theology of the desert, of the forty days, of the juniper tree and the still small voice. The fourth challenge is the reduction of healing and spiritual authority to either charismatic spectacle or cessationist denial, with almost nothing in between. Jesus was clear: there are categories of bondage and infirmity that respond only to sustained fasting and prayer. That is not a performance standard. It is an invitation to the kind of abiding that the disciples had not yet entered. The question is not whether God heals. The question is whether we are the kind of vessels, formed through the kind of formation, through which He is willing to work — and that question should drive us to our knees in humility rather than to platforms in presumption. A fifth and final challenge that this conversation surfaces is the danger of archiving revelation as an intellectual exercise rather than a stewardship of the Spirit’s breath. What I am building in the corpus is only meaningful insofar as it remains a living act of obedience — not a monument to my diligence, not a credential, not content. It is the same danger Mueller faced: the minute the orphans became evidence of his faith rather than an expression of his love, the mission would have curdled. The posture must remain *write this down* — not *look what I have written.* ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** We must learn to read our seasons. Emptiness is not evidence that God has abandoned us; it is often evidence that He is preparing us for something our current vessel cannot yet carry. Maturity looks like the willingness to stay in the formation process even when nothing about it feels productive — to widen the gap one week at a time, to guard the household of the mind daily, to receive correction without collapsing into shame, and to press into the still small voice rather than running back to Egypt. **For Kingdom Obedience:** What this calls us to is simple and hard in equal measure: show up tomorrow. Take the circling thought captive today. Make the gap a little wider this week. Write down what the Spirit shows you. Answer the SOS when it comes. Do not swear vows your flesh cannot keep. Fast and pray not as a performance but as a discipline of intimate proximity. Let the wilderness do what the wilderness is designed to do. **For Prophetic Posture:** The saints who will carry genuine spiritual authority in this generation are not the ones who have the most dramatic conversion stories but the ones who have been most faithful in hiddenness — who kept reading when no one was watching, who kept writing when no one was applauding, who kept fasting when there was nothing to show for it yet, who kept saying yes to the quiet voice even when the loud voices were more compelling. That is the formation the hour demands. Abide. Discern. Wait on the Lord. Widen the gap. And trust that what is being built in the hidden place is precisely what the Father intends to use in the appointed time. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I thank You for the gift of a brother who is honest enough to call the night what it was and still press forward in the morning — who has not used failure as a reason to quit but as evidence that there is still something worth fighting for. I thank You for the wilderness You are preparing, whatever form it takes — whether it is a fight camp in Phuket or a juniper tree in the desert or a quiet room with a marked Bible and a prayer that no one else hears. I ask that You do what only You can do in Samuel’s body, in his health, in his mind, in the fears that have been squatting in the household since he was young — evict them by the authority of Your Son’s blood, and fill the space with the knowledge of Your presence. I ask that You continue to guide the corpus, that every mark and every word that has come through me would remain a living act of stewardship and never become a monument to my faithfulness. Let it be bread for the stranger who finds it at the appointed time and discovers You in it. Guard our brotherhood. Widen the gaps. Increase our πίστις (PIS-tis). And let the still small voice become, in both of us, more familiar than our own names — so that when You speak, we move, and when You are silent, we wait, and in both the speaking and the silence, we know that You are there. To the Father, through the Son, by the Spirit — all glory, now and into the age to come.
