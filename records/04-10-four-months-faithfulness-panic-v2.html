# 04-11 Call Reflection: Spiritual Formation Through Four Months of Faithful Scripture Engagement **Title:** 04-11 at 02:05 — Four Months of Faithfulness: The Panic Has Gone but the Presence Remains **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture — Phone Call) **Classification:** Call Reflection --- ## ⚡ Opening Observation As I listened to this early-morning call with my brother Samuel, something quietly profound arrested me before either of us had the theological language to name it. He had woken at five in the morning in England, stumbled into the grief of old photographs and unanswered longing, and then — almost accidentally — looked up and saw something larger than his pain: consistency. Four months of showing up. Four months of Scripture. What struck me most deeply was not the eloquence of what was said, but the faithfulness of what was not abandoned. The Spirit does not always announce Himself with a banner; sometimes He reveals Himself through the simple, stubborn fact that you are still reading. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Body of Christ is saturated with beginning-stories and arrival-stories, but desperately thin on the testimony of the middle — the unremarkable, painful, pre-dawn faithfulness that no one photographs and no one applauds. Samuel did not call to report a breakthrough. He called to report that the panic attacks were gone and the pain was bigger, and that in some strange way that felt like progress. That tension — less chaos, more grief, but more Word — is one of the most honest descriptions of genuine sanctification I have heard in a long time. The saints need to hear this. Not because suffering is the goal, but because the presence of the Spirit through suffering is the testimony, and that testimony is built in the dark, at five in the morning, before the rest of the world is awake. --- ## 🌍 The Worldly Mind's Interpretation The natural mind, hearing this conversation, would likely frame it as two men processing heartbreak through a shared coping mechanism. One has ritualized his grief — checking his phone each morning, scrolling through photographs — and the other has built a mutual accountability structure that functions as emotional support. From a psychological lens, the consistency Samuel identified would be labeled a healthy behavioral pattern, a stabilizing routine that has reduced anxiety symptoms over time. The music playlists, the podcast recommendations, the shared reading — all of this would be understood as a social support network performing its expected therapeutic function. The world would affirm the friendship, commend the emotional honesty, and perhaps even celebrate the reduction in panic attacks as evidence that the coping strategy is working. It would see two wounded young men holding each other together, and it would call that healing. **The Limitation:** What the natural mind cannot perceive in this conversation is that what looks like a coping strategy from the outside is actually a covenant of obedience from the inside. The distinction is not subtle — it is total. A coping mechanism is something a person reaches for when the pain exceeds their tolerance. What Samuel described is something different: a practice that was begun in panic, sustained through pain, and has now outlasted the original crisis that started it. That is not coping. That is formation. The flesh interprets consistency as habit; the Spirit recognizes it as yieldedness. And the moment I described — the Spirit hovering above me and redirecting me from my own striving back to the Word — that is not a metaphor for mindfulness or self-regulation. That is the living reality of the Holy Spirit functioning as Counselor, exactly as the Lord promised He would. No psychological framework has a category for that, because no psychological framework begins with the premise that God is actually present and actually speaking. But He is. And that is the entire difference. --- ## ✝️ What the Spirit Reveals Through It What I am seeing in this conversation — and I want to sit with it carefully, because I do not want to over-theologize what was a simple, honest exchange between brothers — is that the Lord has been conducting something through these four months that neither Samuel nor I fully named in the moment. Samuel noticed the anniversary almost by accident. He was not looking for a milestone. He was looking at old photographs, grieving a woman, and stumbled sideways into the realization that something had held. That quality of accidental discovery is itself significant. The Spirit often reveals His work in retrospect, not in proclamation. He does not always announce what He is building while He is building it. There is a pattern throughout Scripture of the Lord establishing faithfulness in obscurity before bringing it into light. Joseph did not know in the pit that he was being prepared for the palace. The years in Potiphar's house and then in prison were not interruptions to the plan — they were the plan. The consistency of character that made Joseph trustworthy in Pharaoh's court was forged in the years when no one was watching and no promotion was coming. When Samuel said, "The panic attacks are gone, the pain's bigger, but we've never been so consistent" — he was, without knowing it, describing exactly that kind of hidden formation. The pain did not leave. The grief did not resolve. But something in the interior architecture of the man was being restructured, and the instrument of that restructuring was the Word of God, taken up daily, in the dark, before work. The writer of Hebrews — and I note with affection that Samuel and I had already planned to study Hebrews together, and that one of the musical albums I sent him moves through the entire book — speaks of the Word of God as "living and powerful, and sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing even to the division of soul and spirit, and of joints and marrow, and is a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart" (Hebrews 4:12, NKJV). The Greek word translated "living" here is ζάω (zaō, ZAH-oh) — to be alive, to have vitality, to function with the force of life. The Word is not a static document that produces consistent results through repetition alone. It is alive. It acts. It divides and discerns and heals in ways that no human discipline can manufacture. What Samuel experienced — the gradual dissolution of panic while grief remained — is not the product of a morning routine. It is the surgery of a living Word applied by a living Spirit to a living soul. And yet I do not want to skip past the grief too quickly, because the Spirit is not asking us to. Samuel is still waking up and checking for a message that is not coming. He is still scrolling through photographs of someone he loves. He is still in pain. And I think the Spirit is doing something deliberate in not removing that pain entirely — at least not yet. The Psalms are full of this tension. Psalm 13, which Samuel himself encountered through the music I had previously sent him — "Rejoice in Salvation, Psalm Thirteen" — opens with one of the most raw lament sequences in all of Scripture: "How long, O Lord? Will You forget me forever? How long will You hide Your face from me?" (Psalm 13:1, NKJV). But the psalm does not end there. It ends with trust: "But I have trusted in Your mercy; my heart shall rejoice in Your salvation. I will sing to the Lord, because He has dealt bountifully with me" (Psalm 13:5–6, NKJV). That arc — from raw lament to declared trust — is not a compression of grief. It is grief held within covenant. And that is precisely the shape of Samuel's four months. What arrested me about my own moment in this call — the Spirit above me, redirecting me from striving to Scripture — is the way it connects to what Jesus said about the Helper He would send. "But the Helper, the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in My name, He will teach you all things, and bring to your remembrance all things that I said to you" (John 14:26, NKJV). The Greek word translated "Helper" is παράκλητος (paraklētos, pah-RAH-klay-tos) — one called alongside to assist, to advocate, to counsel. This is not a Spirit who stands at a distance and observes. This is a Spirit who comes near, who hovers — and yes, I used that word deliberately, because it echoes the רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) of Genesis 1:2 hovering over the face of the deep before creation was spoken into order — and who redirects. The Spirit who hovered over formless chaos at the beginning of creation is the same Spirit who hovered over my impulse to optimize my podcast metrics and said, simply: *Read your Bible.* That is the Creator still working. That is the same movement, the same brooding, the same redemptive orientation toward order out of chaos. I find something deeply significant in the fact that the Spirit's redirection in my life was not dramatic. It was not a vision or an audible voice or a prophetic word delivered through another person. It was a quiet, internal check. A gentle stink eye, as I put it — and I mean that with full reverence, because that is exactly what it felt like. This is consistent with how the Spirit often operates in the season of formation. Elijah, exhausted and broken under the juniper tree, was not met with fire or earthquake or rushing wind in that moment. He was met with an angel who touched him and said, "Arise and eat" (1 Kings 19:5, NKJV). The provision was quiet, immediate, and practical. And then Elijah slept again, and the angel came a second time: "Arise and eat, because the journey is too great for you" (1 Kings 19:7, NKJV). The Spirit knows the length of the journey. He provisions accordingly. He does not always explain. He simply redirects. Samuel's observation about consistency — that we have been more consistent in this than in business — is not a small thing. It is a testimony about what happens when something is rooted in the Spirit rather than in the will. Every business endeavor I have undertaken has been driven by my own energy, my own strategy, my own timing, my own ambition — some of it redeemed, some of it merely religious-looking striving. But the reading, the early morning phone calls, the Scripture, the shared playlists, the prayer — those were never driven by a strategy. They began in desperation, in panic, in Samuel's Tennessee confession at five in the morning, and they have simply continued because the Spirit has continued to sustain them. What sustains what the Spirit initiates is not our discipline alone. It is His faithfulness feeding ours. "He who calls you is faithful, who also will do it" (1 Thessalonians 5:24, NKJV). The music Samuel and I were sharing — Gordon Lightfoot, the Psalm 13 worship, the Hebrews album, the grief playlists and the faith playlists — speaks to something I believe the Spirit endorses in the season of formation: the sanctification of lament. There is a Western Christian habit of rushing past grief into praise, as though the Psalms of lament are embarrassing older relatives who should not be allowed to speak at the dinner table. But the canon does not share that embarrassment. Roughly a third of the Psalms are lament psalms. The book of Lamentations exists. Job is canonical. The Psalms of Ascent — that pilgrimage collection — are not all triumphant. Some of them are barely holding on. "Out of the depths I have cried to You, O Lord" (Psalm 130:1, NKJV). The Hebrew word translated "depths" is מַעֲמַקִּים (ma'amaqqim, mah-ah-mak-KEEM) — the deep places, the subterranean, the place where the light does not reach. The psalmist does not pretend he is not in the depths. He cries from there. And the Lord hears from there. What Samuel was doing at five in the morning — scrolling through grief and stumbling onto faithfulness — is a form of that cry. He did not wake up to deliver a testimony. He woke up in pain, opened his camera roll, and the Spirit showed him four months. That is how the Lord often works: not by removing us from the depths, but by illuminating within the depths the evidence of His faithfulness that we could not see because we were too close to it. The panic is gone not because Samuel has mastered his emotions but because the Word has been doing what the Word does — it has been alive and active, dividing and healing, restructuring the interior man in ways that neither of us can fully account for or take credit for. I want to note, too, the geography of this conversation: Samuel in England, I somewhere else, five in the morning there, two in the morning on my end of the clock, four months after Samuel's Tennessee. The physical distance between us has never interrupted what the Spirit was doing through us together. This is the nature of the Body of Christ functioning as the Spirit intends — not contained by geography, not dependent on proximity, not reducible to a local church program. Two men, broken in similar ways, given to each other by the Lord, meeting in the Word and in prayer across an ocean. That is not a podcast strategy. That is not a networking outcome. That is the Spirit of God weaving threads that human hands did not arrange. And finally, I am struck by the simplicity of what Samuel said at the end: "I'll get sad, and then I'll listen to the faith ones, and then I'll bring myself back up." There is a kind of spiritual wisdom in that rhythm that I do not want to spiritualize into something more sophisticated than it is. Feel it. Then bring it to the Lord. Let the grief be real, and then let the faith speak into the grief. That is not emotional manipulation. That is the pattern of the Psalms. That is lament moving toward trust. That is exactly the arc of Psalm 13: cry out honestly, declare trust anyway. Samuel named it instinctively, without theological vocabulary, because the Spirit had already been teaching him through four months of early mornings and shared Scripture what the canon has always said: you are allowed to feel it all, and you are not allowed to stay there alone. --- ## 🤔 Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation presses against is the modern Christian tendency to measure spiritual maturity by spiritual experience rather than by quiet faithfulness over time. Samuel's testimony would not make a compelling conference illustration. There was no dramatic deliverance, no sudden breakthrough, no mountaintop moment to report. There was only four months of showing up to the Word, together, in the dark. The evangelical machine is not well-designed to honor that kind of testimony because it cannot be packaged, sold, or scaled. But the Scripture honors it. "Let us not grow weary while doing good, for in due season we shall reap if we do not lose heart" (Galatians 6:9, NKJV). The due season is real. The reaping is promised. But it comes to those who do not give up in the between-time, and the between-time looks nothing like a highlight reel. The second challenge is to the habit of grief-suppression in Christian community. Samuel is still hurting. He is still waking up and checking for a message from someone he loves who is not messaging him. And the Spirit did not rebuke him for that. The Spirit met him there and showed him faithfulness. The church must learn again to create space for the people in the depths — the מַעֲמַקִּים (mah-ah-mak-KEEM) — without rushing them toward premature resolution. Grief held in the presence of God is not a failure of faith. It is faith functioning in one of its most honest forms. The third challenge is to the striving spirit that I named in my own life — the impulse to optimize, to build, to achieve, to measure. I described it honestly: I was about to sign up for Google Analytics for my podcast website, and the Spirit redirected me to the Word. That is not a criticism of podcast websites or analytics. It is a confrontation with the idolatry of productivity that masquerades as Kingdom building. The παράκλητος (pah-RAH-klay-tos) does not redirect us away from good work forever; He redirects us to the foundation that makes good work fruitful. First things must remain first. When the Word is the foundation, everything built upon it has a different quality than what is built on strategy alone. The fourth challenge is to spiritual isolation. Both Samuel and I are in seasons that could easily justify withdrawal — from community, from vulnerability, from one another. Grief is a powerful argument for isolation. But the Spirit has kept us in contact, in accountability, in shared reading. The Hebrew concept of עֵדָה (edah, ay-DAH) — the assembly, the congregation, the company of the faithful — is not optional. Even in pain. Even at five in the morning across an ocean. The Body needs its members, and its members need each other in the darkness as much as in the light. A fifth and gentler challenge: the music we share matters. The songs Samuel and I have been exchanging — psalms, laments, worship rooted in Scripture, the entire book of Hebrews set to bluegrass — are not mere entertainment choices. What we fill our ears with in the season of grief either deepens the wound or tends it. Gordon Lightfoot's poetry is honest and beautiful, and there is a place for honest beauty in grief. But the faith playlists matter too — the Psalm 13 worship, the Hebrews album, the songs that carry the Word into the places where the mind is too tired to form sentences. "Speaking to one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing and making melody in your heart to the Lord" (Ephesians 5:19, NKJV) is not a Sunday morning instruction alone. It is a daily formation practice, and Samuel and I stumbled into it without naming it as such. --- ## 🚀 What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity is not the absence of grief or struggle. It is the accumulation of faithfulness in the presence of both. What Samuel named — four months of consistency, panic gone, pain larger but Word constant — is one of the most honest portraits of genuine spiritual growth I have encountered. It means we must stop measuring our maturity by how we feel and begin measuring it by how we have continued. The ζάω (ZAH-oh) Word does its work regardless of our emotional state. We show up. He operates. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Kingdom obedience in this season looks like taking the next early morning call. It looks like sending the playlist. It looks like putting down the analytics tool and picking up the Book. It looks like two brothers, separated by an ocean and sustained by the Spirit, choosing the Word over the world's prescription for healing. It is not glamorous. It is not viral. It is not a testimony that fills auditoriums. But it is the stuff of which genuine disciples are made, and the King is pleased with it. **For Prophetic Posture:** The saints who will endure the days ahead are being formed right now in obscurity — at five in the morning, in grief, in England, on the phone, in the Word. The prophetic posture this meditation calls us to is not one of loud proclamation but of quiet, sustained abiding. "Abide in Me, and I in you" (John 15:4, NKJV). The ruach (ROO-akh) hovers over those who wait. The Word lives in those who receive it. The testimony that will matter most in the days of shaking will not be the one delivered from a platform — it will be the one that was forged in four months of showing up when showing up was the hardest thing to do. --- ## 🔥 A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who has been the recipient of four months of Your faithfulness that I did not deserve and could not manufacture. I thank You that You are the God who does not leave us in the pit, and I thank You that You are also the God who does not rush us out of it before the work is finished. I ask that You continue to meet my brother Samuel in his early mornings, in his grief, in the photographs and the silence and the absence he is learning to carry — and that You would let him feel, in increasing measure, that You are the One who is actually present when everything else is gone. I ask that the ζάω (ZAH-oh) Word continue to do its surgery in both of us — not gently when it must be sharp, not harshly when it must be tender — and that the παράκλητος (pah-RAH-klay-tos) would continue to redirect us from our own striving back to the foundation that does not shift. I receive this season as preparation, not punishment. I receive the consistency as Your gift, not our achievement. I agree with Your will, Lord — that what You began in desperation in Tennessee would be completed in fullness in its proper time, in both of our lives, to the glory of Your Name and the benefit of all who will come behind us to read what You have done. Let the testimony of the middle season be as powerful as the testimony of the breakthrough. Let the saints hear that You are faithful in the dark. And let that be enough. In the Name above every name, Amen. --- ## 📝 Scripture References & Original Language Notes | Passage (NKJV) | Original Language | Theological Insight | | --- | --- | --- | | Hebrews 4:12 — "the word of God is living and powerful" | ζάω (zaō, ZAH-oh) — to be alive, to have vitality | The Word is not static text; it is an active, living agent that surgically divides soul from spirit and produces formation that no human discipline alone can achieve | | Genesis 1:2 — "the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters" | רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — Spirit, breath, wind | The same Spirit who brought order from formless chaos at creation continues to hover over the chaos of human grief and loss, bringing formation in obscurity | | John 14:26 — "the Helper, the Holy Spirit" | παράκλητος (paraklētos, pah-RAH-klay-tos) — one called alongside to assist, advocate, counsel | The Spirit's role is not passive observation but active redirection — coming alongside to counsel and bring to remembrance the Word of Christ | | Psalm 13:1 — "How long, O Lord?
