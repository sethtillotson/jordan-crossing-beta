# 05-07 Call Reflection: Covenant Manhood, Celibacy, and Becoming Worthy of What You Pray For **Title:** 05-07 at 21:09 — The Kind of Man She Deserves **Recorded:** 2026-05-07 21:09:02 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture / Phone Call) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother walk through the corridors of military selection, marital dissolution, sobriety, celibacy, and covenant manhood — all in a single phone call — something in me recognized that the Holy Spirit had been quietly threading a single needle through every scattered subject. What began as good news about a military callback ended as a meditation on what it means to become the man worthy of the woman you are asking God to provide. The Lord has a way of doing that — taking what looks like a casual conversation between brothers and turning it into a kairos moment neither of us saw coming. **Why This Matters:** The crisis of covenant manhood is not merely a cultural problem — it is a discipleship problem, a liturgical problem, a problem of formation. When a man begins to ask himself whether he can genuinely pursue what he is praying for, whether he is becoming who that prayer requires, something has shifted at the level of the spirit. That shift matters for the entire Body of Christ, not merely for one man navigating divorce and military selection. The Church desperately needs men who are asking the harder question — not “Will God give me what I want?” but “Am I becoming what that gift requires?” The conversation I shared with my brother landed there, unexpectedly and tenderly, and I am not willing to let it pass without pressing into it. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind listening to this conversation would catalog it as what it appears to be on the surface: two friends catching up, one sharing exciting career news, the other offering companionship and occasional wisdom. The worldly interpreter might note the emotional volatility of a man fresh from divorce — the sobriety experiment, the rejected rebound opportunity, the sudden spiritual seriousness — and attribute all of it to the predictable arc of post-relationship grief. Psychology would name the celibacy commitment as an overcorrection, a temporary idealism born of guilt and loneliness. Sociology would call the reflection on marriage a product of disillusionment. And the military thread would be read as displaced ambition — a man redirecting pain into purpose through institutional structure. All of this interpretation is compassionate in its own way, and some of it even contains partial truth. But it cannot see what I was watching unfold in real time. **The Limitation:** The natural eye is capable of cataloging behavior and drawing reasonable inferences from visible cause and effect. What it cannot do is perceive the movement of grace beneath the surface of ordinary conversation. It cannot see a man’s moral conscience quietly recalibrating toward covenant — not because he has been lectured into it, but because something alive in him is responding to the weight of what he is genuinely asking God for. The flesh is fluent in outcomes but mute on origins. It has no language for the moment when a man stops performing repentance and begins inhabiting it. What I heard in my brother was not the psychology of grief managing itself into stability. It was something far older — the beginning of a man letting the image of who he wants to become reorder who he is willing to be today. That movement is not natural. It is initiated from above. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with this conversation now, what arrests me most is not the military news, as genuinely exciting as that is. What arrests me is the quiet moment near the end when my brother said, essentially: *If I am asking God to put this kind of woman in my life, then I need to be the kind of man who deserves her.* I have heard versions of that statement many times in Christian circles, often repeated as a slogan. But the way it landed in his mouth, in the middle of an unscripted phone call, after a weeks-long sobriety experiment and the decision to walk away from a woman he easily could have used — that was not a slogan. That was formation. That was the Spirit doing in secret what the sermon tries to do in public. The Scripture that immediately rises in me is from Proverbs 31 — not the famous catalogue of the virtuous woman’s qualities, but the lesser-noticed literary fact that the passage was originally given as a teaching to a *king*. Lemuel’s mother did not speak this to women searching for an identity. She spoke it to a man learning what kind of woman to recognize, honor, and be worthy of. The implication is staggering: the virtuous woman is not simply discovered; she is *recognized* — and she is only recognizable to the man whose own character has been trained to see what she is. A man who has not been formed in faithfulness will walk right past her, or worse, squander her. This connects directly to what my brother articulated without knowing he was exegeting Proverbs 31. He said that the woman he is praying for has remained celibate, has remained honest, has remained faithful to Christ — and that any woman like that deserves a husband who is also those things. The Greek New Testament has a word for this kind of worthiness: ἄξιος (axios, AX-ee-os), meaning to have sufficient weight, to balance the scale on the other side. Paul uses it in Ephesians 4:1 — *“Walk worthy of the calling with which you were called”* — and the entire moral structure of that letter is built on the premise that position and conduct must come into alignment. You cannot occupy a calling you have not grown into. My brother is beginning to grasp, on the level of marriage covenant, what Paul was pressing into on the level of ecclesial identity. What strikes me further is the way the rejected rebound decision connects to this axis of ἄξιος (AX-ee-os). He said: *It doesn’t sit right with me. It’s not even about Emily. It’s about the woman I’d be using.* That instinct — toward the protection of someone else’s dignity rather than the satisfaction of his own appetite — is not merely ethical reasoning. It is the beginning of what Scripture calls self-denial operating at the level of the body. Jesus said in Matthew 16:24, *“If anyone desires to come after Me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow Me.”* The cross is not primarily a symbol of suffering. It is the instrument of the death of the self that would grasp, use, and discard. My brother picked up something that day — he may not have named it as a cross, but that is what it was. I am also sitting with the dream he described — the dream about contacting his drug supplier, the conversation inside the dream affirming the sobriety, and then the waking disappointment that the substance he had dreamed of ordering was not actually there. And the strange gift in that: I said to him, *pay attention to the message of the dream.* Even the plug in the dream was saying it was probably good to take a break. There is a biblical precedent for the Lord speaking through dreams to men in transition — to Joseph in Egypt, to Solomon at Gibeon, to the Magi warned not to return to Herod. The Lord is not limited by the content of the dream; He is sovereign over its architecture. I am not projecting prophecy onto this, but I will not dismiss the possibility that the Spirit was using the imagery of his own craving to communicate a gentle, non-condemning word of confirmation: *you are doing the right thing; stay the course.* The thread about the cross emerged from a very dark place in the conversation — when my brother asked about men who have witnessed the most extreme violences of war perpetrated against their families. It is one of those questions the human soul is right to struggle with, because it names something so catastrophically wrong that the mind reels. And I found myself reaching, instinctively, for the cross — not as a quick answer but as the only thing that is actually large enough to hold both the victim and the perpetrator in the same frame of love. Jesus on the cross in Luke 23:34 did not merely forgive His killers theoretically. He interceded for them in real time, in the middle of the act that killed Him: *“Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do.”* The Greek here is significant — the verb ἄφες (aphes, AH-fes), from ἀφίημι (aphiēmi, ah-FEE-ay-mee), means to release, to send away, to let go of the claim. Jesus was not minimizing what was being done to Him. He was releasing the debt in real time, from the place of maximum suffering. My brother’s response to that moment was not theological — it was visceral. He said: *“So we should probably say what Jesus said on the cross when something like that happens — Lord, forgive them.”* That is not the conclusion of a man being pious. That is a man letting the Cross become the only answer his conscience will accept in the face of the worst thing he can imagine. And that matters because the military life he is now considering will expose him to those kinds of decisions — not as thought experiments but as lived realities. The formation happening in him now, through sobriety, through rejected temptation, through covenant recalibration, is the formation that will hold him when he is on deployment and the easy answers are not available. There is also something quietly powerful in the observation he made about modern marriage: that many men complain about their wives but do not give their wives the opportunity to be good wives, because they themselves are not good husbands. He said that women, subconsciously, will follow in the footsteps of the man. This is not simply a sociological observation. It resonates with the Pauline theology of headship in Ephesians 5 — not the distorted, domineering version that culture has reacted against, but the sacrificial, servant-leading version Paul actually describes. The husband who loves as Christ loves the Church is not managing a subordinate; he is laying down his life for her flourishing. The passage says in verse 28, *“He who loves his wife loves himself.”* The unity is so complete that the husband’s spiritual health literally shapes the wife’s capacity to thrive. My brother has arrived at this insight through the school of loss rather than through exegesis, and sometimes that school teaches what no commentary can. I notice also the moment when my brother said that this is the first thing in a long time that he feels *God wants him to do* — not just something he himself wants. And he was honest that it carried both excitement and dread. *“Oh gosh, okay. Brilliant thing… but oh shit.”* That combination — the sense of divine calling coupled with a holy sobriety about what it will cost — is remarkably consistent with the pattern of biblical call narratives. Moses at the burning bush was not thrilled. Gideon was threshing wheat in a winepress to hide from the enemy when the angel called him a mighty man of valor. Jeremiah said he was too young. Jonah ran. The ones who leap at calling with pure enthusiasm and no trembling often have not yet understood what they are being called into. The ones who say, *“I feel like God wants this, and that’s exactly what makes me nervous”* — those are often the ones who have begun to grasp the weight of the thing. What binds all of this together — the military calling, the celibacy commitment, the cross theology, the rejected rebound, the sobriety, the marriage reflections — is a single spiritual movement: a man who is beginning to let what he wants to *receive* from God reshape what he is willing to *give* to God. That movement is the narrow path. It is not dramatic in the moment. It happens in phone calls, in dreams, in turned-down temptations, in honest admissions to a brother. But it is real. And the Spirit honors it. ## Implications and Challenges There is a version of modern Christian culture that wants the promise without the formation, the covenant partner without the covenant character. The prosperity framework has infected even relational theology — if I pray for it with enough faith, it will materialize, regardless of whether I have been shaped to carry it. But the Scriptures consistently reveal that God’s gifts are given to people being formed to steward them, not merely to people who want them badly enough. My brother’s instinct to say, *“If I am asking God for this woman, I need to become the man who deserves her,”* is more theologically sound than a great deal of what passes for relational teaching in Western churches. The challenge for all of us is to take that instinct seriously and let it restructure our actual behavior, not merely our aspirational language. The tendency to use other people’s pain as a vehicle for managing our own is one of the most normalized sins in the modern West. It is normalized in dating culture, in therapeutic frameworks that prioritize self-care above other-care, and in a consumer spirituality that has absorbed the language of healing while retaining the posture of self-service. My brother named something honestly when he said that going to the woman he could have used would have made him *just as bad* as what had hurt him. That kind of moral honesty — turning the mirror on oneself rather than escaping into the comparative comfort of relative virtue — is rare and it is holy. The theology of the cross cannot be reserved for Sunday sermons and left at the door when the atrocities of history and current geopolitics are brought up. My brother’s question about wartime violence was not an abstract theological probe. It was the question of a man who may soon find himself in the presence of exactly that kind of suffering. The answer I gave him — that Jesus loves both the victim and the perpetrator, and that the cross is large enough to hold both — is not sentimentalism. It is the most radical claim of the Christian faith, and it either shapes us for the hardest moments of human existence or it means nothing at all. The male tendency to frame spiritual growth as intellectual achievement rather than behavioral transformation is another challenge the conversation quietly surfaces. My brother did not arrive at his celibacy commitment through a theological treatise on sexual ethics. He arrived there by sitting with the question: *Would I actually wait?* That kind of internal honesty — the willingness to hold one’s own stated values against one’s actual willingness — is more generative of real formation than a hundred doctrinal position papers. The Church would do well to cultivate more of that kind of ruthless, compassionate self-examination in its men. Finally, there is the challenge of calling and fear coexisting faithfully. Western Christian culture tends to celebrate fearless confidence as the mark of genuine faith, while treating anxiety about a calling as a sign of weak faith. But the biblical pattern is consistently more honest than that. The trembling that accompanies true calling is often the sign that a man has actually understood what he is being asked to do. The dread my brother described is not unbelief. It may be the beginning of the kind of reverence that will hold him steady when the thing gets hard. We need to stop telling men to suppress that trembling and start teaching them to walk *through* it as part of the call itself. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Genuine maturity is not the absence of desire — it is the willingness to let desire be disciplined by covenant. My brother’s turn toward celibacy, his rejection of the rebound, his honest self-examination about whether he would actually live what he prays for — these are not signs of spiritual perfectionism. They are signs of a man learning that who he is becoming matters more than what he is acquiring. That is the beginning of wisdom in the biblical sense — the alignment of inner formation with outward covenant posture. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The call before each of us is not merely to pray for godly relationships, fruitful deployments, or covenant communities. The call is to become, through daily small choices — turned-down temptations, honest dreams, difficult phone calls — the kind of person the Kingdom can actually use. Obedience is rarely one dramatic decision; it is the accumulation of smaller decisions that nobody sees except God and the occasional brother on the other end of a late-night phone call. **For Prophetic Posture:** We are in a season where the Lord is forming men for things that are harder than they appear. The right posture is not bravado and it is not passivity. It is the combination my brother described: genuine excitement about what God seems to be initiating, held alongside honest sobriety about what it will cost. That is watchfulness. That is the lamp kept trimmed and burning. That is the servant found faithful when the Master returns, not because he was performing readiness, but because readiness had become his actual condition. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as a co-heir of this Kingdom, standing between the heavens and the earth in the Name that is above every name, in full agreement with Your will — which You do not hide from Your servants the prophets. I thank You for the unexpected altar of a phone call, for the way You thread covenant truth through laughter and military selection news and honest confessions of temptation resisted. I pray now for my brother — that the formation You have begun in him through sobriety, through rejection of self-serving behavior, through the trembling recognition of his own calling — would not be stolen by impatience or loneliness or the enemy’s counteroffers. Strengthen him for the selection ahead, both the military one and the deeper one You are running him through in the chambers of his own soul. Raise him up as a man of ἄξιος (AX-ee-os) weight — a man whose inner life balances the scale of everything he is asking You for. And let this word go out now as bread on the waters, feeding every man in the Body who is asking the same question my brother asked: *Am I becoming the man worthy of what I am praying for?* Let the answer not be condemnation but invitation — the same invitation Your Son gave when He said, *“Follow Me.”* We agree with Your will. We receive Your formation. We yield to Your cross. To the Lamb who was slain and lives forever — all glory, all honor, all dominion. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Ephesians 4:1 — “Walk worthy of the calling with which you were called” | ἄξιος (axios, AX-ee-os) — of sufficient weight, deserving, to balance the scale | Paul’s command ties identity to conduct; the calling cannot be occupied by a character not yet formed to carry it | | Luke 23:34 — “Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do” | ἄφες (aphes, AH-fes) from ἀφίημι (aphiēmi, ah-FEE-ay-mee) — to release, send away, relinquish the claim | Jesus releases the debt in real time, at the point of maximum suffering; this is not theological abstraction but priestly intercession from the altar of the cross | | Matthew 16:24 — “Let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow Me” | ἀπαρνησάσθω (aparnēsasthō, ah-par-NAY-sas-thoh) — let him utterly deny, disown | The aorist imperative indicates a decisive, complete act of self-disownment as the precondition of discipleship | | Ephesians 5:28 — “He who loves his wife loves himself” | ἀγαπάτω (agapatō, ah-gah-PAH-toh) — let him love with covenantal, self-giving love | The husband’s love for his wife is not sentiment but self-extension; her flourishing is inseparable from his own | | Proverbs 31:10 — “Who can find a virtuous wife?
