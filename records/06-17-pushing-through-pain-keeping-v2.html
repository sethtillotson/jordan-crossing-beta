# 06-17 Call Reflection: Samuel - Faith, Pain, and Kingdom Perseverance **Title:** 06-17 at 15:13 — Pushing Through the Pain, Keeping the Faith **Recorded:** 2026-06-17 15:13:43 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture — Phone/Video Call) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened back to this call with my brother Samuel, what struck me most was not the business updates or the practical problem-solving — it was the quiet, persistent current of faith running beneath all of it. Here is a man in genuine physical pain, navigating real obstacles, and still saying, *“Everything will be well. I have faith.”* That is not a small thing. That is the substance of things hoped for, pressing through cracked teeth and rejected ad campaigns and exhausted nights. **Why This Matters:** In the Body of Christ, we are often tempted to imagine that genuine faith only speaks from mountaintop moments — clean, composed, fully articulate, and surrounded by spiritual aesthetics. But the call I had with my brother on 2026-06-17 tells a different story. Faith here sounded like a man with a broken tooth still showing up to work. It sounded like pressing forward when the platform flags your content, when your body hurts, when you’re wiped at the end of the day and you don’t know what to do with yourself. This matters because the saints are not being trained for ideal conditions. We are being trained to carry the Kingdom through the difficult, the inconvenient, the painful, and the ordinary — and to still say *it will be well* and mean it. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, this conversation would be categorized as two friends catching up — a mix of entrepreneurial strategy, tech talk, physical complaints, recreational habits, and the kind of casual camaraderie that keeps people sane through stressful seasons. The natural mind would look at Samuel’s TikTok ad rejection and see a compliance problem to solve. It would look at the broken tooth and see a dental bill. It would look at the weed conversation and see harm reduction logic. It would scan the whole exchange and conclude: *two guys grinding through life, supporting each other, no big deal.* And on one level, that reading isn’t wrong — it simply cannot see the deeper current. **The Limitation:** The natural mind is equipped to analyze circumstances but not to perceive the covenantal thread woven through them. It can identify resilience as a psychological trait without recognizing it as the fruit of trust in a faithful God. It can note that two men end a call with *“Grace and blessings, God bless”* and file it under social habit rather than hearing it as a genuine priestly benediction exchanged between brothers. The veil over natural perception is not a matter of intelligence — it is a matter of orientation. When the inner man has not been renewed by the Spirit, the ordinary remains merely ordinary, and the sacred remains invisible. But those whose eyes have been opened begin to see that the texture of daily life — pain, persistence, laughter, honesty, encouragement — can all be held within the hands of a God who is present in the grinding, not only in the glorious. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with this conversation, the first thing that presses on me is the image of a man pushing through physical pain to do what he has been called to do. Samuel is speaking through discomfort — literally, his mouth hurts, his tongue is cutting against a broken tooth — and still he is showing up, still processing strategy, still receiving encouragement, still holding hope. I cannot read that and not think of the Apostle Paul writing in 2 Corinthians 12:9 — *“My grace is sufficient for you, for My strength is made perfect in weakness.”* The Lord did not remove Paul’s thorn. He reframed it. And I am seeing something of that reframing in this exchange: the weakness is present, the pain is real, but the mission does not stop. There is something worth naming in the moment where I suggested to Samuel that the broken tooth itself could become part of his story — *“My tooth just fell out, but I don’t care because I like money. If you like money, DM me.”* I said it with humor, but underneath the joke is a genuine spiritual principle: authenticity in weakness disarms defenses and opens doors. Paul understood this. He boasted in his infirmities precisely because the power of Christ could be seen more clearly through them. The world is not moved by polished perfection — it is moved by a man who is visibly broken and still standing, still believing, still pressing. The business obstacle — the TikTok ad being flagged for alleged financial misrepresentation — is also worth pressing into. Samuel built something worth promoting. The platform’s algorithm doesn’t know the difference between deception and a legitimate high-ticket offer. This is the friction the Kingdom worker faces in Babylon’s marketplace: the tools are not neutral, the gates are not always open, and the rules are written by those who serve different masters. But I notice what the Spirit pressed me to say in that moment: *“Maybe you’re just overcomplicating it. Maybe you just got to go on there and be like, Yo, what up TikTokers?”* There is a wisdom here that is older than marketing strategy. It is the wisdom of directness — of simply speaking to people where they are, without elaborate machinery, and letting the message carry its own weight. This connects to something Jesus modeled throughout His earthly ministry. He did not always work through established channels. He bypassed religious infrastructure, sat with people in their ordinary contexts, and spoke in language that met them where they were. *“The kingdom of heaven is like…”* — over and over again, He took what people already understood and used it as a door. Samuel’s most powerful marketing tool may not be a perfectly crafted claude-sonnet-4.6-generated ad. It may be his face, his voice, his honesty, and a direct invitation. I also find myself sitting with the brief but weighty exchange about prayer and specificity. I said to Samuel: *“That goes back to what I always told you — be careful what you pray for and be very specific when you do.”* And he responded without hesitation: *“Damn straight.”* That exchange is short, but it carries real weight. The saints have historically understood that prayer is not merely wish-making — it is covenantal conversation with a God who takes words seriously. Ecclesiastes 5:2 warns, *“Do not be rash with your mouth, and let not your heart utter anything hastily before God.”* James 4:3 reminds us that we ask and do not receive because we ask wrongly. Precision in prayer is not a lack of faith — it is an expression of mature faith that understands who it is addressing. My own story surfaced in this call in a way I did not anticipate. When Samuel was lamenting his tooth pain, I found myself sharing that I had once smoked meth — and that the dental destruction it caused was just part of the daily reality of that season. I did not say it as confession, and I did not say it to dramatize. I said it to let Samuel know that pain in the mouth while trying to work is something I have lived through, and he is not alone in that. There is a pastoral dimension to testimony that often goes unrecognized. Paul wrote in 2 Corinthians 1:4 that the God of all comfort *“comforts us in all our tribulation, that we may be able to comfort those who are in any trouble, with the comfort with which we ourselves are comforted by God.”* My history — including the ugly parts — has not been wasted. It has been repurposed as material for genuine connection with people in pain. The conversation also touched on fatherhood and the baby coming — and I notice that my posture in that exchange was one of genuine moral reasoning, not legalism. I said that certain habits would need to stop because of the baby. Samuel affirmed it as healthy thinking. What I find spiritually significant is that the coming child is already reshaping my priorities, already functioning as a refining presence before he or she has drawn a first breath. This is covenant thinking. The next generation exerts a pull on the present generation. Abraham was called to walk blamelessly not merely for his own sake — the promise ran through his children and his children’s children. The child changes things. I want to sit with the closing of the call for a moment. I mentioned to Samuel that I was going to send him a meditation on our last phone call — and that he should send it to his mother too, because she had been present for that conversation. There is something quietly significant in that instruction. The word that comes through one conversation is meant to travel further than the room it was spoken in. That is the nature of prophetic witness — it does not terminate at the first hearer. It moves, it spreads, it plants. Whether Samuel’s mother reads the meditation or not, I said, *“Doesn’t matter to me, but just send it anyway.”* That is faithfulness without attachment to outcome, which is exactly the posture the Spirit requires of those who carry a word. And then the call ends: *“Grace and blessings. See you brother. God bless. Bye.”* It is brief. It is casual in tone. But it is a benediction. The Hebrew concept of שָׁלוֹם (shalom, shah-LOME) — wholeness, completeness, peace as the active flourishing of a life rightly ordered — lives inside a goodbye like that, even when neither party is thinking about it formally. We do not always know when we are pronouncing blessing. But the saints who have made blessing the atmosphere of their speech find that it happens naturally, even in ordinary farewells. What I carry away from this conversation is not a single doctrinal thesis but a living picture of what faith looks like in the week-to-week. It looks like a man with a broken tooth still building his business. It looks like an appeal filed with TikTok while trusting that something else will work if that doesn’t. It looks like laughter about hard things, honesty about ugly seasons, and the quiet conviction — stated simply and without fanfare — that *everything will be well*. That phrase, spoken by Samuel near the end of the call, carries in it the echo of Julian of Norwich, who wrote in the fourteenth century what the Spirit had pressed into her: *“All shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well.”* She did not say it from a place of ease. She said it from a place of revelation — from having seen, in the Spirit, that the love of God is the ground beneath all ground, and it does not give way. I believe Samuel said it from a similar place, even if he would not frame it in those terms. Faith sometimes knows more than it can articulate. And I am grateful to be a witness to that kind of faith — rough around the edges, honest about the pain, unashamed of the struggle, and still rooted in something that does not move. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation presents is to the idea that a spiritual conversation must sound explicitly theological to be spiritually significant. Much of this call was casual, practical, and punctuated with humor and mild profanity. The temptation for the religious mind is to dismiss such exchanges as spiritually thin. But the Spirit is not confined to formal devotional register. He moves in the honest fabric of real life — and those who can only recognize Him when the language is polished will miss a great deal of what He is doing. Second, this call invites a serious reflection on what we do with our personal history. I did not hide my past in this conversation. I used it — not for drama, not for self-flagellation, but as a bridge to genuine empathy. The modern Western church has often treated testimony as something that belongs behind a pulpit on a special Sunday, sanitized and packaged for maximum impact. But testimony also belongs in a casual phone call, offered as simple solidarity to a brother who is hurting. The willingness to be known — truly known, including the before — is part of what makes the grace of God visible in us. Third, this conversation raises the question of how we carry ambition in the Kingdom. Samuel is building something. He is excited about 200,000-view minimums and high-ticket sales and crypto wallets. This is real entrepreneurial momentum, and it sits alongside genuine faith and real physical pain. The Kingdom does not require its servants to be indifferent to flourishing. But it does require that ambition be held loosely — pursued with diligence, surrendered without grief, and never treated as the source of identity or security. Fourth, the exchange about prayer specificity is worth pressing on more broadly. There is a broadly practiced habit in modern Christianity of praying in vague generalities — bless me, bless them, fix this, fix that — without the kind of precise, covenantal engagement that Scripture models. Nehemiah’s prayers were specific. Daniel’s were specific. The Psalms are full of precise emotional and circumstantial language laid before God. Vagueness in prayer is often a form of unbelief — a way of hedging so that we are not held accountable to what we actually asked for. This call reminded me that specificity is not a restriction on faith. It is an expression of it. Fifth, and finally, this meditation carries an implicit word for those who are building in the Kingdom during seasons of pain or transition: do not wait until the conditions are ideal to begin. Samuel could have said, *“I’ll focus on the business once my tooth is fixed.”* He didn’t. I could have said, *“I’ll start sending out meditations once my life is more settled.”* I haven’t. The Kingdom is built by people who show up broken and keep going. That is not stoicism. That is the faith that endures to the end. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity does not look like the absence of pain or the elimination of messy circumstances. It looks like the capacity to hold faith and hardship in the same hand without letting one cancel the other. Growing in maturity means learning to hear the voice of the Spirit not only in cathedral silence but in the middle of a phone call, a business problem, a physical ache, and a casual conversation with a brother you care about. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this meditation calls forth is simple and immediate: show up. Do the thing you have been assigned to do, even when your body hurts, even when the platform rejects your content, even when you are exhausted at the end of the day. Obedience in the small and the ordinary is the training ground for the large and the significant. And when someone in your circle is pushing through pain, speak life into them — specifically, honestly, and with enough humor to make the medicine go down. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this call calls me to is one of attentive companionship — remaining present to the people the Lord has placed around me, watching for the movements of His grace in their ordinary struggle, and being willing to name what I see. The word I am sending Samuel about our last call, the instruction to forward it to his mother — these are small prophetic acts. They are not dramatic. But they are the kind of faithful, attentive ministry that sustains the Body over time, multiplies revelation, and honors the God who wastes nothing. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who has been set apart — not by my own merit but by the blood of Your Son and the seal of Your Spirit — and I stand in the gap for my brother Samuel and for all the builders, the wounded, the grinding, the faithful, and the exhausted who are pressing forward in this hour. You are not a God who waits for ideal conditions to move. You moved through a manger, through a cross, through an empty tomb, through broken vessels and improbable people, and You are moving now. I ask You to sustain my brother through his pain, to open the doors that need to be opened and close the ones that need to stay shut, and to let the word that has gone out between us take root in every heart it reaches — including the heart of his mother. I thank You that You are faithful when we are faithless, that Your purposes are not frustrated by broken teeth or rejected ads or scattered plans. You are the God who says *all shall be well*, and I agree with that word from the ground of covenant and not from the presumption of comfort. Let the faith in this exchange be multiplied. Let the grace in every farewell be real. And let the life of Your Son be visible in the ordinary, unremarkable, beautiful fabric of our days together. In Jesus’ name, and for the glory of His Kingdom — amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | 2 Corinthians 12:9 — “My strength is made perfect in weakness” | ἀσθένεια (astheneia, as-THEN-ee-ah) — weakness, infirmity | Paul’s thorn reframes weakness as a vessel for divine power rather than an obstacle to it | | 2 Corinthians 1:4 — “that we may be able to comfort those who are in any trouble” | παράκλησις (paraklēsis, pah-RAH-klay-sis) — comfort, encouragement, exhortation | The same root as Paraclete (the Holy Spirit); comfort received becomes comfort extended | | Ecclesiastes 5:2 — “Do not be rash with your mouth…before God” | אֱלֹהִים (Elohim, eh-loh-HEEM) — God in His fullness and majesty | The warning about careless speech before God implies that intentional, specific speech before Him is the right alternative | | James 4:3 — “You ask and do not receive, because you ask amiss” | αἰτέω (aiteō, eye-TEH-oh) — to ask, to request | The verb implies active, directed asking; the problem is not asking but the character of the asking | | שָׁלוֹם (shalom, shah-LOME) — wholeness/peace | Hebrew root שָׁלֵם (shalem) — to be complete, whole | More than the absence of conflict; the active, holistic flourishing of a life rightly ordered under God | ---
