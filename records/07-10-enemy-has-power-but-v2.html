# 07-10 Theological Reflection: Exousia vs. Dunamis, Spiritual Authority, and Covenant Fellowship **Title:** 07-10 at 17:31 — The Enemy Has Power but No Standing: Exousia, Dunamis, and the Liberation of the Committed Heart **Recorded:** 2026-07-10 17:31:09 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Theological Reflection ## Opening Observation As I sat with this conversation — a late-night fireside call stretched across continents, smoke and laughter and spicy Cheetos and Scripture all woven together — I found myself arrested not by its informality but by what the Spirit was quietly threading through it. My brother Samuel and I were simply talking the way brothers talk, and yet something genuinely theological kept breaking the surface, again and again, across themes that at first seemed unrelated but turned out to be facets of the same diamond. The distinction between two Greek words, a testimony of a former jihadist brought to Christ through a spare bedroom and a car key, and the question of what covenant fellowship is actually for — these are not separate conversations. They are one conversation about a single reality: the Kingdom of God is more precise, more personal, and more costly than we have allowed ourselves to receive. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ is simultaneously riddled with two opposite errors regarding spiritual warfare and with a domesticated version of fellowship that has drained both of their power. On one side, believers either dismiss the enemy as a toothless irrelevance or live in perpetual dread of him as though he were a second sovereign — and both errors produce the same fruit: defeated, confused saints who do not know why they still suffer after coming to faith and who cannot explain what Jesus actually meant when He said He had given us authority. On the other side, the word *fellowship* has been reduced to a social calendar — potlucks and handshakes and small groups that never go deep enough to bleed — while the apostolic design for covenant community, with all its costly hospitality and honest transparency and sustained pastoral follow-through, sits largely unactivated. What I heard rising through my conversation with my brother Samuel is that Scripture is far more precise than we have treated it on all of these fronts, and that precision is not a technicality. It is liberation. The Greek text is exact where our English translations are vague. The apostolic model is costly where our modern practice is comfortable. And a single evening of honest, Spirit-threaded conversation between two men who came out of darkness can carry more theological freight than a carefully polished sermon — if we have ears to hear what the Spirit is saying through it. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, this conversation looks like two old friends catching up across a long distance — swapping stories about cats and concerts and county fairs, complaining about the heat, laughing about old memories, and occasionally wandering into the territory of religion. The natural mind, hearing phrases like “spiritual warfare” and “legal standing in the courtroom of heaven,” would instinctively reach for psychological or sociological categories: perhaps this is a coping mechanism, a way of giving structure to the chaos of life, or a community of belonging built around shared mythology. When my brother Samuel describes praying for Muslim men at a motorway service station rather than stewing in irritation, the secular interpreter might appreciate the ethical growth but dismiss the theology entirely. When a testimony surfaces about a former jihadist converted through the radical hospitality of a believing family, the natural mind celebrates the outcome — a radicalized individual de-radicalized through human kindness — while crediting therapeutic proximity and emotional resonance rather than the sovereign hand of God. And when my brother says plainly that a spirit attached when he left that house after our last gathering, the natural mind has no category for it whatsoever. It hears superstition at best, or the projective language of a mind trained in a particular religious subculture. The natural interpretation is, in each case, tidy, reasonable, and flatly insufficient. **The Limitation:** What the natural mind cannot perceive is that the vocabulary of this conversation is not borrowed from emotion or folklore — it is derived from a text written in a language that makes technical legal and governmental distinctions that English simply collapses, and from an apostolic tradition of covenant community that operated in spiritual dimensions the modern therapeutic framework has no instruments to measure. The flesh reads Luke 10:19 and hears something general about Jesus making the believer feel more confident. The Spirit reads it and sees a governor issuing a jurisdictional decree. The flesh sees the testimony of a man converted through hospitality and finds the warm efficiency of social psychology. The Spirit sees a God who arranged a truck and a coma and a hospital room and a dusty apartment and a loaded gun and a Voice — in a sequence so precise it could only be called sovereign. The flesh sees two friends talking on the phone. The Spirit sees priestly intercession threaded through ordinary time, covenant fellowship doing the work it was designed to do, and prophetic discernment operating calmly between two men who have been in enough spiritual warfare to recognize the terrain without drama. Until the veil that cannot be lifted by reasoning alone is removed by the Spirit of truth, the things freely given to us in Christ remain theoretically acknowledged and practically unused. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I have been sitting with this conversation for days now, and I want to lay out what I am seeing as carefully as I can — not as a systematic theology, but as a living meditation on the threads the Spirit kept weaving through an evening that looked, from the outside, completely ordinary. The first thread is the one that arrested me most immediately: the distinction in Luke 10:19 between two Greek words that our English translations treat as near-synonyms but that carry entirely different freight. Jesus says, in the standard English rendering, *“Behold, I give you the authority to trample on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy.”* But the original text is doing something far more surgical. The first word is ἐξουσία (exousia, ex-oo-SEE-ah) — legal authority, delegated governmental standing, the right to act within a jurisdiction. The second is δύναμις (dunamis, DOO-nah-mees) — raw miraculous power, ability, force. Jesus says: *“I give you* ἐξουσία *over all the* δύναμις *of the enemy.“* He is not saying Satan’s power has been removed. He is saying Satan’s legal standing — his right to deploy that power against the children of God — has been stripped and reassigned. The courtroom of heaven has issued a judgment. The adversary’s credentials have been revoked. He still has δύναμις (DOO-nah-mees). He simply has no ἐξουσία (ex-oo-SEE-ah) left over the redeemed. This reframes the entire question of why believers still suffer under spiritual attack. If Jesus had simply eliminated the enemy’s capacity, there would be nothing left to contend with. But that is not what happened at the Cross. The Cross did not annihilate the adversary; it dismantled his jurisdiction. He remains, as Peter describes, like a roaring lion walking about — but the key word is *seeking whom he may devour*. He is seeking permission. He is looking for legal grounding. And this is precisely why his primary strategy has shifted from raw domination to deception regarding identity and provision. He cannot simply crush a believing child of God because ἐξουσία (ex-oo-SEE-ah) has been transferred. So what he does instead is attempt to convince the believer that he still has that standing — that the old condemnation still holds, that the old identity still defines, that the old lack is still the truth of the situation. He operates through suggestion and agreement. He whispers the accusation, and if the believer receives it, speaks it, rehearses it — that spoken agreement becomes the grounding he needs. Every word carries weight in the unseen realm. This is why renunciation is not merely a psychological technique. When I say aloud, *“I renounce every word I have ever spoken in agreement with the enemy, every generational tie, every unclean thing I have welcomed through ignorance or rebellion”* — I am not performing a ritual. I am making a legal withdrawal of every inadvertent consent I gave. I am collapsing the false claim. And the enemy, having no remaining ἐξουσία (ex-oo-SEE-ah) of his own and now finding his borrowed grounding revoked, has nothing left to stand on. This is why James 4:7 is written in the order it is written. It does not say *“resist the devil and then submit to God.”* It says *“submit to God”* first — ὑποτάγητε (hupotag ēte, hoo-po-TAG-ay-teh) — *“resist the devil, and he will flee from you.”* Commitment to the Father establishes the jurisdictional reality. Resistance — ἀντίστητε (antistēte, an-TIS-tay-teh) — activates it in the field. The order is not arbitrary. It is covenantal mechanics. As I was reflecting on this with my brother Samuel, he observed that the most spiritually grounded believers he has known are simply not rattled — that they hold everything in light of eternity rather than allowing present distress to eclipse the reality of the Kingdom. I want to be careful here, because what he was describing is not stoicism. It is not emotional suppression or philosophical detachment. It is a people who have internalized the truth that Satan’s δύναμις (DOO-nah-mees), as fearsome as it feels in a given moment, cannot touch the core of who they are in Christ. Their identity is settled. And because their identity is settled, they are not susceptible to the enemy’s primary weapon — the assault on the self. The bomb can fall, and the soul is unmoved. *“Even if you didn’t have breath in your lungs, you’d still have Jesus, so you’d still be alive.”* That is not bravado. That is a theology of resurrection applied to present suffering, and it is available only to those who have genuinely yielded sovereignty to the One who holds it. The conversation then moved into territory that surprised me with how naturally it connected to the same framework. My brother recounted a testimony — a man trained as a jihadist, sent to America to wage war against the very people who would become the hands and voice of his salvation. What arrested me first was not the dramatic moment of encounter — the voice in the dark apartment — but everything that made that moment possible: the believing family who gave him the nicest room, prepared his meals, paid his first month’s rent, handed him a key to their car. They did not know they were part of a divine operation. They did not know the man they were housing had come to their country to destroy people like them. They simply had the capacity, and they used it. And Hebrews 13:2 carries a weight here I do not want to rush past: *“Do not forget to entertain strangers, for by so doing some have unwittingly entertained angels.”* The table you set in obedience may be the altar where someone’s eternity turns. The enemy overplays his hand — he trains someone to destroy the gospel and sends him into the heart of it, and the gospel does what the gospel does: it finds the man before the man can find his target. He has δύναμις (DOO-nah-mees). He has no ἐξουσία (ex-oo-SEE-ah). And the God who is sovereign over the willing and the unwilling alike wastes nothing — not even the adversary’s strategies. The moment in the apartment is the moment I keep returning to. He had been away for months. The dust was thick. The room was dark and dreary. He was alone, armed, and at the edge. And into that vacuum, a voice spoke his name. I think of Samuel in 1 Samuel 3 — called by name in the night, in a place of ministry, not yet knowing the voice. I think of Elijah under the juniper tree in 1 Kings 19, spent and suicidal, and the angel touching him saying, *“Arise and eat.”* I think of Paul on the Damascus road, undone by a question rather than a condemnation: *“Why are you persecuting Me?”* In each of these moments, God’s initiation preceded human response. The man in the apartment did not find God. God found him — by name, in the dark, with mercy shaped like a question. And then the voice declared its identity: *“I AM that I AM”* — יְהוָה (YHWH, YAH-weh), the self-existent, covenant God of Exodus 3:14, speaking now through the mouth of Jesus Christ, identifying Himself with the language of Revelation 1:8 — Alpha and Omega, beginning and end, absolute priority and finality. When Eternity speaks in a dark room, the only appropriate response is to fall to one’s knees. And he did. My brother Samuel made an observation that evening I want to weigh carefully: that many people have prayed their whole lives and never heard God’s audible voice, yet believe with genuine heart-conviction — and that God may not need to speak audibly to those whose hearts are already fully His. I think he is right, and I think it deepens the theology rather than complicating it. Hearing God’s voice audibly is not the measure of faith — it is sometimes the mercy offered to those who, like this former jihadist, are so far from the truth that only a direct interruption will reach them. The still small voice speaks through Scripture, through community, through answered prayer, through the slow accumulation of faithfulness. God is not louder for some because He loves them more. He is louder for some because they need a louder mercy. And that observation quietly challenged something in my own testimony: I said plainly in our conversation that I claimed the name of Jesus for a season without truly believing it in my heart — and during that time, I heard very little. Romans 10:9 does not merely say confess — it says *“believe in your heart.”* The Greek word is πιστεύω (pisteuō, pis-TYOO-oh) — to entrust, to commit oneself to, to rest one’s full confidence upon. I had the word without the weight. I had the confession without the commitment. The silence was not God’s absence. It was His patience with my unfinished surrender. The conversation with my brother then surfaced something I did not expect to carry this much weight: the Pharaoh thread. The hardening of Pharaoh’s heart in Exodus is one of the most theologically difficult passages in all of Scripture for the natural mind. But what the Greek of Romans 9 and the Hebrew of Exodus reveal together is that the hardening is not an imposition from outside against the man’s will — it is a releasing. God withdrawing His softening influence from a heart that has repeatedly and willfully refused it, allowing that heart to accelerate in the direction it has already chosen. And then the extraordinary thing: that hardened heart, swallowed in the Red Sea, becomes the testimony that travels five hundred miles to Nineveh. The Hebrew word שׁוּב (shuv, SHOOV) — to turn, to repent — describes Nineveh’s response, and that תְּשׁוּבָה (teshuvah, teh-SHOO-vah) was almost certainly prepared by the shockwave of what God had done to Egypt. God used rebellion. He used judgment as a sermon, and the sermon saved a city. Every Pharaoh is a message in transit. Every swallowed-up kingdom is a warning that travels. And the question the Spirit presses through this is always the same: am I, in this moment, the one whose heart is softening toward the word — or the one whose continued resistance is becoming someone else’s cautionary parable? I am also sitting with a thread that seemed incidental when it surfaced but has refused to leave: the singing. My brother mentioning a Bible verse about always having reason to sing. My own admission that I used to sing more than I do now. Both of us landing on worship as something natural and continuous rather than programmatic. This is not incidental. Singing in Scripture is not merely emotional expression — it is declaration. Paul and Silas sang at midnight in a Philippian prison, and the foundations shook. The Psalms are not supplementary to the real theology; they *are* theology, embodied in sung declaration, addressed to God and in the hearing of the principalities. Philippians 4:4 — χαίρετε (chairete, KHEH-reh-teh) — is a present active imperative: *rejoice always* — not as a response to favorable circumstances but as a continuous commanded posture of declaration in defiance of them. When I hum a worship song at work without knowing how loud I am, something in me knows that the sound matters in a register beyond the office. To always be singing is to always be declaring. And to declare is to continually remind the heavens — and myself — of who holds ἐξουσία (ex-oo-SEE-ah). The thread that perhaps runs deepest beneath all of these is the one about covenant fellowship itself — about what brotherhood is actually for and what it actually costs. My brother Samuel’s practice of calling someone from his contacts every week, someone he has not spoken to in five or six years; my own pattern of checking in on people through lunch break calls and Facebook DMs and voicemails that nobody asked for — this is not nostalgia. It is the practice of shepherds who cannot stop counting heads. Ezekiel 34 contains perhaps the most severe indictment in the entire prophetic corpus, and it is aimed not at pagans but at shepherds who fed themselves while leaving the weak unstrengthened, the sick unhealed, the broken unbound, and the lost unsought. The word the Spirit keeps pressing on me from this conversation is this: deliverance without discipleship is an incomplete work. When an unclean spirit goes out of a man and the house is swept clean but left empty, the last state of that man is worse than the first. Jesus said this in Matthew 12, and He said it as a diagnostic, not a metaphor. The vacancy must be inhabited. And the inhabitation happens through exactly the kinds of things this conversation contains — Scripture and Spirit threaded through ordinary time, priestly maintenance enacted through a voicemail and a check-in call, the fire tended so it does not go cold. What I said to my brother and need to sit with more carefully is this: if I had spoken nothing more to him theologically after his deliverance — if I had simply handed him Jesus and wished him well — he would not have lasted. I said it conversationally, almost in passing. But the apostolic model stands behind it with full weight. Matthew 28 is not a conversion commission — it is a formation commission. *“Teaching them to observe all things that I have commanded you”* is the language of sustained, sequential, relational impartation. The Greek word μαθητάς (mathētas, mah-thay-TAHS) — disciples, learners — does not describe people who attended an event. It describes people who remain in structured, ongoing relationship with a teacher. Paul understood this: he told the Thessalonians that he shared with them not only the gospel of God, but his own ψυχήν (psychēn, psoo-KHAYN) — his very soul — because they had become dear to him. The gospel travels embedded in personhood. You cannot disciple at arm’s length. You cannot intercede for someone you have never called on a lunch break. And then there is the house. My brother said plainly that when he left that house after our last gathering, a spirit attached. I confirmed immediately that I knew which one — that it had spoken to me as well. I want to name what we were doing in that moment, because the Western church often defaults to either nervous dismissal or theatrical exaggeration around this kind of disclosure, and neither serves the saints. Scripture is consistent from Deuteronomy’s prohibition of graven images to Acts 19’s bonfire of sorcery books in treating objects and spaces as capable of carrying spiritual significance. This is not animism. It is covenantal realism. A house holding four or five generations of one family is not merely a sentimental artifact — it is a בֵּית (bayit, BAH-yeet), a household, a covenant trajectory, carrying both the inheritance of faithfulness and the residue of whatever agreements with darkness were made within its walls. The smashed figurines I referenced are not decorative objects. They are vessels. And the principality that used them did not simply disappear when the objects broke; it sought a new host. My brother named this plainly, without theater, without shame. I recognized it immediately. That kind of calm, mutual discernment is what years of covenant proximity produces — and it is precisely the kind of prophetic community the Spirit is calling the church to recover. What ties every thread of this conversation together — the ἐξουσία (ex-oo-SEE-ah) stripped from the adversary, the hospitality that positioned a jihadist for salvation, the voice that spoke in a dark apartment, the singing at midnight, the covenant follow-through across lunch breaks and liquor counters, the spirit named in a grandmother’s kitchen — is a single, stubborn, beautiful truth: God is relentlessly personal. He knows names. He speaks into dark rooms. He arranges surgeons and spare bedrooms and keys to cars. He gives one man the capacity to bear what another man carries through a wilderness season. He wastes nothing. He is Lord over the willing and the unwilling alike. And the New Covenant community He designed is not a theological system to be managed at a distance — it is a living organism through which grace flows, often without warning, often through the most unlikely vessels, always in the form of someone who has already said yes to being used. ## Implications and Challenges There is a version of Christianity that is deeply sincere and deeply imprecise about spiritual authority — one that prays generally, rebukes vaguely, and wonders why the same patterns keep returning. The distinction between ἐξουσία (ex-oo-SEE-ah) and δύναμις (DOO-nah-mees) is not splitting hairs. It is the difference between knowing that the deed to the house is in your name and actually living in the house rather than sleeping on the porch because someone told you the locks were still changed. The legal reality and the experiential reality can diverge — and it is almost always because the believer has not yet understood or activated what was legally accomplished at Calvary. The invitation here is not to theological exactitude for its own sake but to practical liberation: audit your words, your inner narratives, your inherited patterns — not with condemnation, but with the precision of one who understands how the courtroom of heaven works. The testimony of a former jihadist converted through costly hospitality confronts the modern Western church with an uncomfortable question: would we have done what that believing family did? Would we have opened the nicest room in our house to a stranger recovering from catastrophic injury — a stranger whose background, ideology, and stated purpose were hostile to everything we believe? The church that answers yes in the abstract but has never actually opened a door to an uncomfortable guest must sit with the distance between what we profess and what we practice. Luke 6:27 — ἀγαπᾶτε (agapate, ah-gah-PAH-teh) — is an imperative and unconditional: love your enemies. Not after they have become something other than enemies. Before. The Kingdom does not wait for ideological alignment before it serves. It serves first, and lets the service be the sermon. The conversation also quietly challenges how we measure successful ministry. We celebrate numbers of salvations, healings, and deliverances. But the apostolic model is not measured in events — it is measured in formation over time. Paul did not celebrate the number of cities he passed through. He wept over the churches he had to leave. He wrote letters for years afterward. He sent Timothy. He sent Titus. He returned. The incompleteness of much modern evangelism is not a failure of theology; it is a failure of follow-through, and the cost is paid by the people who were delivered and then left without inhabitation. The empty, swept house is not a safe house. Deliverance must be followed by discipleship, and discipleship requires proximity, consistency, and the willingness to be available across lunch breaks and not only at altar calls. There is also a challenge embedded in my own honest confession during that conversation — that I heard one word from God, ran with it, and forgot to come back to the conversation. The error was not in the hearing. It was in the stopping. Revelation is not a single download. It is a sustained exchange with a God who has more to say than any one word can contain. The discipline of ongoing receptivity — fasting, returning, waiting, re-presenting oneself before the Throne — is not a spiritual luxury. It is the posture of the servant who understands that the Master still speaks, still directs, still corrects, and still has more light to release. Come back to the conversation. Keep the ears open. Let πιστεύω (pis-TYOO-oh) mean what it means: not mere verbal assent, but the full entrustment of a heart that has yielded sovereignty. The church’s occasional discomfort with the doctrine of divine judgment also deserves a gracious but direct word here. We want a God of love without a God of wrath — but Scripture offers no such deity. The good news of Nineveh’s repentance is inseparable from the seriousness of the warning Jonah carried, and the warning was credible partly because of what judgment had already done to Egypt. The wrath of God is not an embarrassment to be softened in preaching. It is part of the testimony. A God who cannot be angered by injustice and unrepentance is a God who does not actually love the oppressed. Let the church recover the full counsel — and carry it with both the urgency of Jonah and the love that intercedes at a motorway service station on behalf of men kneeling toward a false god. Finally, the conversation’s freedom — two men who came out of dark places, sitting in a grandmother’s kitchen with honest transparency about warfare and attached spirits and gray market history — is itself a theological statement. The church often struggles with testimonies that cannot be sanitized for a newsletter. But Scripture is full of people whose biographies could not be curated: Rahab, Matthew, Paul, the demoniac of the Gerasenes who became the first missionary to the Decapolis. The Kingdom has always been populated by the unlikely. The safety that makes this kind of honesty possible is not a product of careful program design — it is a product of covenant, sustained over time, built through shared risk and shared deliverance, and maintained through the kind of priestly consistency that checks in even when nobody is watching. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Understanding the distinction between ἐξουσία (ex-oo-SEE-ah) and δύναμις (DOO-nah-mees) reshapes how I read every spiritual battle in my own life. When darkness presses, the first question is no longer *“why is God allowing this?”* but *“what agreement have I made, spoken, or inherited that gave the enemy the grounding he needed?”* Maturity means learning to audit words, inner narratives, and inherited patterns — not with condemnation, but with the calm precision of one who understands how the courtroom of heaven works and who knows that the deed is already in their name. Maturity also means being the kind of person through whom God reaches the unreachable — someone who has given enough to have something to give, who has opened enough doors to know what it costs, and who follows up long after the dramatic moment has passed. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this calls forth is threefold and immediate. First: develop a discipline of daily declaration — speaking truth over identity, speaking the blood of the covenant over inherited dysfunction, speaking the Word over circumstances before circumstances speak their interpretation over me. Second: practice costly hospitality — open the nicest room, hand over the spare key, feed the stranger, and do not wait until the person seems deserving. Third: tend what God has lit — make the calls, send the messages, check in on the ones who were delivered, and stay close enough to the ones you are walking with that they can tell you when a spirit attached. There is no logistical excuse for failing to tend what God has ignited. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture of this hour is the posture of the intercessor and the shepherd combined — watching for the καιρός (kairos, KY-ros) moments God is arranging around us without our awareness, standing in ἐξουσία (ex-oo-SEE-ah) on behalf of those who do not yet know they need it, and discerning together rather than alone. What my brother Samuel and I did in that kitchen — naming a spirit plainly, covering each other without theater, maintaining the shared watchfulness of people who have walked this terrain before — that is the model. Not individual spiritual heroism. Covenantal prophetic partnership. Eyes open, together. And always singing — at work, in the car, at midnight — because the declaration never stops mattering in the realm where the real battle is being decided. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as a co-heir, a servant, a priest standing between the heavens and the earth — grateful that You are precise where I have been vague, personal where I have expected You to be distant, and patient where my understanding has been slow. I receive, right now, the full weight of what was legally accomplished at Calvary on my behalf: ἐξουσία (ex-oo-SEE-ah) over all the δύναμις (DOO-nah-mees) of the enemy, purchased at a price I cannot fully measure and extended to me through a covenant I did not initiate. I renounce every word I have spoken in agreement with the enemy’s narrative — about who I am, about what I lack, about what my family carries, about what is possible — and I withdraw every inadvertent consent, collapsing every false claim. I stand in agreement with Your will concerning the men trained to destroy the gospel who are Damascus Roads waiting to happen, concerning the men kneeling toward false gods at motorway service stations, concerning the newly delivered who are navigating the warfare of the recently freed without enough priestly follow-through around them. Fill every empty, swept house with Your inhabiting presence, Lord — let no vacancy remain. I call covered every person I have prayed for, every person delivered through the Name of Jesus who is somewhere out there walking through what comes after the miracle. For the houses carrying inherited darkness — the vessels smashed, the spirits named — I declare that Your blood speaks a better word than any ancestral agreement, and that the generations yet to come in these bloodlines are not bound by what was made in ignorance before them. Let the Body of Christ rise from the porch of experiential defeat into the house that is already legally theirs. Let what was spoken in this ordinary evening — in the warmth of covenant brotherhood, across the distance of continents, through the laughter and the Scripture and the honest transparency — become bread for the brethren who are hungry for something real. Every knee shall bow. Every tongue shall confess. You waste nothing. To You be all the glory, the honor, and the dominion — now and forever. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Luke 10:19 — “Behold, I give you the authority to trample on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy” | ἐξουσία (exousia, ex-oo-SEE-ah) — delegated legal authority / governmental standing; δύναμις (dunamis, DOO-nah-mees) — raw miraculous power / ability | Jesus grants ἐξουσία over the enemy’s δύναμις — jurisdiction over power, not elimination of power.
