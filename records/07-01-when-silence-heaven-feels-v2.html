# 07-01 Call Reflection: Divine Silence, Spiritual Testing, and Prophetic Calling **Title:** 07-01 at 07:03 — When the Silence of Heaven Feels Like Abandonment **Recorded:** 2026-07-01 07:03:14 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture — Phone/Video Call) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to this call unfold between myself and my brother Samuel, something kept surfacing beneath the exhaustion, the raw language, and the economic frustration — a man genuinely wrestling with God in real time, not performing faith, not pretending. I heard in both of our voices the sound of two servants who have not abandoned the Lord but who are genuinely staggered by His silence. The central tension of this conversation is ancient and it is this: what does a man do when God called him, clearly and unmistakably, and yet the present circumstances offer no visible confirmation that the call was real? **Why This Matters:** This matters because the modern church has produced a generation of believers who are largely unprepared for the silence of God. We have been taught to expect immediate confirmation, emotional warmth, and measurable blessing as the normal currency of a walking-with-God life. When the silence comes — and it always comes — many interpret it as abandonment, divine indifference, or evidence that they misheard the call in the first place. But the witness of Scripture from Genesis to Revelation is that divine silence is not the withdrawal of divine presence. It is often the most severe and most sacred form of divine trust. What I heard in this conversation is not two men losing their faith. It is two men being refined in it, even while they use vocabulary that would make a Sunday school teacher blush. God is not afraid of our honesty. He never has been. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural perspective, everything I described in this conversation has a reasonable, traceable, psychological explanation. A man facing financial instability, an unplanned pregnancy, physical exhaustion, relational strain, and the grinding monotony of survival-level labor would be expected by any counselor or social worker to exhibit exactly the symptoms I named — depleted motivation, loss of focus, irritability, hopelessness about the future, and a kind of dark ambient anger at the systems that seem to grind people down without mercy. The world would call this situational depression, economic anxiety, burnout, or perhaps the normal psychological weight of an approaching major life transition. It would prescribe rest, therapy, community support, and realistic financial planning. It would be sympathetic, and it would not be entirely wrong. These are real pressures pressing down on a real man in a real season of life. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind, no matter how compassionate, cannot perceive what is actually happening in the unseen order when a man whom God has called begins to sound like he is caving in. It cannot hear the covenantal weight behind the cry of “Eli Eli lama sabachthani” when it surfaces not in a theology lecture but in a car driving down a ND road. It cannot distinguish between a man who is losing his faith and a man whose faith is being purified through fire hot enough to burn away every substitute comfort. The natural mind sees only the surface — the complaining, the profanity, the suggestion of weed or worse — and either dismisses the whole conversation as spiritually immature or tries to resolve it with better advice. But the Spirit sees the hidden man underneath all of it, the one who said with remarkable clarity, *“I have faith. I know I am saved,”* and who in the same breath invoked the language of the Psalmist from the cross. That is not a man who has abandoned God. That is a man being held by God through something he cannot yet see. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with this conversation and let the Spirit move through it, the first thing I notice is how Samuel’s initial word — *focus* — was not accidental. He said he was being called, being impressed in the spirit, to speak that word over me. And I confessed immediately that I had lost all focus. That exchange, brief and almost swallowed by the roughness of the rest of the call, has the fingerprints of the Holy Spirit on it. When one member of the Body receives a word for another, even mid-sentence, even in a phone call full of noise and distraction, that is not coincidence. That is the Spirit of God moving through fraternal love to address a real condition. The word *focus* in the Hebrew wisdom tradition is not merely a matter of concentration — it carries the weight of שָׁמַר (shamar, SHAH-mar), meaning to guard, to keep watch over, to preserve. To lose focus in a scriptural sense is not merely to be distracted. It is to stop standing guard over the interior life. And that is exactly what I was confessing. I think about Job. I think about him often when I find myself in seasons like the one I described to Samuel. Job was a man who had genuinely walked with God, who had not hidden sin, who had offered sacrifices on behalf of his children — and yet God permitted a season of devastation so total that Job’s friends interpreted it as evidence of secret guilt. The modern church often reads Job’s suffering as an exceptional case, a cosmic drama not meant to repeat. But the structure of Job’s story — divine calling, satanic challenge, divine permission, season of silence and stripping, faithfulness under pressure, eventual restoration at a higher level — that structure is not exceptional. It is the pattern of the life God designs for those He trusts with the most weight. And I told Samuel something in the middle of that call that I believe was genuinely prophetic without me fully realizing it: *“He came to me. I didn’t go to Him. I ran from Him. He came to me.”* That is not the language of a man who stumbled into religion looking for comfort. That is the language of Jonah. Of Paul on the Damascus road. Of Isaiah in the temple saying *“Woe is me, for I am undone.”* When God initiates the call rather than receiving a human application, the weight of that calling is qualitatively different. And the suffering that accompanies it is proportionally heavier, because the one being formed is being formed for something the Lord has already decided requires that specific furnace. The prophet Jeremiah tried to quit. He said in Jeremiah 20:9, *“But His word was in my heart like a burning fire shut up in my bones; I was weary of holding it back, and I could not.”* The fire does not go out. That is the evidence of a genuine call. Not the joy, not the ease, not the visible blessing — but the fact that you cannot make the fire stop burning even when everything in you wants to walk away. I also want to sit with what Samuel said back to me in one of the most spiritually significant moments of the call, even though it came wrapped in casual language. He asked me: *“Have you asked the Lord if He has abandoned you?”* And when I confirmed I had, he pressed: *“What are you — stunned into anything? Nothing. So what does that tell you?”* The answer I gave was the cry of Christ Himself from the cross — אֵלִי אֵלִי לָמָה שְׁבַקְתַּנִי (Eli Eli lama sabachthani, AY-lee AY-lee LAH-mah sheh-vak-TAH-nee) — *“My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?”* — the opening of Psalm 22. And what I said next was truthful but incomplete: *“So I’m just going to keep doing what I am doing, I guess.”* What I did not fully articulate in the moment, but what the Spirit is showing me now as I reflect, is that Psalm 22 does not end in abandonment. It ends in vindication. It moves from the cry of desolation in verse 1 all the way to *“For He has not despised nor abhorred the affliction of the afflicted; neither has He hidden His face from Him; but when He cried to Him, He heard”* in verse 24. The silence I am experiencing is not the end of the Psalm. I am somewhere in the middle verses, in the pit, surrounded by the bulls of Bashan, bones out of joint, heart like wax — but the Psalm is not finished. There is something I said that Samuel seized on immediately and correctly: *“Usually when He goes quiet, it means you’re being tested.”* This is true and it runs deep in canonical pattern. The Spirit of God led Jesus into the wilderness immediately after the voice from heaven declared, *“This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased”* (Matthew 3:17). The affirmation and the testing were not in tension. They were sequential steps in the same divine program. The forty days of wilderness silence from the Father were not a withdrawal of love. They were the necessary proving ground for everything that would follow. The same pattern governs Abraham’s call to offer Isaac. God had already established covenant with Abraham, had already renamed him, had already promised nations from his body — and then came the most severe test. Genesis 22:1 says, *“Now it came to pass after these things that God tested Abraham.”* After these things. After the covenant was confirmed. Testing comes to those who have already received the promise, not to those who have not yet been called. The question I asked Samuel — *“How do I even complete the test?”* — is the right question. And Samuel’s answer was right: *“Keep returning to the Lord. Keep returning to the Word.”* But I want to press deeper into why that answer is not merely good advice but is in fact the mechanism of the test itself. The test is never about whether I can achieve a sufficient output of righteousness. The test is always about whether I will remain tethered to the source when the source goes quiet. The Hebrew concept of בָּטַח (batach, bah-TAKH) — trust, confident reliance, the kind of leaning that transfers the full weight of one’s body onto a surface — is what is being forged in this season. Proverbs 3:5 commands: *“Trust in the LORD with all your heart, and lean not on your own understanding.”* The phrase *lean not on your own understanding* is not a rebuke of intelligence. It is a warning against the specific temptation of this season — which is to interpret God’s silence through the lens of visible circumstances and conclude that He has withdrawn. בָּטַח (bah-TAKH) does not require a felt sense of God’s nearness. It is a volitional posture maintained precisely when that felt sense is absent. What also moves me is the moment when I said, *“I can curse the world and bless His name in the same breath.”* That is not contradiction. That is the voice of the Psalms. James 3 addresses the tragedy of blessing God and cursing men from the same mouth. But cursing the world system — the economic machinery that grinds people into poverty wages, the \$60 tank of gas that disappears in a week, the math that says a child is coming and the numbers don’t add up — that is not the curse James warns against. That is the honest lamentation of a prophet who has not yet made peace with Egypt. The Psalms of lament, nearly a third of the entire Psalter, are saturated with this exact posture: raw, unfiltered grief about the world as it is, coupled with an unwillingness to let go of God as He is. Psalm 73 opens with Asaph nearly slipping entirely because he saw the prosperity of the wicked, but it closes with *“Whom have I in heaven but You? And there is none upon earth that I desire besides You”* (Psalm 73:25). I am in the early verses of Psalm 73, not the last. I want to name something else that Samuel said which has the character of a word: *“You’ve received the biggest blessing — now you must pay the biggest costs. You must take the biggest tests. And you’ve been honored with the ability to take it.”* This is sound theology delivered in real time between two brothers without formal theological training, and it is backed by the entire arc of Scripture regarding divine promise and divine preparation. Hannah wept before she received Samuel. Sarah laughed before she received Isaac. Elizabeth hid herself in shame before she received John. The pattern is consistent: the magnitude of the promised gift is proportional to the depth of the preparatory suffering. The child coming into my life — this Elijah — is not arriving into a finished, comfortable, well-resourced situation by accident. He is arriving into a crucible. And the crucible is not a sign that God has abandoned the promise. The crucible is the means by which the promise is made safe to carry. I also notice something I said almost in passing: *“Maybe the enemy is winning the war in my mind.”* This is not self-pity. This is discernment. Paul wrote in 2 Corinthians 10:4-5 that *“the weapons of our warfare are not carnal but mighty in God for pulling down strongholds, casting down arguments and every high thing that exalts itself against the knowledge of God, bringing every thought into captivity to the obedience of Christ.”* The war in the mind is a real theater of spiritual conflict. The fog I described, the inability to relax, the ambient anger, the fatigue that sleep does not touch — these are not only symptoms of economic pressure. They are marks of a spiritual assault targeting the one thing that cannot be taken by force: my faith. The enemy cannot remove my salvation. He cannot erase my call. He cannot undo what God has covenantally established. What he can do is generate a fog thick enough that I stop acting from the position of what I know and start reacting from the position of what I feel. I renounced that spirit in the moment — *“Adonai rebuke it”* — and I mean that renunciation now with even greater clarity. The business conversation that filled much of the second half of this call is not spiritually irrelevant to the meditation. Samuel spoke with genuine faith about the path forward, about getting to ten thousand dollars a month each, about what it would mean to have that stability in place before Elijah is born. And while the natural mind hears a sales and marketing discussion, the Spirit shows me something else: two men who were, moments earlier, in varying degrees of spiritual despair, finding their footing again through the exercise of faith applied to concrete, temporal responsibilities. This is not the prosperity gospel. This is stewardship theology. The parable of the talents in Matthew 25 does not spiritualize the five talents into something abstract. They are real resources given to real servants who are expected to engage, risk, and produce while the master is away — while the master is, from the servant’s perspective, silent and absent. The servant who buried his talent did so out of fear. The servants who invested did so out of faithfulness. What I heard in Samuel’s voice as he mapped out the path to ten thousand dollars a month was not greed. It was the activation of the dormant faith that had been present all along, redirected toward practical obedience. Finally, I come back to the word Samuel brought at the very opening: *focus*. And I hear it now with greater weight than I did in the car. The opposite of שָׁמַר (SHAH-mar) — guarding, keeping watch — is not distraction in the mild, modern sense. It is the condition described in Proverbs 25:28: *“Whoever has no rule over his own spirit is like a city broken down, without walls.”* A city without walls is not merely inconvenient. It is defenseless. Every marauding thought, every demonic accusation, every economic fear, every comparison with others whose prayers seem to be answered faster than mine — all of it walks in unopposed when the walls are down. Focus, in the Spirit’s economy, is not a productivity strategy. It is a matter of spiritual survival. And the way the walls go back up is not through self-discipline alone but through return — return to the Word, return to the presence, return to the altar of honest and unadorned prayer where I bring exactly what I brought to Samuel today, minus nothing, and lay it before the One who already knows it all and has not moved. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this conversation raises is against the prosperity gospel and its deep infection of Western Christian expectation. I said it plainly in the call: *“There is no such thing as the prosperity gospel, dude. You can work your entire life and still get nothing to show for it.”* This is not pessimism. It is biblical realism. Paul worked with his hands as a tentmaker. Jeremiah died without seeing the restoration he prophesied. John the Baptist was beheaded in a dungeon. The call of God is not a guarantee of material comfort in the present age. It is a guarantee of eternal inheritance, covenantal presence, and the formation of a character capable of bearing the weight of glory. Any theology that makes present material prosperity the primary evidence of divine favor will produce saints who cannot survive a season like the one described in this conversation. The second challenge is against the tendency to measure God’s activity by emotional feedback. I said plainly that I have heard nothing from Him for months, and that it feels like abandonment. The feeling is real. The feeling is also not authoritative. Feelings are data points, not doctrinal sources. Hebrews 13:5 quotes God directly: *“I will never leave you nor forsake you.”* The Greek verb for “leave” here is ἀνίημι (aniēmi, ah-NEE-ay-mee) — to loosen, to let go, to send back — and it is reinforced with a double negative in the original, making the construction emphatic beyond anything English grammar typically produces. God is not being politely reassuring. He is making a categorical covenantal declaration. The silence in my experience is not evidence against that declaration. It is the occasion for believing it beyond feeling. Third, this conversation challenges the common Christian assumption that the proper response to spiritual struggle is to clean up the language and present a tidy testimony. Both Samuel and I used profanity throughout this call. I am not endorsing that as a spiritual ideal. But I am noticing that God did not withhold His movement from the conversation on account of the vocabulary. Samuel brought a genuine word. I received genuine clarity. The Spirit moved through unwashed vessels, as He always has. The church would do well to stop gatekeeping access to God behind a particular register of language and start asking whether the heart underneath the words is genuinely reaching toward the Lord. David’s laments in the Psalms are not polished. They are primal. And God called David a man after His own heart. Fourth, I want to be clear about something I said in this call that requires honest examination: the suggestion of returning to methamphetamine, even stated as a joke, was not a harmless comment. I recognized it myself and retracted it. But it surfaces something important: when the Spirit is pressing for focus and the flesh is desperate for relief, the enemy will offer counterfeit forms of both. The fake focus of stimulants, the fake peace of substances — these are not neutral choices. They are spiritually charged substitutes for the genuine article. Paul wrote in Ephesians 5:18, *“Do not be drunk with wine, in which is dissipation; but be filled with the Spirit.”* The filling of the Spirit is the only legitimate answer to the fog I described. Every other filling is a temporary masking of the very condition God is working to transform. Fifth and finally, the moment when I renounced the spirit of complaint — *“I renounce that spirit. It’s not of God. Adonai rebuke it”* — was perhaps the most powerful moment of spiritual authority in the entire call, and it happened quickly and without ceremony. This is how spiritual authority often operates in the lives of those who are genuinely walking in covenant. Not in formal settings with elaborate protocol, but in real time, mid-conversation, in a car on a North Dakota road. The saints do not need a stage to exercise dominion. They need a will submitted to God and a mouth willing to speak what the Spirit presses. That moment was a genuine prophetic act, and its fruit will be seen. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** The maturity this conversation calls me toward is not the polished kind that knows all the right things to say in a small group setting. It is the kind that can hold the full tension of Psalm 22 — crying out in genuine desolation while refusing to let go of the covenant God who permits the desolation. Maturity means learning to distinguish between the silence of abandonment and the silence of the furnace. One destroys. The other refines. The evidence of which one I am in is not found in my circumstances. It is found in whether the fire of the original call is still burning in my bones, whether I still cannot make it stop even when I want to. If I can still say, even in the hardest conversation, *“I know I am saved”* — the fire has not gone out. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this calls for is specific and immediate: return to the Word. Not as a religious exercise, not as a therapeutic technique, but as the act of a covenant son returning to the covenant document to remember who he is and whose he is. The Bible Samuel and I both agreed I needed to go home and read is not merely a book of comfort. It is the living and active word described in Hebrews 4:12, dividing between soul and spirit, between what I feel and what is actually true. Kingdom obedience in this season means refusing to make decisions about the future based on present fog, and refusing to interpret God’s silence as evidence against His faithfulness. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this season demands is the posture of Habakkuk in chapter 2: *“I will stand my watch and set myself on the rampart, and watch to see what He will say to me.”* That is שָׁמַר (SHAH-mar) in active form — a watchman who has not left his post, who has not decided the battle is over, who remains positioned to receive what the Lord will speak. The prophetic life is not a life of continuous dramatic revelatory experience. It is a life of sustained positioned faithfulness in seasons when heaven seems closed. Those who hold the position through the silence are the ones who are ready to receive and transmit the word when it breaks. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one of Your servants — a son You called before I called upon You, a voice You lit before I knew I was burning. I bring this conversation to You unedited and unashamed, because You were present in every mile of that drive and every ragged word of that exchange, and nothing I said was hidden from You or surprising to You. I thank You that You have not abandoned me, even when the silence has been long enough to make abandonment feel like the only explanation. I thank You that the cry of אֵלִי אֵלִי לָמָה שְׁבַקְתַּנִי (AY-lee AY-lee) is not the end of the Psalm, and that You, who heard it from the lips of Your own Son in the darkest hour in history, hear it from me now with the same covenantal love that raised Him from the dead. I ask for the restoration of focus — not as a productivity gift but as the return of the walls around my spirit, the rebuilding of the watch-post, the renewal of the guard over the interior life You have been forming in me through this furnace. I speak agreement with Your Word over the child coming into my life — that You, who tested Abraham after the covenant was established, already know exactly what You are doing in the timing of this arrival, and that You are faithful to complete what You began. I stand in agreement with my brother Samuel over the declaration he made in faith — that provision will break through before Elijah is born — and I receive that not as positive thinking but as a prophetic word spoken by a man who carried the Spirit’s impression to call me back to focus. Let this season of silence produce in me what it is designed to produce: a בָּטַח (bah-TAKH) that does not require visible confirmation to remain operative, a faith that holds its position on the rampart when nothing is moving, and a voice that will know, when You speak again, exactly what to do with what You say. To You be the glory in the fog, in the furnace, in the silence, and in the breaking through.
