# 04-06 19:26 Personal Meditation: Where Are You? **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation (live phone call between Samuel Hunt and Seth Tillotson) with integrated post-reflection and closing spoken prayer **Personal Meditation: Where Are You? — The Father’s Unanswered Question and the Altar That Must Fall** ## Opening Observation I have been sitting with a conversation — a late-night phone call that moved from grief to theology, from a brother’s heartbreak to the deepest question God has ever asked of a human soul. What began as a check-in on a man carrying real pain became, by the Spirit’s sovereign threading, an unfolding of something much larger: the distance between religious familiarity and genuine surrender, between saying *it’s whatever* and being utterly undone before the Living God. As I listened to my brother Samuel work through his grief, his father’s deflections, and his own longing, I felt the Spirit anchoring everything to a single, ancient, unanswered cry: *Where are you?* That question has never stopped being asked. And I am not sure how many of us have stopped long enough to answer it honestly. **Why This Matters:** The stakes of this conversation are not merely personal — they are generational. My brother Samuel is in a season of stripping, and his father’s exchange revealed something that is not merely one man’s struggle but a condition that has quietly spread through entire households and congregations: a posture that acknowledges God in the abstract while remaining completely unreachable by Him in the particular. When a man can cite the Bible, reference Christ, debate the Catholic Church, and still answer *“Do you accept Jesus Christ as your Lord and Savior?”* with a smirk and a metaphor — something has gone profoundly wrong. And if the Body of Christ cannot name what has gone wrong and speak to it with both love and clarity, we leave entire generations stranded in a spiritual no-man’s-land: close enough to religion to feel covered, far enough from Christ to be lost. This meditation is for those who are somewhere in that space — and for those of us who love them enough to stay on the phone. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, looking at what Samuel described — his own heartbreak over Emily, his father’s intellectual resistance, the frustration of sending message after message into silence — would organize all of it into a coherent psychological narrative: a young man in an attachment crisis, a father exhibiting classic defense mechanisms in the face of existential pressure, and a friend group trying to offer emotional scaffolding to someone whose nervous system is overwhelmed by romantic loss. The psychological lens is not without mercy here; it would note that Samuel’s father, trained as a psychologist, is doing precisely what psychologists often do — he is intellectualizing the threat, redirecting the emotional charge through philosophical abstraction, and using the Catholic Church’s failures as a displacement technique to avoid the more personal and piercing question being laid at his feet. In this reading, the whole conversation is about identity threat, coping mechanisms, and the understandable human resistance to vulnerability. And the world would say: give him time, meet him where he is, don’t push, let him come to his own conclusions. **The Limitation:** But this framework, however compassionate in its surface texture, is categorically blind to what was actually happening in that room and on that phone call. It has no category for the fact that God was *already present* in that conversation — not as an idea being discussed but as a Person being deflected. The psychological mind cannot perceive that Samuel’s father was not simply *protecting his ego*; he was, in the language of the prophets, stopping his ears and closing his eyes against a light he could not name. The natural mind has no lens for the spiritual reality that a man who says *“me and God have an understanding”* has, in that very sentence, redefined God into a crisis-response utility — a deity on standby, activated only when the flesh has exhausted its own options. And it has no framework for what it actually costs a soul to stand before Holiness and remain unchanged. The mind of the flesh will always reframe surrender as weakness, repentance as psychological regression, and the cross as an interesting ancient metaphor. But the cross is not a metaphor. It is the architecture of reality, and everything else is shadow. ## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation As I listened to Samuel recount that conversation with his father, something in me recognized the terrain immediately — not because I have walked in his father’s particular life, but because I have watched the same pattern play out in my own household and in the lives of people I love. There is a specific kind of spiritual resistance that does not look like atheism. It borrows the language of faith. It references the Bible. It even defends certain moral principles. But when the direct question is finally asked — *Do you accept Jesus Christ as your Lord and Savior?* — it reaches for metaphor, pivots to institution, deflects to philosophy, and finally settles into something like *“it’s whatever.”* That phrase — *it’s whatever* — is not indifference. It is a closing of the door. And I felt the weight of it. The question I keep returning to is the one the Father asked first — and has never, in the full sweep of human history, stopped asking. When God walked through the Garden in the cool of the day and called out *“Where are you?”* to Adam and Eve who were hiding among the trees, He was not asking for coordinates. He knew exactly where they were. This was not a search; it was an invitation. And it was the first question ever spoken by God to a fallen human soul, which means it is the question underneath every other question He has ever asked since. Where are you? The Hebrew beneath that moment — אַיֶּכָּה (ayekah, ah-YEH-kah) — carries within it not only spatial inquiry but a kind of relational ache. It is a word that only makes sense if proximity was the original design. You only ask *where are you* if someone was supposed to be *here*. And here is what the Spirit has been pressing into me since that call: He never stopped asking. The incarnation did not silence the question — it amplified it. When the Word became flesh and walked among us, when He stood at the tomb of Lazarus and wept, when He asked Peter *“Do you love me more than these?”* — all of it was the same ancient question taking new form: *Where are you? Are you here with me, or are you somewhere else?* Paul captures the relational urgency of this in 2 Corinthians 6:17–18 when he renders the Father’s voice: *“Come out from among them and be separate, says the Lord. Do not touch what is unclean, and I will receive you. I will be a Father to you, and you shall be My sons and daughters.”* The condition is directional. *Come out. Come here. Where are you?* What arrested me in Samuel’s account of his father is how precisely his father illustrates a figure the prophets knew well: the man who honors God with the correct vocabulary while remaining, at the deepest level of the will, entirely somewhere else. Isaiah 29:13 captures it with startling precision — *“These people draw near with their mouths and honor Me with their lips, but have removed their hearts far from Me.”* The Lord does not say their theology is wrong. He says their hearts are far. This is the most dangerous kind of distance — not the distance of the confessed atheist, but the distance of the man who has arranged all the furniture of religion around an empty throne. Samuel’s father can talk about Tony’s self-improvement courses, the Catholic Church’s failures, the private nature of faith — but none of it brings him one inch closer to the Person behind all those conversations. He is near to the discussion and far from the Presence. I said to Samuel during the call — and I believe it now more than I did then — that this is why so many people settle for the phrase *it’s whatever* when Christ is offered to them. They are not being casual. They are being self-protective. Because accepting Jesus is not a transaction; it is a death. It is what Paul called in Galatians 2:20: *“I have been crucified with Christ; it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me.”* The verb there in Greek is συνεσταύρωμαι (synestaurōmai, soon-es-TOW-ro-my) — *I have been co-crucified*. It is a perfect passive: a completed action, received from outside the self, whose effects continue into the present. You do not self-generate this. You cannot negotiate it. It happens to you when you stop resisting it. And the flesh, whether in the body of a philosophy student or a practicing psychologist or a fifty-something man who has built an entire worldview without a crucified center, will do almost anything to avoid that complete undoing. And yet — this is where the Spirit deepens it — the Father’s question is not accusatory. It is longing. When I think of what it cost Him to pay the price that would allow Him to ask that question face to face, without a burning mountain or a curtain or an intermediary, my understanding of *where are you* shifts entirely. He did not die to give us a religion. He did not rise to become the subject of a self-improvement curriculum. He did it so that the distance between His breath and our breath could be permanently, covenantally closed — so that He could be, as He always intended, *with us*. And when Samuel’s father says *“me and God have an understanding,”* what he is unknowingly confessing is that he has kept the distance. He has allowed God into the category of *emergency resource* while blocking Him from the category of *first love*. He has made a utilitarian agreement where a betrothal was on offer. Samuel said something that I will not forget quickly. He said that accepting the love of a Father is hard — not because it is absent or weak, but because it comes with correction. And this is precisely why the Hebrew concept of מוּסָר (musar, moo-SAR) — fatherly discipline, moral instruction, the correction that forms character — is so consistently connected in Proverbs and in Hebrews 12 not to wrath but to *sonship*. Hebrews 12:6 says it plainly: *“For whom the Lord loves He chastens, and scourges every son whom He receives.”* The man who has never been corrected by God has never been received by God as a son. He may be a created being under God’s general mercy — but he has not entered the covenant relationship of fatherhood and sonship that the New Covenant makes possible. Samuel’s father’s resistance to correction — his deflection, his defensiveness, his settled intellectual distance — is not merely a theological error. It is a relational wound dressed up as philosophy. I was also struck by what Samuel said about greed and posture — a thread I had been exploring with him previously that he was now carrying into the crucible of a real conversation with his own father. The insight that greed is a posture rather than a tax bracket is scripturally rich in ways the natural mind tends to miss. When the woman with two λεπτά (lepta, LEP-tah) — the small coins Jesus noticed in Mark 12:41–44 — cast them into the treasury, Jesus did not calculate the amount. He discerned the posture. She gave from her poverty; the others gave from their surplus. The Lord’s economy measures orientation, not output. And when Tony, whoever he is, sells the words of the Gospel as a life-improvement product, the sin is not the dollar amount. It is the posture of extraction: treating the Living Word as raw material for a personal enterprise rather than bowing before it as the revelation of a sovereign God. Samuel was right to name it. And his father’s discomfort with that naming tells me he has more invested in that posture than he has yet admitted to himself. What Samuel said about Paul — *“if a man like Paul had to go through what he went through, do not think that your ignorance is now accepted by Christ”* — stopped me. Because there is a form of spiritual entitlement that is perhaps the most culturally embedded heresy of our generation: the belief that sincerity is sufficient, that a private arrangement with God exempts one from the terms of the covenant. Paul was arguably the most qualified man in first-century Judaism — trained under Gamaliel, zealous for the law, blameless according to the righteousness of Torah, as he says in Philippians 3:6. And when he encountered the risen Christ on the Damascus road, all of that collapsed. He did not negotiate a private understanding. He was undone, blinded, left without food or water, and then rebuilt from the ground up. And even after all of that, when he carried the Gospel to city after city — being stoned in Lystra, shipwrecked three times, beaten with rods, imprisoned, left for dead — God’s answer to his pleading was not deliverance from the thorn but *“My grace is sufficient for you, for My strength is made perfect in weakness”* (2 Corinthians 12:9). This is not the god of a private understanding. This is a Father who loves His sons too much to spare them the refiner’s fire. I spoke during that call about four different fires — a thread I have been developing in studying the remnant theology of the prophets — and I want to sit with the refiner’s fire in particular, because I believe that is the fire Samuel is in right now. Malachi 3:2–3 asks: *“Who can endure the day of His coming? And who can stand when He appears? For He is like a refiner’s fire and like launderers’ soap. He will sit as a refiner and a purifier of silver; He will purify the sons of Levi.”* The refiner does not cast the silver away. He applies heat. The purpose of the heat is separation — dross from metal, impurity from substance. What is being refined in Samuel right now is not merely his heart toward Emily. It is his entire relational architecture. God is asking: *Where are you?* And the answer He is cultivating in my brother is: *Here. Undistracted. Turned toward You.* I closed that call with prayer, and I want to return to the heart of it now, because I believe it captures what the Spirit was doing in the full arc of that conversation. I prayed for μετάνοια (metanoia, meh-TAH-noy-ah) — a genuine, lasting, directional change of mind that is not merely remorse but reorientation. Not just sorrow for what was lost, but a turning of the whole self toward what has always been the true and first love. Samuel does not need Emily to come back. He needs the altar to fall. He needs the idol — not because Emily herself is wicked, but because any love that eclipses the First Love becomes a competing throne — to be cleanly dismantled, not violently, but with the steady, gracious hand of a Father who will not share His place. And then, from that cleared and ordered place, from that throne room of the heart fully surrendered, every other love can be given back, properly ordered, received as gift rather than grasped as necessity. This is the word I am sitting with for Samuel, for his father, and honestly for myself: the question has never changed. The Father is still walking through the garden. The day is still cool. And He is still asking — not in accusation, not in condemnation, but in that ancient, aching, covenantal voice that has been searching the earth since the first hiding began — *Where are you?* ## Implications and Challenges There is a version of faith that has become so domesticated by Western culture that it can coexist indefinitely with a completely unchanged life. Samuel’s father is not a caricature — he is a portrait of a generation that grew up adjacent to Christianity, absorbed its moral architecture, and concluded that proximity to the conversation constitutes participation in the covenant. The challenge this meditation lays at our feet is uncomfortable: it is entirely possible to be fluent in the language of the faith, willing to reference God in a crisis, and still be standing at infinite distance from the Person the language is about. We must be willing to name this — lovingly, clearly, and without retreating into comfortable vagueness. The modern Western church has, in many streams, replaced encounter with attendance, and presence with performance. The result is a generation of people who have learned to schedule God rather than abide in Him — who set aside twenty minutes in the morning for devotion as though the Father is a meeting on a calendar, rather than the atmosphere they are breathing. I said it plainly during the call and I will say it again here: if you are struggling to maintain a prayer life through religious discipline, the mechanism is wrong. Prayer is not a spiritual exercise routine. It is the breath of the new creation. If it requires the white-knuckled effort of schedule-keeping to sustain it, something more fundamental needs to change — not the schedule, but the heart’s orientation toward the one you are supposedly talking to. The self-improvement industrial complex that Samuel’s father cited is not a peripheral distraction — it is a genuine spiritual competitor, and it is winning in many lives precisely because it offers the fruit of transformation without the cost of surrender. It takes the wisdom of Proverbs, the psychology of the Psalms, the leadership principles of Nehemiah, and packages them as human technology for human flourishing. And in doing so, it achieves something the enemy has always wanted: it makes God useful without making Him Lord. We must be clear — not harsh, but clear — that the Gospel is not a success strategy. The cross is not a life hack. And anyone using Scripture as raw material for personal development courses, without bowing before the God those Scriptures reveal, is doing precisely what Samuel named: extracting from the King’s treasury without pledging allegiance to the King. The Catholic Church conversation is worth pausing on, not to relitigate institutional failures — which are real and serious — but to name the rhetorical sleight of hand that Samuel’s father was performing, likely without realizing it. He was using the corruption of the institution to discredit the Person the institution claims to represent. But this logic, followed consistently, would require us to reject fatherhood because some fathers have been abusive, or to reject love because some loves have been manipulative. The failures of the visible church — Catholic, Protestant, Orthodox, Charismatic — are real and will be answered for. But they do not alter the identity of Jesus of Nazareth, who died and rose and lives. The invitation to know *Him* — not the institution, not the tradition, not the cultural expression — remains open and unadulterated by any human corruption. Finally — and this one is for those of us who love people in the wilderness, who are on the phone at midnight with a brother who is in grief and reaching for the wrong things — we must resist the temptation to give pastoral advice as a substitute for prophetic truth. Samuel needed both that night. He needed to be heard in his pain, and he needed to be told, clearly and with love, that the things he was doing to ease the pain were making it worse, and that the thing he was searching for was not in the direction he was running. That is not cruelty. That is the physician’s hand on the wound before the healing begins. Grace does not mean the absence of hard truth. It means hard truth delivered by someone who is willing to stay on the phone until 1:00 in the morning to make sure it lands. We close where we began — with a question, not an answer. *Where are you?* The Father is not distant. He is present, searching, aching for proximity. The only movement required is the honest answer: *Here I am. Undone. Turned toward You.* That answer — if it is real, if it comes from the marrow rather than the lips — is the beginning of everything. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity is not the accumulation of theological knowledge or the discipline of religious habit — it is the progressive dismantling of every false altar that competes with the Father’s rightful place. This conversation challenges me to examine not just what I believe *about* God but where I actually *run* when the pain hits. Do I run to the secret place, or do I run to the phone, the bottle, the feed, the relationship that cannot ultimately hold the weight I am placing on it? Maturity is measured by the speed and instinct of that turning — how quickly the soul, when it feels the pull of a counterfeit comfort, redirects its whole weight toward the only One who can carry it. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The call to obedience that emerges from this meditation is specific and immediate: when the urge to send another message, make another call, or reach again for the thing that has already been withheld — stop. Go to the secret place. Bring that specific longing to the Father by name. Let Him be the first recipient of what your heart wants to pour out. This is not repression; it is consecration. It is the lived practice of *“seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things shall be added to you”* (Matthew 6:33). The discipline of the secret place is not religious performance — it is the daily reordering of desire around the right throne. **For Prophetic Posture:** Those of us who carry a prophetic function in the Body of Christ must resist the modern softening that would have us validate every emotional state without confronting the spiritual reality beneath it. Samuel’s father needed someone who would agree with him long enough to find the fault line in his own argument — and that is exactly what Samuel did, by the grace of what had been imparted to him. This is prophetic posture: not shouting, not condemning, but following the thread of a man’s own logic until it leads him to the place where only the Gospel can speak. We must be trained in that kind of patient, scripture-saturated discernment. And we must be willing to speak the hard word with warmth, stay in the conversation when it gets uncomfortable, and trust the Spirit to carry what we deposit into the ground of another person’s heart. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I stand before You now as one who has been found — not as one who arrived by his own searching, but as one who was known before the foundations were laid and called out by name from the noise of the world. I stand as a co-heir of the Kingdom through the blood of Your Son, as a servant of the Living God, as one to whom You have shown secrets and entrusted words — and I do not take that lightly. I bring Samuel before Your throne now, with everything he is carrying: the grief that will not quiet, the love that has nowhere right to land, the messages sent into silence, and the aching that no human voice has yet been able to reach. You are not surprised by any of it. You were present in every one of those messages. You were present in that phone call. You are present now. Lord, I decree and declare in agreement with Your revealed and immovable will: let the altar fall. Let every competing throne in my brother’s heart be gently and permanently dismantled — not in violence, but in the steady mercy of a Father who loves His son too much to share His place. Let the metanoia (meh-TAH-noy-ah) be real and lasting — not a moment of emotion but a reorientation of the whole man. And I bring Samuel’s father before You now, not in accusation but in intercession: You are still walking through his garden. You are still asking *where are you.* Let that question reach him in the night, in the quiet, in the place where no psychological framework can give an answer. Let him run out of metaphors. Let him run out of deflections. And let him find, in that exhaustion, that You were there all along — not as a crisis resource, not as a private agreement, but as the Father who gave everything so the distance could be closed forever. I thank You that You do not forget the prayers I have prayed and lost track of. I thank You that Your memory of my brother is longer and more faithful than my own. I surrender him and his father into Your hands — hands that were pierced for exactly this — with thanksgiving, with confidence, and with the full weight of a covenant that cannot be shaken. In the matchless, eternal, ever-living name of Jesus Christ — the same yesterday, today, and forever — Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Genesis 3:9 — “Where are you?” | אַיֶּכָּה (ayekah, ah-YEH-kah) | A relational cry, not a spatial search; God’s first question to fallen humanity, expressing covenantal longing rather than judicial accusation. | | Isaiah 29:13 — “These people draw near with their mouths and honor Me with their lips, but have removed their hearts far from Me.” | לֵב (lev, LEV) — heart / inner person / will | The Hebrew lev encompasses not just emotion but the seat of the will and moral orientation; to have a far lev is to have a fundamentally redirected self. | | Galatians 2:20 — “I have been crucified with Christ; it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me.” | συνεσταύρωμαι (synestaurōmai, soon-es-TOW-ro-my) — co-crucified | Perfect passive indicative: a completed event received from outside the self, whose effects persist in the present.
