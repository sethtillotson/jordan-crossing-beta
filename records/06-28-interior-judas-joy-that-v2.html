# 06-28 Call Reflection: Faith, Discernment, and Kingdom Ethics in Commerce **Title:** 06-28 at 12:05 — The Interior Judas and the Joy That Confounds Kingdoms **Recorded:** 2026-06-28 12:05:12 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture / Call Reflection) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother work through a business proposal with genuine intelligence and entrepreneurial vision, I found myself repeatedly drawn back to a single undercurrent pulling beneath all the financial architecture and market strategy: the ancient temptation to fix the fallen world on the fallen world’s terms. What arrested me most deeply in this exchange was not the business model itself, but the theological question hiding inside it — the question of what it actually means to be set apart, and whether the calling to *climb to the top* in order to *bring it all down* is a word from the Lord or a very sophisticated version of the same impulse that moved Judas toward the sword. I am not certain I answered everything correctly in the moment, and I am still sitting with much of it now, but I know the Spirit would not let me pass certain things in silence. **Why This Matters:** The question of how a follower of Jesus engages with commerce, empire, and systemic evil is not peripheral to discipleship — it is near the center of it. Every generation of the church has faced a version of this tension: the temptation to leverage the mechanisms of Babylon in order to accomplish Kingdom ends. This is not merely a financial or entrepreneurial question. It is a question about identity, eschatology, and what it means to live as an alien and stranger in a system that runs on entirely different logic than the Kingdom of God. If we get this wrong, we do not merely make poor business decisions. We misrepresent the Christ who overturned tables not to seize the temple economy but to expose it. Getting this right — or at least staying honest about the tension — matters for every believer who earns a wage, builds a company, or wonders how their work intersects with their witness. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the perspective of natural human reasoning, everything my brother described makes pragmatic sense. He identified a gap in the market, studied the regulatory and financial infrastructure around it, found a legal path through high-risk payment processing, and constructed a model that diversifies revenue, expands the total addressable market, and monetizes an underserved category of app developers. His instinct to use sophisticated AI tooling for deep research, to structure sub-accounts in order to manage churn, and to layer setup fees with revenue share is the kind of thinking business schools reward. Even his theological framing — that God told him to get to the top of the game in order to bring it down — reads, through natural eyes, as an inspiring fusion of calling and competence: a man who wants to use his gifts at scale for righteous ends. The ethical concern I raised would be dismissed in most boardrooms as either naivety or overcaution, since disclosure, clear terms and conditions, and legal compliance are, by the world’s standard, sufficient justification for any monetization strategy. **The Limitation:** But the reasoning of the natural mind, however sophisticated and even sincerely intentioned, operates within a framework that cannot perceive the spiritual weight of the posture being adopted. There is a kind of blindness that is not stupidity — it is the structural limitation of a lens that was not designed to see certain things. The mind that evaluates a business model primarily through legality, market fit, and revenue potential will always miss the dimension that the Spirit is most concerned with: not *what* is being built, but *what spirit* is building it, and *what image* it ultimately reflects in the one doing the building. The question is not only whether the T&Cs are clear. The question is whether the soul of the builder is being quietly conformed to the logic of extraction, leverage, and systemic advantage — even while the builder sincerely believes he is operating under divine commission. That is the very danger that makes this conversation worth sitting with long after the call has ended. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sat with this exchange after the call, I kept returning to one phrase my brother said that I think deserves the most careful attention: *“The Lord told me His first words to me were, ‘Get to the top of the game so you can bring it all down.’”* I want to be clear that I am not dismissing my brother’s faith or his sincerity. What I am pressing into is the discernment question beneath that claim, because the history of the church is full of sincere people who received what they believed was a divine commission to infiltrate and reform a corrupt system — and who were, in the process, slowly formed *by* that system rather than remaining agents of transformation *within* it. The leaven works quietly. That is precisely what makes it leaven. The scriptural pattern I keep returning to is the contrast between two kinds of kingdom strategy: the strategy of Judas and the strategy of Christ. Judas was not a villain in his own mind. He was a visionary. He believed that if Jesus could be maneuvered into a corner — forced to reveal His power, compelled to call down fire, pushed into open confrontation with Rome — the messianic kingdom would break through by force and the occupation would end. His logic was not irrational. It was the logic of a man who saw a genuine evil, possessed a genuine relationship with genuine power, and could not comprehend why that power was being held in reserve while people suffered. The tragedy of Judas is not that he was faithless — it is that his faith was in a version of Jesus that Jesus refused to be. I said it to my brother directly, and I want to press it deeper here: what set the first-century believers apart from every other sect, every other reform movement, every other revolutionary current in the Greco-Roman world was not their theology alone, and certainly not their economic strategy. It was their joy under pressure. It was the inexplicable posture of men and women who were dragged before magistrates, stripped of property, imprisoned, and martyred — and who did it without bitterness, without a counter-strategy, without needing to win on the world’s terms. That was the scandal. That was the thing the empire could not process. The Roman system was built on the assumption that everyone ultimately wants power, safety, wealth, and survival. When they encountered people who genuinely did not — people who had found something more stabilizing than all of those things — the system had no category for it. That *categorylessness* was itself a form of testimony. The Greek word that keeps surfacing in me as I reflect on this is χαρά (chara, KAH-rah) — joy. Not happiness, which is circumstantial and dependent on favorable conditions. χαρά (KAH-rah) in the New Testament is almost always associated with conditions that would, by any natural accounting, produce the opposite. James opens his letter by saying to count it all χαρά (KAH-rah) when you fall into various trials — not after the trials resolve, but *in* them, because the proving of faith produces something that no business infrastructure can manufacture: ὑπομονή (hypomonē, hoo-poh-moh-NAY) — patient endurance, the kind of staying power that is not grinding your teeth and waiting for circumstances to improve, but is genuinely rooted in a reality that circumstances cannot touch. When I told my brother that he should be able to find joy waking at four in the morning, working twelve-hour shifts in a place where he feels disliked, surrounded by people whose lives carry real pain — I was not being glib. I was pressing into what I believe is the actual commission. Not the commission to build a payment processing architecture that reshapes the app economy, but the commission that every believer carries regardless of their industry or platform: to carry the love of God so completely that it overflows into everything around you without needing the right circumstances to justify the overflow. That is the witness that confounds systems. That is the thing empires cannot co-opt. There is a word the Lord has been pressing into me recently, and I shared it in the call: *it is not about the destination — it is about changing the journey to the destination, and reframing how you perceive all of reality.* I want to sit with that here more carefully. Because I think my brother heard it as a call toward a more exploratory, adventurous posture — a rebranding of the journey. But what I was reaching for was something more radical than adventure. I was reaching for μετάνοια (metanoia, meh-TAH-noy-ah) — not merely repentance in the sense of behavioral correction, but a fundamental reorientation of perception. A different mind. A mind that sees the same circumstances and draws entirely different conclusions about what those circumstances mean, what they are producing, and what is being asked of us within them. The grief I feel in a world where England is watching Russian troops move and Iran escalate and America cycle through its perpetual war economy is real. My brother’s instinct to *do something* with that grief is not wrong in itself. The impulse to act, to build, to leverage skill and intelligence and vision toward something better — none of that is inherently corrupted. What I want to watch carefully is whether the grief over the fallen world is being transmuted into a desire to beat the fallen world at its own game. Because that desire, however spiritually framed, is still operating within the logic of the world. It is still trying to win on terms the world set. And the Kingdom of God, as Jesus consistently demonstrated, refuses to compete on those terms. Here is the tension I want to name without resolving too quickly: my brother is not wrong that God calls people into economics, commerce, and systemic engagement. Joseph entered Pharaoh’s financial system and was used by God within it. Daniel served in the courts of Babylon and Persia without being absorbed by either. Lydia was a merchant of purple cloth whose home became a church. The call to come out of Babylon does not mean the call to have no economic life. But Joseph never forgot he was a Hebrew slave who arrived through a pit. Daniel never ate the king’s food. Lydia’s home opened downward — toward baptism, toward the river, toward the community of the poor and the traveling apostle. There is a consistent pattern in the biblical record: those whom God uses within corrupt systems are marked by a profound *detachment* from the system’s logic even while functioning competently within it. They do not need the system to validate them. They do not need to win by its metrics. And that detachment is not merely intellectual — it is spiritual and emotional and visceral. It is the product of having found in God something that the system cannot offer and therefore cannot threaten. What concerns me about the framing of *getting to the top in order to bring it down* is not the ambition per se, but the implicit assumption that influence at scale is the primary vehicle of Kingdom impact. Jesus had twelve disciples and spent most of His ministry in a small region of an occupied territory. He never went to Rome. He never addressed the Senate. He never scaled His operation. He gathered twelve, poured into them, and died at thirty-three having never left the land of His birth — and that apparent failure became the hinge of all human history. I am not saying that no one is called to platform or scale or systemic influence. I am saying that the measure of Kingdom faithfulness is not scale. And when the primary energy of a person’s spiritual imagination is focused on *getting to the top*, there is a real and documented danger that the top will reshape the person long before the person reshapes the top. I also want to be honest about something I said in the call that I want to hold with more nuance here. I invoked Judas, and my brother rightly pushed back that he had been taken somewhat out of context. He was making the point that since God is here, we should *live fully*, not waste days, love deeply. That instinct is right and good and I honor it. The Judas comparison was reaching for something real — the interior Judas, the part in all of us that wants to accelerate the Kingdom by force, that grows impatient with the slowness of love, that wants to hand God a plan rather than surrender to His — but I should be careful not to weaponize that image beyond what the conversation actually warranted. The interior Judas is a warning for *all of us*, including me. Especially me, in moments when I feel the clarity of the prophetic call and lean into it harder than the relationship requires. What the Lord seems to be saying through this whole conversation, as I sit with it now, is something simpler and more demanding than any business model or geopolitical strategy: *be where you are*. Not as passivity, not as resignation, but as the most radical form of presence available to a human soul. Carry the love of God so fully in the place where you actually are — the job you hate, the family situation that is difficult, the geopolitical moment that is frightening, the conversation with a brother whose vision you respect even when you cannot fully affirm it — that the Kingdom of God becomes visible not through what you build but through *who you are becoming* in the building. ## Implications and Challenges The most immediate challenge this conversation surfaces is the tendency among believers with genuine gifts and genuine calling to spiritualize ambition without subjecting it to the full weight of scriptural discernment. Receiving a word from the Lord is a beginning, not an ending. The prophets tested words. The early church tested words. Paul subjected his own revelation to the scrutiny of the Jerusalem apostles not because he doubted his calling but because he understood that isolated revelation, however sincere, is always at risk of being shaped by the desires and wounds of the one receiving it. My brother’s willingness to sit with the word, to come back to it, to discuss it openly — these are signs of genuine spiritual maturity. But sitting with a word must include asking not only *what does this confirm* but *what does this challenge in me*. There is also a challenge here for those of us who operate in the discernment and prophetic function within relationships. I said several true things in this call, but I also said some of them too quickly, in the heat of real-time conversation, without the measured care they deserved. Prophetic correction offered too bluntly — even when accurate — can land as condemnation rather than invitation. The goal of fraternal discernment is not to win the theological argument. It is to love the person in front of you toward greater clarity and freedom. I am asking myself whether I did that as well as I could have, and I think the honest answer is: partly. The instinct was right. The love was real. The execution was uneven. The subscription trial model my brother described — the 365-day free trial with clear terms and conditions — is not inherently unethical in legal terms, but it does operate in an ecosystem where the primary revenue mechanism depends on a percentage of users failing to cancel. This is worth naming honestly. The ethical legitimacy of clear T&Cs does not fully resolve the question of whether our business model should be architecturally dependent on human inertia, forgetfulness, or cognitive overload. Those are the mechanisms that subscription trap models exploit, even legal and transparent ones. Being set apart from the world does not mean refusing to participate in commerce. It means asking, at every structural level, whether the way we make money reflects the character of the God we claim to serve. That is a higher standard than legality. It is the standard Paul articulates in 1 Corinthians 10:23 — all things are lawful, but not all things are helpful; all things are lawful, but not all things edify. The geopolitical section of this call — the discussion of Russia, Iran, England’s vulnerability, America’s war economy — was brief but spiritually significant. My brother lives inside a different information environment than I do, and the divergence between what American media was reporting and what his monitoring tools showed was striking and sobering. The lesson I take is not primarily geopolitical but spiritual: we cannot afford to be formed by the information ecosystems of any single nation-state, media outlet, or political system. The people of God in the first century were not shaped by Roman news. They were shaped by Scripture, by apostolic teaching, by the community of believers, and by prayer. Our discernment of global events — including the very real possibility of escalating conflict — must be rooted in the same formation, not in the anxiety of whoever is most effectively alarming us today. Finally, I want to name something that lives underneath this entire conversation: the weight of carrying a prophetic sense of calling while working a job that feels beneath that calling. My brother’s restlessness is not simply entrepreneurial ambition. It is the ache of a person who believes he was made for something larger than where he currently stands. I know that ache. The temptation in that ache is to make the future the place where your real life begins — to defer joy, presence, and identity until the infrastructure is built and the vision is realized. The Lord’s consistent word, at least as I have been receiving it, is that this deferral is a subtle trap. The Kingdom does not begin when your plan succeeds. The Kingdom is already at hand, which means it is available right here, right now, in the job you hate, in the body that is tired, in the relationships that are complicated, in the moment that is insufficient. That is precisely where the glory lives — not in spite of the insufficiency, but through it. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This conversation calls me — and all who will read this reflection — toward a more rigorous practice of discernment that distinguishes between sincere spiritual experience and confirmed scriptural revelation. Not every word we receive in the secret place is from the Lord. Some are amplifications of our own desires, however genuinely we have submitted those desires to God. Maturity means learning to hold a received word with open hands long enough for it to be tested by Scripture, by community, and by the fruit it produces in our character — not only in our circumstances. And maturity also means extending the same generous patience to a brother’s unfolding discernment that we would want extended to our own. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The call that emerges from this conversation is not to stop building, stop thinking, or stop engaging the economic and geopolitical realities of our moment. It is to build *from* a settled identity rather than *toward* one. The obedience asked of us is the obedience of χαρά (KAH-rah) — joy that does not require the right platform, the right revenue stream, or the right geopolitical conditions. Find it now, here, in the insufficient present. Carry it into every interaction. Let it be the witness that confounds systems more thoroughly than any market disruption ever could. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this call reinforces is one of watchful abiding. We are not called to fix the fallen world. We are called to be present within it as people whose citizenship is elsewhere, whose joy is unshakeable, and whose love is not contingent on being understood, rewarded, or vindicated. We watch. We discern. We speak what the Spirit gives us to speak, with as much grace as we can muster, and then we release the outcome. That is the narrow path. It has many trees, much shade, and no shortcuts — but it leads somewhere the wide road cannot reach. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who is still learning the difference between the voice of the Spirit and the amplified echo of my own desires. I thank You for the brother You placed in my life — a man of genuine vision, real intelligence, and sincere love for You — and I ask that You guard his soul with the same care You have shown mine. I ask that the seed of this conversation would fall on ground that is being prepared by Your hand, not crushed by impatience or swept away by the wind of the next urgent thing. Lord, I confess that I carry the interior Judas too — the part that wants to accelerate what only surrender can produce, that wants to engineer what only love can build. Forgive me where my correction was sharper than it needed to be and fell short of the gentleness You model. I agree with Your Word that the joy of the Lord is our strength, and I ask that χαρά (KAH-rah) — that deep, unshakeable, circumstance-defying joy — would become the primary mark of everyone who bears Your name in this fractured, frightening, beautiful moment in history. Let us be the people who get arrested full of joy. Let us be the ones whose witness the empire has no category for. Let us be found faithful — not at the top of any game, but at the feet of the One who descended lower than any of us will ever go, and by that descent, drew all things to Himself. To Him be the glory, now and in the age to come. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | James 1:2 — “Count it all joy when you fall into various trials” | χαρά (chara, KAH-rah) — joy | Not circumstantial happiness but a settled gladness rooted in what trials produce rather than what they feel like;
