# 05-09 Call Reflection: Kingdom Stewardship, Wilderness Formation & Divine Sovereignty **Title:** 05-09 at 16:56 — The Geometry of Increase: Kingdom Stewardship, Wilderness Formation, and the Sovereignty That Holds All Things Together **Recorded:** 2026-05-09 16:56:41 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother Samuel unfold a property sourcing model layer by layer — with genuine precision and entrepreneurial creativity — something in me recognized more than strategy. I heard the echo of a parable. I heard a young steward learning to multiply what has been entrusted to him, and doing it not by accident but by thinking beyond the obvious layer into the next, and then the next. But what I did not anticipate when the conversation began was how quickly it would move from the practical to the theological — from deal structure and referral commissions to the Parable of the Hidden Treasure, from financial formation to the question of free will and sovereignty, and finally to the cosmological declaration that the Son of God is actively holding the fabric of all creation together at this very moment. **Why This Matters:** The temptation in the Body of Christ is to artificially divide the sacred from the secular — to imagine that Kingdom principles operate only inside church walls while the real world runs by different laws. But the parables of Jesus were not decorative illustrations imported from commerce. They were drawn from commerce, from agriculture, from property management, from lending — because the God who created the laws of sowing and reaping also created the laws of margin, referral, compound return, and network leverage. What I heard in this conversation was not merely two men strategizing about real estate. It was the sound of formation made visible — two years of partnership, failure, learning, and refinement producing a wisdom that could not have been purchased at any business school. And beneath all of it, threading through every topic like a hidden root system, was one of the most pressing questions a steward can carry: how does a man who genuinely believes in a sovereign God also genuinely believe his choices matter? That question is not a distraction from discipleship. It is one of its most essential disciplines. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the natural vantage point, this conversation is straightforward: two entrepreneurs strategizing, one presenting an income model, the other affirming and expanding it with complementary thinking from a different market context. The world would read this as networking, as business consulting, as two ambitious young men trying to find financial traction in an unforgiving economy. The analysis would center on market viability, risk assessment, scalability, and the audacity of attempting something complex without prior capital. When my brother mentioned his divorce as the catalyst for his new clarity and focus, the secular mind would file it neatly under resilience — the self-help industry’s favorite narrative of turning pain into power, packaging suffering as rocket fuel for ambition. When the conversation turned to private credit funds, EBITDA multiples, and behavioral data mining, the natural mind would celebrate the financial sophistication and perhaps caution about the risks of overreach. And when the remark about childlike faith surfaced, or the cosmological tangent about space-time and divine transcendence appeared, the natural observer would classify these as motivational overlay — psychologically useful framing that makes risk-taking feel cosmically endorsed. The world hears the strategy. It does not hear the formation that produced it. **The Limitation:** What natural reasoning cannot perceive is that this conversation carried at least three distinct spiritual registers simultaneously — and the movement between them was not accidental. The natural mind can chart the distance between a man crying in a foreign country over a broken marriage and a man confidently describing a multi-layered property deal to a potential investor, but it cannot weigh what happened in the interior during those years. It can measure output but not consecration. It cannot see that the specific mechanics of multi-layer margin extraction, contractor pre-vetting, project management fees, and estate agent relationship-building did not emerge from raw intelligence alone — they emerged from a curriculum the Spirit ran in the furnace of two years of trying, failing to fully capitalize, and learning what the gaps were. It cannot detect the invisible thread of formation running through every phase of the exchange. And it is utterly without capacity to perceive the significance of a single question — *“Faith in who?”* — that functioned not as a rhetorical challenge but as a theological plumb line dropped into the middle of a conversation that had been proceeding entirely at the level of practical vision. The Spirit was doing something underneath the economics, and only those with ears to hear it could receive it. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I want to begin where the Spirit began pressing me — at the foundation, before the deal structure or the investor pitch or the cosmological excursion. There is a parable in Matthew 13 that surfaced mid-conversation almost without my intending it, and it has not released me since. Matthew 13:44 reads, *“Again, the kingdom of heaven is like treasure hidden in a field, which a man found and hid; and for joy over it he goes and sells all that he has and buys that field.”* The conventional reading celebrates the sacrifice — he sold everything. The common application is about prioritizing the Kingdom above comfort. But what arrested me when I first read this, and what surfaced again mid-conversation, is something more particular and more strange: the text does not say he dug up the treasure. It says he bought the field. He became the steward of the land that contained the treasure. And I cannot get past that distinction. The Hebrew concept embedded throughout the Old Testament’s relationship to land is one of custodianship rather than mere ownership. Leviticus 25:23 makes this explicit — אֶרֶץ (erets, EH-rets) — the land, the earth — belongs ultimately to God: *“The land shall not be sold permanently, for the land is Mine; for you are strangers and sojourners with Me.”* Human beings were the land’s stewards, not its ultimate proprietors. So when Jesus places a parable of the Kingdom inside the framework of a man purchasing a field, He is not simply describing a real estate transaction. He is evoking the entire covenantal theology of Israel’s relationship to the earth — and redefining what it means to possess something of Kingdom value. The man does not extract and move on. He remains. He tends. He becomes responsible for the ground that contains what God buried. And that is precisely the posture I heard forming in my brother as I listened to him describe pre-vetting contractors in multiple cities, building trust relationships with estate agents before a deal exists, and constructing systems for a business that hasn’t fully opened yet. He is buying the field. Not just hunting the treasure. This field-buying posture is where I begin to see the clearest connection between stewardship and what the Spirit showed me about the two years. My brother said it plainly: *“Dude, a hundred percent. I’ve learned so much.”* And I confirmed it — almost two years of building businesses that did not reach their intended scale. The world calls that failure. The Spirit calls it curriculum. Proverbs 24:27 says, *“Prepare your outside work, make it fit for yourself in the field; and afterward build your house.”* The Hebrew wisdom tradition is consistently practical before it is theoretical — חָכְמָה (chokmah, KHOK-mah) — wisdom — in Proverbs is never abstract. It is the capacity to look at a field and understand what it requires before demanding from it what it can give. My brother now carries that capacity because the seasons of non-capitalization trained it into him. The furnace was not punishment. It was preparation. And what looked like a wasted chapter was the season in which the tools were being forged. The same Spirit that trains a steward through formation also teaches him to recognize value where others miss it. I am thinking here of the specific moment in the conversation when my brother described the mistake most beginning property investors make — going too small, choosing two-bedroom flats when the real margin lives in properties of a different scale. As I heard this, something shifted in me spiritually. Luke 16:10 says, *“He who is faithful in what is least is also faithful in much.”* The Greek word πιστός (pistos, PIS-toss) — faithful, trustworthy, reliable — denotes proven reliability, a quality demonstrated over time and through process. But that verse has a trajectory. Faithfulness in little is not a ceiling — it is a launchpad. The servant who remains forever at the smallest scale out of fear or false humility has not honored the text. He has stopped at the first clause and missed the destination. There is a smallness of vision that disguises itself as humility, and it is one of the subtler ways the enemy keeps Kingdom stewards from the full scope of their assignment. And yet — and I must hold this with equal weight — the greatest danger facing the steward who has graduated from small to large is not external. It is the moment the vision dresses itself in Kingdom language while secretly serving the self. This is the tension I named openly in the conversation when I said I heard my own past self in some of what was being articulated — the excitement about buying churches, deploying capital, mixing financial accumulation with prophetic language. I want to be careful not to condemn what I was naming, because the impulse itself is not wrong. Solomon built the Temple with extraordinary resources and received divine commendation for it. Abraham was wealthy. Lydia, who opened the continent of Europe to the Gospel, was a prosperous dealer in luxury textiles. The issue is not the scale. The issue is the *mastery*. Jesus was unambiguous about Μαμωνᾶς (Mamōnas, mah-MOH-nahs) — the Aramaic personification of wealth as a rival lord — in Matthew 6:24. He did not say wealth is impossible for the righteous. He said no one can serve two masters. The question is whether wealth is the tool of the servant of God, or whether the servant of God has subtly become the instrument of wealth. That question does not get answered in the business plan. It gets answered in the interior — and it requires the kind of honest self-examination that only the Holy Spirit can perform. This is what brought me to ask my brother the question I felt I had to ask: *“Faith in who?”* He answered it beautifully and correctly — *“Of course, God.”* But I was pressing further than the object of his faith. I was asking about the *shape* of it. There is a faith that says “God will make it go exactly as it’s supposed to go” as a form of genuine surrender, and there is a faith that says the same words while secretly meaning “God will validate the plan I’ve already constructed in my imagination.” The Hebrew distinction here is worth honoring. בִּטָּחוֹן (bittachon, bih-tah-KHON) — confident trust, reliance — is a posture of genuine vulnerability before a God who may redirect rather than simply bless. The Psalmist must again and again reorient his בִּטָּחוֹן (bih-tah-KHON) away from the expected deliverance and back toward the Deliverer Himself. My question was that reorientation offered in real time — not as correction, but as invitation to inhabit the theology he had already articulated more fully. My brother’s answer to the follow-up — what would he think if it didn’t go as he’d imagined — was in fact eschatologically sound. He said, essentially: *“We’ve already won. It doesn’t matter.”* That is not spiritual bypassing. That is the confidence Paul articulates in Romans 8:28, where συνεργέω (synergeō, sin-er-GEH-oh) — to work together, to co-operate toward an end — appears in the present active tense. God’s governance of outcomes is not singular; it is ongoing and simultaneous. All things — the deal that closes and the one that doesn’t, the season of two years that didn’t reach its scale and the season now beginning — are being actively governed toward a good that exceeds what any single outcome could produce. My brother had intuited this. The posture question I was pressing was not correcting his theology. It was inviting him to let that theology govern his urgency, not merely his vocabulary. I find something prophetically significant in the conversation’s movement toward what my brother described as his newfound focus — the clarity that emerged from devastation. The image of him sitting in America weeping over a broken marriage and then becoming someone who thinks in multi-layered property deals and private credit structures is not merely a resilience story. It is the fingerprint of God in the ruins. The Scripture that arrests me here is not a triumphalist prosperity text. It is the forty years in the desert of Sinai, where God took a nation of slaves — broken by Egypt, weeping for what they had left behind, unable to imagine themselves as anything other than what Pharaoh had told them they were — and used that very crucible to strip them of everything that was not covenant. Moses emerged from the backside of the desert not merely motivated but *formed*. The burning bush did not find a man who had never suffered. It found a man who had survived both palace and pasture, both power and obscurity. The wilderness was not the punishment. It was the preparation. And Psalm 51:17 describes the specific kind of breaking God honors: נִשְׁבָּר (nishbar, neesh-BAHR) — shattered, structurally fractured, the kind of breaking that changes the composition of what remains. God does not break His servants cruelly. He breaks them surgically. And what rises from the נִשְׁבָּר (neesh-BAHR) season carries something that comfort alone could never produce. The conversation opened — almost unexpectedly — into cosmology, and I want to honor that moment because it was not a distraction. My brother raised the question of dimensional reality, the fabric of space-time, and without planning it I found myself at Colossians 1:17 — *“And He is before all things, and in Him all things consist.”* The Greek word συνέστηκεν (synestēken, sin-ES-tay-ken) — the perfect active indicative of a verb meaning to hold together, to cohere, to stand in ordered relation — is not a metaphor. It is a present-tense declaration. The reason the fabric of creation has not collapsed is not the laws of physics; it is because the Son of God is actively holding it together at this moment. My brother said it well: God is outside space and time, which is precisely why He can hold them. He is not subject to what He created. He is the ground of its existence. And once that reality lands — once it actually lands rather than remaining theological vocabulary — everything downstream changes. The investor meeting becomes less fraught. The deal structure becomes less totalizing. The two-year season of incompletion becomes neither failure nor waste. When the One who holds all things together is the One you have handed the outcome to, the weight of the thing shifts off human shoulders and back onto the shoulders of the only One actually strong enough to carry it. This brought the conversation, finally, to what I think was its most revelatory point — the paradox of Kingdom agency. My brother said something worth letting breathe: *“As much as it’s in my hands, it’s not in my hands at all.”* That is not fatalism. That is not passivity. It is the living tension Scripture holds across its entire narrative arc. We are co-laborers with God (1 Corinthians 3:9), which means we truly labor. But Psalm 127:1 holds the other side: *“Unless the LORD builds the house, they labor in vain who build it.”* The Hebrew שָׁוְא (shav, SHAHV) — vanity, emptiness, nothingness — describes what human effort produces when disconnected from divine mandate, regardless of how polished the execution appears. I am not building a case for passivity. I am building a case for the specific kind of active surrender that Nehemiah modeled — praying in the moment before the king, then acting the instant the door opened (Nehemiah 2:4–5). Planning is real. Action is real. The question is whether the plan preceded the commission, or whether it emerged from it. Proverbs 16:9 holds both sides in one verse: *“A man’s heart plans his way, but the LORD directs his steps.”* לֵב (lev, LAYV) — heart, inner person, the seat of genuine human will — plans genuinely. And the LORD directs genuinely. Both things are fully true at the same time. The paradox is not a problem to be solved. It is a covenant reality to be inhabited. I also cannot move past the moment where I noted that what my brother was developing was essentially what I had poured into a book, and he laughed and said, *“Bro, you are taking what I taught in the book.”* That exchange carries something of 1 Corinthians 3:6 — *“I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the increase.”* The Greek ηὔξανεν (ēuxanen, ay-OOK-sah-nen) — he caused to grow, gave increase — is always God’s contribution. Two men in partnership, each planting something in the other across nearly two years, and then watching ideas come back transformed, enhanced, applied in ways neither fully anticipated — that is the Kingdom ecology of iron sharpening iron. The increase was not the exclusive product of either mind. It emerged from the communion of the partnership and ultimately from the blessing of God upon seed that was faithfully sown. I did not take credit in that moment, and rightly so — because what my brother built upon what I shared belongs to him. The Spirit adds the increment. The steward does not manufacture growth; he creates the conditions the Grower honors. What I want to say as I close this section of the meditation is this: the Kingdom of God is not embarrassed by competence. It is not threatened by margin. It is not suspicious of financial strategy or multi-layered deal structures. What the Kingdom is allergic to is the *idolatry* of competence — the moment stewardship becomes self-worship, or when multiplication becomes the end rather than the means. The financial systems being discussed in this conversation are not neutral in their current architecture — the behavioral data economy, the AI-driven demographic profiling, the harvesting of human psychological architecture for commercial consumption — these carry within them the signature of what John saw in Revelation 18:13, where Babylon trades in ψυχάς (psychas, psoo-KHAHS) — the accusative plural of ψυχή (psoo-KAY), the human soul, the inner life, the animating self. I said it plainly during the call: the appropriate response to a Babylon economy is not withdrawal into irrelevance. It is the building of an alternative. The saints who are present in those spaces — literate in the language of capital and systems and leverage — are the ones positioned to build something holy when the exodus from the current architecture comes. And it will come. George Müller, whom I named in the conversation, is the right model: radical faith and genuine responsiveness, not recklessness baptized in prayer, but attentiveness so complete that recklessness becomes unnecessary. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this conversation surfaces is one I feel personally and name with full grace: the danger of dressing Kingdom language over entrepreneurial ambition and calling the combination a divine mandate. The impulse to use capital for Kingdom purposes — to buy churches, fund Christian ventures, deploy resources for the spread of the Gospel — is not inherently wrong. But the question is never merely “Is this a good thing for God?” The question is “Did He send me to do this particular thing at this particular time?” Uzziah offered incense he was not appointed to offer (2 Chronicles 26:16–19), and it was not the incense that was the problem. It was the unauthorized hand. Good intentions do not sanctify unauthorized action. The corrective is not to abandon the vision but to hold it open-handed before the Lord and submit it fully — before the first step is taken, not merely in retrospect. A second challenge concerns the two years. It would have been easy — and is easy for many believers — to interpret seasons of incompletion as divine abandonment or personal inadequacy rather than formational process. The Body of Christ desperately needs a theology of productive suffering — a framework for understanding how God uses devastation not merely to comfort us but to *restructure* us for Kingdom purpose. In a Western church that has largely traded the theology of the cross for a theology of comfort, believers are left without a hermeneutic for their pain. They do not know what to do with a marriage that ends, with a business that doesn’t reach its intended scale, with the silence that feels like screaming. The Spirit challenges us to re-read our histories with prophetic eyes: where did the formation happen, and what did it produce? The answer almost always exceeds what success alone would have taught. Third, and I say this with genuine warmth, the strategy of leveraging human psychology and biological attention to close financial deals — whether through a carefully curated team member or through AI-driven behavioral targeting — sits uneasily in my spirit not because it is unusual in the marketplace, but because it is a form of working *around* people rather than *with* them. The Kingdom standard for commerce is not merely “does it work?” but “does it honor the dignity of the person being engaged?” This is not a prohibition but a question worth sitting with. Revelation 18’s language about Babylon trading in souls was written for a generation that would need ears to hear it in a new register. The saints need to name the spiritual architecture behind the digital systems they inhabit daily, not merely critique them from the outside, but begin building and championing alternatives. Fourth, the theology of the wilderness must be recovered in full, held with precision. Pain is not a detour from God’s purpose — it is frequently the route to it. But prolonged residence in a pain-powered identity can itself become a subtle form of idolatry. The breaking is not the destination. The formation that the breaking produces is meant to graduate the servant into greater stewardship. The energy that emerges from a devastated season is real and powerful, but it is not automatically consecrated. The drive born from a broken marriage can produce extraordinary fruit, but it requires the intentional submission of that drive to the lordship of Christ — not once as a ritual but in every decision about where the capital flows and whose interests are actually being served. The flesh and the Spirit can produce nearly identical behaviors in seasons of high motivation. The test is not intensity. The test is fruit over time, and the fruit is love, not leverage. Fifth, the free will and sovereignty tension I raised in the conversation is not a philosophical puzzle for seminary classrooms. It has direct practical implications for how decisions are made under pressure. If I believe that God is sovereign *and* that my choices are genuinely real, I am neither fatalistic (“it will happen if it’s meant to be”) nor anxious (“it all depends on me getting this right”). I am positioned as a genuine steward — one who plans, acts, builds, and remains genuinely surrenderable to divine redirection. That posture produces a particular peace that neither pure determinism nor pure autonomy can generate — the εἰρήνη (eirēnē, ay-RAY-nay) of Philippians 4:7, translated “peace,” meaning wholeness, settled well-being — available precisely in the tension of what has not yet been resolved. The answer is not a framework to be constructed but a covenant reality to be received. Finally, my admission during the conversation that I have only read perhaps half of the full text of Scripture — particularly in the Old Testament — is not a confession to be filed away. It is an invitation. The Old Testament contains the full weight of covenant theology, land theology, Jubilee economics, prophetic economics, and the entire typological framework that makes the New Testament intelligible. The parable of the hidden treasure cannot be fully heard without Leviticus 25. The question of sovereignty and free will cannot be fully held without the prophets. The wisdom about field-preparation cannot be fully applied without Proverbs read in its Hebrew wisdom context. The whole land must be walked. There is treasure buried in the portions of the canon I have not yet crossed, and the work of reading, studying, and abiding in the full text is itself a form of field-buying — a slow and patient labor that produces the kind of understanding no shortcut can imitate. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity in this season means learning to read formation seasons correctly — to see two years of incomplete outcomes not as failure but as curriculum, not as divine silence but as active shaping. Maturity asks not only “did this work?” but “what did this produce in me that would not otherwise exist?” And maturity also means developing what I can only call the posture of the long-view steward — one who does not gamble the field for the treasure but learns to love the land itself because God has called it good. This means learning to hold the tension between genuine human agency and sovereign divine governance without collapsing into either fatalism or anxiety. It means allowing the question of sovereignty and free will to be a formation tool rather than a frustration, and trusting that the Spirit will reveal what we need to see through the means He chooses — including conversations with brothers about real estate that suddenly become theological excavations of the nature of God. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Kingdom obedience in the area of stewardship means refusing the false piety that treats competence, strategy, and multiplied return as spiritually suspect — and equally refusing the false spirituality that dresses ambition in Kingdom language without submitting it to genuine examination. It means stewarding every layer of every assignment with the same faithfulness: not just the visible parts, but the contractor relationships, the referral agreements, the management structures, the long-term network-building. It means bringing the Lord *into* the planning, not merely presenting Him with the plan after the fact and calling it prayer. And for those with financial and entrepreneurial capacity, it is the call to submit that capacity to Kingdom purposes rather than using Kingdom language to sanctify personal empire-building. The call is to genuine integration: stewardship as worship, planning as prayer, building as discipleship. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation cultivates is one of watchful engagement — present in the financial and technological spheres of the age, literate in their language and systems, but never assimilated into their values. We are to name the Mammon architecture clearly and publicly, begin building and championing Kingdom alternatives, and maintain the prophetic clarity to see both the redemptive threads God is weaving through human suffering and the exploitative systems He is calling His people out of. For those who carry prophetic gifting — or who have experienced that gift being mishandled in their own hands — the corrective posture is not silence but submission. The prophetic is not leverage. It is not a tool for constructing our own futures. It is a window through which we see what the Father is already doing and join Him there. We are servants of the Word, not its architects. And we abide in Him precisely so that we can remain in the world without being of it — seeing what others cannot see, naming what others will not name, and building what the age does not yet know it needs. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one of Your servants — a co-heir of Your Kingdom, covered by the blood of Jesus Christ and empowered by Your Spirit — and I bring this entire conversation, this unfolding of strategy and formation and theological excavation, back to You as an offering. You were in the two years. You were in the incomplete seasons and the deals that didn’t fully capitalize. You were in the laughter, in the recognition, in the cosmological turn that arrived mid-sentence and opened into Colossians 1:17 — the declaration that the Son is actively holding all things together at this moment, including the things that feel like they are falling apart. I thank You for the brother You gave me in this walk, and I ask that every layer of margin he builds would be offered back to You in proportion — that the multiplication would not terminate in comfort but would become fuel for Your purposes. I ask that You would keep us from the idolatry of our own cleverness, that we would remain men who know the increase comes from You alone. I receive the conviction You pressed in me during this conversation about the systems being built to harvest souls — and I agree with Your call, Lord: we choose You over Mammon, we choose the alternative over the exploitation, we choose the field over the mere extraction of the treasure. Continue the deep work You have begun in my brother — that the focus born from his breaking would be submitted daily to Your lordship, that the vision growing in him would be purified by Your fire, and that every resource he ever touches would carry the fingerprint of the Kingdom rather than the stamp of Babylon. I intercede for every believer in a wilderness season right now — who cannot yet see that the breaking is surgical, not punitive, that the nishbar is the precondition of fruitfulness, that they are not off the path but may be closer to the burning bush than they have ever been. Let nothing be buried in the ground out of fear. Let every talent be put to work for Your glory and the flourishing of others. And let this kind of thinking — multi-layered, infrastructure-building, long-horizon, genuinely surrendered — become the standard by which Your people approach stewardship of every resource You place in our hands: time, talent, treasure, relationship, and revelation. In the name of Jesus Christ, the faithful and true Steward of all things, the One who purchased the field at the cost of everything He had, and in whom all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden — Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Matthew 13:44 — “The kingdom of heaven is like treasure hidden in a field, which a man found and hid; and for joy over it he goes and sells all that he has and buys that field.” | — | The man purchases the field, not merely the treasure — becoming the steward of the land that contains the treasure.
