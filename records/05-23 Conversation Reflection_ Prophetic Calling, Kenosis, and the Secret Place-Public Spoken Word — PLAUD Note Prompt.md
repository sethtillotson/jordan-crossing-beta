# 05-19 Call Reflection: Spiritual Insight — Sabbath Rest, Blind Men, and the Grammar of Grace

**Title:** 05-19 at 15:10 — When Pain Wears a Costume: On Sabbath Rest, Blind Men, and the Grammar of Grace

**Recorded:** 2026-05-19 15:10:24

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture / Phone or Video Call)

**Classification:** Call Reflection

## Opening Observation

As I listened to my brother navigate the ordinary chaos of his evening — clocking out sick from a food factory, settling into an unplanned night of rest — something much less ordinary broke through the surface of the conversation. In the middle of casualness and laughter, my brother arrived at a revelation that most men chase for years without catching: the person who hurt him is not an enemy to be defeated but a wounded soul in desperate disguise. That moment of clarity — unprompted, unforced, arriving like light through a cracked door — stopped me in my tracks. I remember saying to him, *blessed are you, for this revelation is not given by man but by the Father*, and I meant it with everything in me.

**Why This Matters:**

The ability to perceive another person’s pain beneath their behavior is not merely emotional intelligence or psychological sophistication — it is a fruit of transformation that the natural mind cannot manufacture. When the Body of Christ learns to look at provocation and see suffering, at aggression and see wound, at performance and see plea, it begins to inhabit the same posture Christ himself occupied toward every sinner he encountered. This matters for discipleship because it determines whether we become people of retribution or people of redemption. It matters for community because it is the difference between a fellowship that defends itself from the broken and one that becomes the very hands and feet of the Healer. And it matters for endurance because the person who understands that acting out is a sign of inward pain will not be as easily baited into cycles of offense and retaliation that drain the life from Kingdom relationships.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

From a purely natural vantage point, this conversation reads as two friends catching up across time zones — one recovering from a sick day, the other on a work break — and stumbling into a few moments of accidental depth before the talk drifts toward fishing trips, chipmunks, and tropical beaches. The natural mind would register my brother’s realization about Emily as personal growth, the kind therapists celebrate and self-help books promise. It would categorize Seth’s framework shift from *have to* to *get to* as cognitive behavioral reframing, borrowed perhaps from a podcast or a motivational corner of the internet. The blind man illustration might read as a clever analogy deployed in friendly conversation. Even the mention of the Sabbath rest might sound like a metaphor for emotional equilibrium — the kind of peace that comes not from God but from distance and time.

**The Limitation:**

What the natural mind cannot perceive is that these moments were not the byproduct of personal growth techniques or emotional maturity alone — they were the residue of genuine encounter with the living God pressing through ordinary speech. The mind that has not been renewed by the Spirit will always reduce spiritual insight to psychological process, always flatten Kingdom revelation into human achievement, and always miss the thread of divine orchestration running through what appears to be accident. There is something the unrenewed mind structurally cannot receive: that God is already at work in the conversation before either participant is aware of it, that the Father is speaking through the cracked-open moments of a Tuesday phone call just as sovereignly as he speaks through a Sunday sermon, and that what looks like two men killing time is, to the eyes of the Spirit, the multiplication of light.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

What arrests me first, sitting with this conversation now, is the nature of the moment my brother described. He was about to get angry. That trajectory was set. The emotional freight of offense was already in motion. And then something interrupted it — not willpower, not discipline, not a breathing exercise — but a thought that landed differently than his own thoughts usually do. *If she wasn’t hurt, she wouldn’t be going to that length.* I find myself recognizing in that the grammar of divine illumination, the kind that arrives as a gift rather than a conclusion. James writes that every good and perfect gift descends from the Father of lights (James 1:17), and I am convinced that includes the sudden capacity to see another person’s pain when your own pain is still very much present and very much real.

This is not a small thing. Mercy that costs nothing is easy. The mercy my brother extended in that moment — the decision not to answer provocation with provocation — came at a real price. And yet the Father met him there. I think of the way Jesus looked at the crowds in Matthew 9:36, that the text says he was moved with compassion because he saw them as sheep without a shepherd — harassed, scattered, hurting. The Greek word behind *moved with compassion* is σπλαγχνίζομαι (splanchnizomai, splonk-NIDGE-oh-my) — a visceral, gut-level, almost involuntary response of the inner organs to someone else’s suffering. This was not a policy Christ had about the crowds. This was a perception of their condition that produced an inward movement. What my brother experienced in that moment, I believe, was the beginning of that same movement taking root in him.

There is a maturing happening in my brother that I want to name carefully, because it is easy to mistake spiritual growth for emotional detachment, and these are not the same thing. When he described the season with Emily as something that gave him XP — experience points in the language of gaming — I heard underneath that metaphor something genuinely theological: the sense that suffering received rightly becomes a form of inheritance. This echoes what Paul says in Romans 5, where he traces a line of becoming: tribulation produces perseverance, perseverance character, character hope (Romans 5:3-4). The progression is not accidental or automatic — it requires that suffering pass through the refiner rather than simply accumulate. My brother is beginning to understand that pain, when surrendered rather than merely endured, becomes equipment for the journey ahead.

I pressed into this during the call, and I want to press into it further now. I offered him a distinction between the framework I used to carry in my secular days — the reframe of *have to* into *get to*, of obligation into opportunity — and the theological ground that now underlies it. The shift from *this is happening to me* to *this is happening for me* is not merely a mental technique. It carries real doctrinal weight. The natural framing of *why me?* and *what did I do to deserve this?* assumes a transactional universe — a cosmos where suffering is always punishment and ease is always reward. That is not the Gospel. That is the old covenant read without Christ, or rather, it is the distortion of even the old covenant that Israel herself kept falling into, and that the Pharisees institutionalized.

This is precisely where the blind man in John 9 becomes so luminous. The disciples asked Jesus, *who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?* (John 9:2). The question is born from that transactional universe — suffering as verdict, pain as consequence. Jesus refuses the entire framework. He says, *neither this man nor his parents sinned, but that the works of God should be revealed in him* (John 9:3). The blindness was not punishment. It was a canvas. The man’s whole life — every morning of darkness, every year of dependence, every humiliation at the gate — was being stewarded toward a single unrepeatable moment of revelation. And when the man was hauled before the Pharisees afterward, the most spiritually sophisticated religious men of his age, his testimony was unassailable in its simplicity: *I was blind, I washed, and I see* (John 9:11). He did not need a theology degree. He had a story. And that story was its own witness.

The theological leap I want to make here — and I believe the Spirit is pressing me toward it — is that when we begin to inhabit the framework of *for me* rather than *to me*, we do not ultimately arrive at a comfortable place where everything serves our own flourishing. We arrive at something larger and more costly than that. We arrive at the possibility that what is happening in my life, what is happening for me, may actually be happening through me for someone I have not yet met. Paul captures this in 2 Corinthians 1:4, where he writes that God comforts us in all our tribulation, that we may be able to comfort those who are in any trouble, with the comfort with which we ourselves are comforted by God. The comfort is not a terminus. It is a transmission. We are comforted so that comfort may flow through us. The blind man’s story did not end with his restored sight — it became the occasion for a theological confrontation that revealed the spiritual blindness of those who could see perfectly well.

I want to stay here for a moment, because this is where *it’s happening for me* becomes something even more expansive than personal peace. Romans 8:28 is often quoted — *all things work together for good to those who love God, to those who are the called according to His purpose* — but it is frequently received as a promise of personal comfort rather than a declaration of sovereign purpose. The Greek word behind *work together* is συνεργεῖ (synergei, sin-ER-gay) — it carries the sense of a cooperative energy, things brought together in coordinated movement toward a single end. All things, including the woman who acted out in pain, including the sick day that cleared the schedule, including the late night phone call at two-thirty in the morning — συνεργεῖ (sin-ER-gay). All of it is being drawn, by a hand I cannot always see, toward a goodness I cannot always yet name.

My brother also touched something when he used the language of Sabbath rest, even if he did not use those exact words. The internal shift he is experiencing — the settling, the refusal to be destabilized by what someone else is doing around him — this is related to what the writer of Hebrews calls entering the rest (Hebrews 4:1-11). The Greek term there is κατάπαυσις (katapausis, kah-tah-POW-sis) — a cessation, a resting down, a laying down of the striving. The Sabbath rest available to the people of God is not an absence of activity but an absence of anxious striving — a settled confidence that the One who holds the outcome does not need us to manage it by worry or retaliation. My brother, high on a Tuesday evening and staring down a situation that could have produced real anger, chose rest instead. That is not nothing. That is the beginning of a man entering into something ancient and holy.

What I also notice, and I want to name this tenderly, is that the conversation did not stay elevated. It moved naturally from the blind man in John 9 to fishing logistics, to car batteries, to chipmunks eating out of my grandfather’s hand on a Minnesota lake. And I do not think that is a failure of focus or a dissipation of the Spirit’s work. I think that is the nature of genuine spiritual community among men who are still embodied, still living ordinary lives, still showing up to food factories and work breaks and plans for summer that are many months away. The holy does not require us to remain in a register of sustained intensity. It visits us in the ordinary, plants something, and then trusts us to carry it back into the ordinariness that follows.

There is something almost sacramental about my grandfather feeding chipmunks by hand every day, for years, until generations of wild creatures learned that humans were a source of provision rather than threat. I mentioned it in passing, almost as a travel anecdote. But as I sit with it now, I feel the Spirit drawing something out of it. Discipleship — real, patient, unglamorous, daily discipleship — changes the environment around us in ways we do not always live to see. My grandfather is gone. But the chipmunks still come. Something of his faithfulness to a small, daily practice outlasted him. Is that not what the Kingdom looks like? Is that not what we are building, every phone call, every revelation, every decision to see pain behind performance rather than responding with anger of our own?

The conversation ended quietly — I was back at work, my brother was managing his battery life, and we said our goodbyes with ease. But I carried something into the rest of my shift. The Father was speaking in that call. Not in spite of the randomness and the laughter and the language of two men who love each other without pretense — but through all of it. The summary says we were just talking. The note says the Spirit was knitting something together across an ocean, across time zones, in the middle of an ordinary evening, and that what was planted in that call will grow in ways neither of us can fully calculate yet.

## Implications and Challenges

There is a deeply embedded assumption in much of contemporary Christian culture that spiritual depth only happens in dedicated, structured environments — in services, in sermon series, in organized Bible studies. This call challenges that assumption directly. The revelation my brother received was not delivered by a preacher or pulled from a study guide. It arrived in a moment of stillness before anger took over. We must cultivate the kind of interior openness that makes us receivable vessels at two-thirty in the morning on a Tuesday, not only on Sunday mornings when we are prepared to receive.

The framework of *it’s happening to me* versus *it’s happening for me* is often dismissed as positive thinking when encountered outside explicitly theological language. But dismissing the theological framework underneath it is equally dangerous. The church must not simply borrow the language of resilience and reframing from secular psychology without grounding it in the actual witness of Scripture — the God who works all things together, the blind man whose whole life was an unrealized canvas, the comforted becoming comforters. Psychological reframing without theological grounding eventually runs out of resources. The Gospel does not.

There is also a challenge here for how we handle those who hurt us within the Body of Christ. My brother’s realization — that the behavior he was observing was a facade for real pain — is precisely the kind of perception that could transform Christian community if it were widely practiced. Instead, much of what passes for accountability in modern church culture is more interested in behavior management than in the wound beneath the behavior. Grace-filled discernment asks not only *what did you do?* but *what happened to you?* Both questions matter. Only the second one opens a door to actual healing.

The temptation to reduce suffering to consequence and ease to reward is not only a secular error — it lives inside much of the prosperity-adjacent theology that still circulates widely in Western Christianity. John 9 is a direct confrontation with that framework. Neither this man nor his parents sinned. The theological violence done by telling a suffering person that they must have done something to deserve their condition is not a minor pastoral misstep — it is a fundamental misreading of how the God revealed in Jesus Christ relates to human pain. We must be willing to preach and practice a theology of suffering that is robust enough to hold John 9 without flinching.

Finally, there is something to be said about the value of ordinary, undramatized Christian friendship between men. This call was not a formal discipleship session. There was no agenda, no structured accountability, no prayer of commitment at the end. And yet real things happened. Real revelation moved. Real encouragement was given and received. The church must stop treating only the programmatic as spiritually significant. The Kingdom moves through phone calls and fishing trips and campfire conversations and chipmunks eating from an old man’s hand. We must make room for those things and trust that the Spirit is working in them as surely as he works in anything else we might design.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

The kind of spiritual maturity being seeded in this conversation is not the maturity of doctrinal comprehensiveness or ministry accomplishment — it is the maturity of a person who can hold their own pain without weaponizing it against someone else’s pain. It is the maturity of someone who, in the moment before anger, finds the eyes to see. Growing into this requires us to stop treating emotional reactivity as something to manage and start treating it as a diagnostic — a signal that we are still operating from a place of unhealed wound rather than settled inheritance. The Father’s invitation is to rest, to see, to receive the situation as canvas rather than verdict.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

The obedience this calls forth is not dramatic. It is the daily decision to frame life theologically — to ask not *why is this happening to me* but *what is the Father doing through this, and who else might need what I am learning here?* It is also the obedience of remaining present to ordinary friendship, of answering the late phone call, of being available for the Spirit to move through casual conversation. Kingdom obedience often looks less like strategic ministry and more like faithful, unhurried presence with the people God places in our lives.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

The prophetic posture this meditation trains us toward is one of perceiving — seeing beneath the surface of behavior and circumstance to the deeper current of what God is doing and what the enemy is attempting. The person who can see pain beneath performance, who can see the canvas beneath the suffering, who can see the transmission of comfort within their own comfort — that person becomes a prophetic witness in every room they enter. We are being trained not just to receive revelation but to carry it, to let what the Father shows us become the substance of how we treat the people around us.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I come before You as one of Your servants, a co-heir of the Kingdom You are building across time zones and telephone connections and cold Minnesota lakes, as one standing between the heavens and the earth in agreement with Your will and Your purposes — and I give You thanks for a phone call that became a classroom, for a brother who stopped before anger and heard something You were saying instead, for the revelation that pain wears costumes and mercy is the only thing that can see through them. I ask You now to seal what was spoken in that call, to water the seed of rest that is taking root in my brother’s life, to make him a man who carries the testimony of the blind man — simple, unassailable, born entirely from personal encounter with You. Let what You began in him continue. Let what You pressed through our conversation multiply into the people we have not yet met who will need to hear it. Let the framework of *for me* and *through me* and *for others* become the grammar of how we both read our lives from this day forward. And Lord, for all of us who read this now — those who are still operating from *why me*, still reading suffering as punishment, still responding to pain with pain — let this be an interruption. Let the eyes of our understanding be enlightened, as Paul prayed, that we may know the hope of our calling and the riches of the glory of our inheritance in the saints. You are the Father of lights, and every good and perfect gift descends from You — including the ones that arrive at two-thirty in the morning, unscheduled and unannounced, through the cracked-open door of an ordinary conversation. To You be all glory, in this age and in the age to come. Amen.
