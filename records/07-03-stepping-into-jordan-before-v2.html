# 07-03 Call Reflection: Kingdom Obedience, Discipleship, and the Jordan Principle **Title:** 07-03 at 06:59 — Stepping Into the Jordan Before the Water Parts **Recorded:** 2026-07-03 06:59:37 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to this conversation with my brother Samuel unfold, one phrase rose above the noise of business strategy, AI tools, and economic analysis and arrested me completely: *we need people willing to step into the Jordan.* It was a passing remark, almost casual in delivery, but it carried the full weight of a Kingdom principle that refuses to stay buried beneath the surface of practical conversation. The tension between willingness and readiness, between preparation and actual obedience, between knowing what must be done and actually doing the gritty, unsexy work — this is not merely a business problem. It is a discipleship problem, and I find myself sitting with it long after the call ended. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ in this generation is saturated with people who are, in a sense, *conceptually all in* — they have attended the training, received the vision, affirmed the mission, and declared their readiness — yet when the monotonous, unglamorous labor of the Kingdom presents itself, they grow bored and withdraw. This is not a peripheral issue of work ethic or personality temperament. It cuts to the very root of what it means to follow a crucified Messiah who did not find the way of the cross glamorous, who did not wait for sufficient evidence before walking it, and who entered the waters of baptism before the voice of the Father had even finished speaking. If we cannot instill in ourselves and in those we lead a willingness to do what is dull, difficult, and presently unrewarded, we will never see the river open. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the perspective of natural human reasoning, the dynamics of this conversation are straightforwardly about organizational management and motivational theory. Kane is unmotivated because the reward cycle is too long and the task too tedious — a standard challenge in any sales or operations environment. The solution, the world would suggest, is restructuring incentives, shortening feedback loops, gamifying the workflow, or simply replacing him with someone more suited to grinding. The broader conversations about the Philippine educational software market, AI tool optimization, and business strategy are simply entrepreneurial brainstorming between partners trying to identify leverage points and growth vectors in an evolving technological economy. These are rational, practical, modern concerns, and from a purely horizontal vantage point, the analysis is thorough and even sophisticated. **The Limitation:** Yet the natural mind, however shrewd, operates within a closed system — one that can calculate inputs and outputs but cannot perceive the invisible architecture beneath them. It sees Kane’s boredom as a management challenge rather than a mirror. It sees the Philippine market gap as an economic opportunity rather than a stewardship moment. And it certainly cannot hear what the Spirit was doing in the moment when the words *step into the Jordan* arrived unbidden into a conversation about Instagram outreach and setter teams. The flesh evaluates by results already visible; the Spirit calls into motion before the evidence has been laid out. To the natural mind, stepping into an uncrossed river is poor risk management. To the Kingdom mind, it is the only thing that has ever moved the waters. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I am struck, first, by the almost involuntary nature of that Jordan reference in my own words. My brother Samuel and I were deep in the weeds of operational reality — calendars, ad spend, setter teams, the slow grind of building something from nothing — and somewhere in the middle of it all, the Spirit surfaced a word that reached far beyond the immediate context. Joshua 3:13 does not record a moment of ideal conditions. The Jordan was at flood stage. The priests did not step in once the waters receded; the waters did not recede until the soles of their feet were wet. This is the architecture of Kingdom movement: obedience precedes provision, and action precedes confirmation. What I recognize in my brother Kane, and if I am honest, in myself at various seasons, is the very ancient temptation to require a visible return before committing invisible labor. He wants to see something before he goes harder. This sounds reasonable on the surface — it has the appearance of wisdom, even prudence. But when I trace this posture through Scripture, I find it again and again in the wrong column. The ten spies who returned from Canaan with a bad report were not lying about the giants. They were simply evaluating by sight rather than by the word already spoken. Numbers 13:31 records their conclusion: *“We are not able to go up against the people, for they are stronger than we.”* They had surveyed the land accurately. They had simply forgotten who commissioned the survey. The contrast Joshua and Caleb offer is not that they saw a different land — they saw the same land, the same giants, the same walled cities. What differed was the authority they were standing under when they looked. Caleb’s famous declaration in Numbers 13:30 — *“Let us go up at once and take possession”* — was not optimism. It was obedience anchored in a covenant word already spoken. This is the difference between someone willing to step into the Jordan and someone waiting at the bank for better conditions. And as I listened to my brother Samuel articulate this with such clarity, I felt the Spirit underline it for me personally, not just as a leadership observation but as a personal recalibration. There is a Greek word that presses on me here: ὑπακοή (hypakoē, hoo-pah-koh-AY) — translated *obedience*, but carrying the root of ἀκούω (*to hear*). True obedience in the Kingdom is always an act of hearing first. It is not blind mechanical compliance; it is the posture of one who has genuinely heard the Commander’s voice and then moves. The problem with waiting for visible results before investing invisible effort is that it subtly repositions the Word of God as a hypothesis to be tested rather than a command to be obeyed. Kane’s hesitation — as my brother Samuel described it — is not a character flaw as much as it is an untrained ear. He has not yet learned to move on the word before the water moves. This pattern of word-before-water runs through the entire canon like a golden thread. Abram departed from Ur without knowing where he was going (Hebrews 11:8). Noah built an ark before a drop of rain had fallen. Elijah’s servant ran seven times before the cloud the size of a man’s hand appeared. In every instance, the labor that looked foolish from the outside was the very labor that positioned the servant to witness what God had already promised. The gritty, repetitive work — the Instagram messages, the cold outreach, the unglamorous backend tasks — these are not obstacles to the breakthrough. They are the riverbank from which the priest descends into the flood. I also cannot dismiss what the Spirit is pressing through the broader conversation about the Philippines. What my brother Samuel and I were discussing — an educational platform designed specifically to fit the cultural, economic, and governmental architecture of a people on the threshold of economic emergence — carries a weight that transcends entrepreneurial ambition. Proverbs 31:8 calls us to *“open your mouth for the speechless, in the cause of all who are appointed to die”* — and while that passage carries prophetic depth beyond market analysis, there is something in the Spirit’s economy about being positioned at the precise inflection point where a people’s need and our capacity overlap. The Philippines is not merely a market gap. It is a people group in transition, and if we approach that stewardship with Kingdom eyes rather than purely economic ones, the question changes from *what is the ROI?* to *what are we being trusted with?* The conversation about certificates matters more than it appears on the surface. In the Philippines, my brother Samuel and I noted, a certificate is not merely a credential — it is a cultural marker of dignity, effort, and upward movement. It is, in a sense, a tangible testimony that labor has been honored. I hear an echo here of the Hebrew concept of כָּבוֹד (kabod, kah-VODE) — glory, weight, honor. Every person in that nation who posts a certificate on their wall is declaring, in their own cultural idiom, that their labor has been recognized and counted. A platform that honors that longing is not merely a software product; it is, if built with the right spirit, a vehicle for כָּבוֹד (kah-VODE) to move through ordinary people’s ordinary lives. The AI and tool discussion — Perplexity, GenSpark, Comma, the automation pipelines — also carries a subtle but real lesson. My brother Samuel and I both noted that these tools, as powerful as they are, are only as effective as the operator’s capacity to direct them. An untrained user with a powerful tool is not necessarily more productive; they may simply make mistakes faster. I hear in this a parable of spiritual gifting: the gifts of the Spirit given to an untrained and unformed character do not automatically produce Kingdom fruit. They can produce noise, confusion, and even harm when operated from a place of immaturity. Paul’s entire argument in 1 Corinthians 13 rests on this very tension — the gifts are real, but without love, which is the fruit of formation and obedience over time, they amount to *“sounding brass or a clanging cymbal”* (1 Corinthians 13:1). The gritty work of character formation is, in this sense, the same as the gritty work of learning to prompt an AI correctly: it cannot be skipped and still produce the intended result. I find myself returning, at the close of this reflection, to the image of my wife carrying what is, at this stage, described simply as a little sea horse. There is something about that image — that tiny, forming life, not yet large enough to ride carnival rides with, not yet visible to the world in any dramatic way — that quietly preaches the entire sermon. Every great thing begins invisible, small, unglamorous, and requiring faithful tending before any evidence of its significance appears. The Jordan does not open to those who wait for it to appear shallow. It opens to those who are willing, in the flood season, to get their feet wet. The phrase my brother Samuel offered near the end of our call carries its own quiet gravity: *“It sounds like the best plan because it’s the only plan we got.”* There is a certain holy sobriety in that statement when filtered through a Kingdom lens. The servant of God is not always afforded multiple attractive options. Sometimes faithfulness simply looks like: this is what is in front of me, these are the people I am trusted with, this is the work that is available, and I will do it. Not because the conditions are ideal, but because the One who called me into it has already accounted for the flood season. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation presses upon me is the danger of *conceptual commitment without incarnated obedience*. Modern discipleship environments — and modern business environments, for that matter — are extraordinarily good at producing people who are articulate about vision and deeply resistant to the mundane labor that vision requires. We must examine ourselves honestly: in what areas am I waiting at the riverbank, dressed in the priestly garments of readiness, but unwilling to wet my feet? The second challenge concerns how we mentor and develop those entrusted to our leadership. My instinct, and my brother Samuel’s as well, was to keep Kane motivated through books and incremental wins while building toward the paper call offer that might ignite his enthusiasm. This is wise practically, but the spiritual question is deeper: are we merely managing his motivation, or are we genuinely forming his character? Motivation management is a short-term solution. Character formation is the long-term work of discipleship, and it cannot be replaced by better incentive structures. The third challenge is one of stewardship and calling. The Philippine platform idea — which emerged not from my ambition but from a partner’s need — carries a question about what we are genuinely being trusted to build and for whom. There is always a temptation to evaluate Kingdom opportunities exclusively through the lens of financial sustainability. Sustainability matters, but it must not become the first question. The first question is: *is this something I am being called to steward?* And if it is, the resources will follow the obedience, not precede it. The fourth challenge touches the way we integrate powerful tools — including AI — into our work and our worship without allowing them to become substitutes for the irreplaceable work of presence, relationship, and formed character. My brother Samuel and I were both clear that untrained users misuse powerful tools. The same is profoundly true of spiritual gifts, scriptural knowledge, and theological vocabulary when they are not anchored in humble, obedient, daily formation. Finally, there is a grace-filled but necessary word about the temptation to evaluate spiritual progress — or business progress — by the speed at which things become visible. The sea horse grows in the hidden place. The flood waters of the Jordan are exactly the conditions under which the miracle is about to happen. We must resist the counsel of our own impatience and trust the faithfulness of the One who set the timetable. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This meditation calls me — and all who carry vision — to honestly inventory the gap between what I am willing to declare and what I am willing to do. Maturity in the Kingdom is not measured by the sophistication of one’s revelation but by the faithfulness of one’s obedience in the ordinary, unrewarded moments. The priests had to carry the ark to the water’s edge and then keep walking. Maturity is learning to keep walking past the point where it still feels safe. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The immediate posture this calls forth is simple, concrete, and unglamorous: do the next available task with full commitment, regardless of whether visible fruit has confirmed the investment yet. For those in positions of leadership and development of others, it means building cultures of character formation rather than merely cultures of motivation management. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic community must model the willingness to step before the confirmation arrives. We are not called to prophesy what is already obvious; we are called to align ourselves with what God has already spoken and then move in agreement with that word. This means cultivating a tolerance for the in-between season — the flood stage, the invisible formation, the unglamorous outreach — without losing the anchor of the promise. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who knows what it is to stand at the riverbank and feel the water rising, who knows the pull of waiting for conditions that may never arrive before the river must be crossed. I thank You that the command to go up and possess was not a suggestion contingent upon favorable terrain, and I thank You that the priests’ feet touching the flood did not need to be accompanied by prior evidence that the water would move. I ask You, Holy Spirit, to press this truth deeper into my own bones and into the bones of every brother and sister who will read this word — that willingness formed in hiddenness is the very thing You honor with open waters. For the little life growing in the hidden place, for the platform in the nation on the threshold of emergence, for the teammates who need character more than they need motivation, and for every area in my own life where I have been dressed for Jordan but standing on the bank — I make agreement with what You have already spoken. Let it be done on earth as it is in heaven. Let those who carry the covenant step into the flood. And let the waters part at the soles of their obedience. To the One who calls water out of stone and armies out of shepherds, be all glory, honor, and authority.
