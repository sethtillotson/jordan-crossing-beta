# 07-17 Personal Meditation: Intercession, Entitlement, and the Priestly Reflex in Crisis **Title:** 07-17 at 08:06 — When Smoke Fills the Sky, What Rises From Our Hearts? **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** A — Personal Spoken-Word Revelation **Classification:** Personal Meditation ## Opening Observation Something grieved my spirit when I saw that reel — not the smoke itself, not the fire, not even the suffering, but the caption beneath it: *“Should Canada compensate us for this?”* That single question exposed something deep and uncomfortable about where we are as a people. When the first posture of the heart in the face of disaster is demand rather than intercession, something has gone severely wrong — and I cannot let that pass without sitting with it before the Lord. **Why This Matters:** The reflex of a heart tells you who that heart belongs to. If the first impulse when suffering arrives is to locate someone to blame and extract payment from, that is not merely a cultural inconvenience or a political frustration — it is a spiritual diagnostic. The Body of Christ lives embedded in a Western culture that has perfected the theology of entitlement, and if we are not careful, that culture will disciple us far more quietly and thoroughly than we realize. What rises from us in the moment of pain — complaint, demand, prayer, compassion, intercession — that rising is the truest measure of what the Spirit has actually been cultivating inside us. This meditation is an invitation to let the smoke become a mirror. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the vantage point of natural reasoning, the question on that caption is not only understandable, it feels reasonable. Wildfires from a neighboring country producing hazardous air quality that crosses borders, harms lungs, disrupts livelihoods, and affects vulnerable populations — legally, economically, and politically, questions of responsibility and remedy are entirely within the scope of international relations and civil discourse. The natural mind operates within a framework of cause and effect, rights and damages, harm and remedy. It is the language of courts and contracts, of insurance policies and liability claims. From that framework, asking “who pays?” is simply pragmatic risk management dressed up in civic clothing. There is nothing remarkable about it to the natural mind — it is, in fact, exactly what the natural mind was designed to produce when it processes injury. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind, for all its reasonable efficiency, cannot perceive the moment of crisis as a threshold of the soul. It cannot read a wildfire as a test of the heart. It cannot hear in the acrid air an invitation to intercession. The Spirit sees what the calculating mind cannot — that between the injury and the response lies a sacred pause, and what a person does in that pause reveals everything. The natural mind moves swiftly from wound to lawsuit because it has no category for the alternative: standing before God in the smoke and asking not *who owes me* but *Lord, what are You saying, and what do You want me to do?* That second question requires something the natural mind simply does not possess on its own — a living awareness of a God who is present, who speaks, who tests, and who heals. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I keep returning to the word *grieved*. I did not merely disagree with that caption intellectually. Something in me was grieved — and I want to be careful about that word, because grief in the Spirit is not the same as mere emotional discomfort. When the רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — the Spirit, the breath, the wind of God — is grieved, something has touched a fault line that runs much deeper than opinion. Paul warns the Ephesians not to grieve the Holy Spirit of God, and the context is not doctrinal error but rather the condition of the mouth, the heart, and the posture toward one another in the community of believers. My spirit recognized in that caption a posture that belongs to a world that does not know God, showing up with casual confidence in spaces where the people of God scroll and consume without discernment. That grief is itself a kind of prophetic signal — a signal that I must not dismiss or explain away. I think about Job. I actually mentioned Job when I was processing this aloud, and I want to stay there for a moment because the Spirit does not bring things to our memory by accident. At the end of the book of Job, God does require Job’s three friends — Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar — to bring burnt offerings and to have Job pray on their behalf, because they had not spoken rightly of God as His servant Job had. This is a form of divinely ordered compensation: a theological debt was incurred, a theological remedy was required. There is nothing wrong with just compensation in its proper place and season. God Himself administers it. But here is what arrests me in the Job narrative — the three friends were not malicious. They were *theologically correct in many of their propositions* while being utterly wrong in how they applied those propositions to the character of God. They spoke *about* God rightly in one sense and wrongly in the deepest sense. And what did they have to do to be restored? They could not simply pay a fine and walk away clean. They needed the intercession of the very man they had wounded with their words. Compensation without intercession is incomplete even in the divine economy. The remedy required a mediator. This presses something into me about the order of things. Compensation, when it is just, follows a process — but that process, even in God’s own design, runs through intercession. Job prayed for his friends, and *then* the Lord restored Job’s losses and doubled them. The sequence matters. Intercession precedes restoration. The instinct to demand first and pray later — or worse, never to pray at all — inverts the order of the Kingdom. We are reaching for the end of the story before we have taken our place in the middle of it as priests. The wildfire itself is worth sitting with. Fire in Scripture is almost never merely an inconvenience. From the burning bush in Exodus to the pillar of fire in the wilderness, from Elijah’s fire on Carmel to the tongues of fire at Pentecost, fire carries divine weight. I am not standing here declaring that this specific wildfire is a specific judgment — I must be careful not to project prophecy beyond what I actually perceive. But I am saying this: when I sit with the possibility that an act of natural scale could be a divine test of the hearts of nations, I find that possibility entirely consistent with the Scripture I know. God tested Israel in the wilderness not to destroy them but to know what was in their hearts, as Moses explains in Deuteronomy 8 — to humble them, to reveal the content of their character, to establish whether they would keep His commandments or not. The wilderness, the manna, the thirst, the serpents — all of it was diagnostic. Why would we assume the living God has retired from the business of testing hearts through the conditions of creation? And the smoke — the air itself becoming hazardous — carries its own weight as a scriptural image. The Psalms speak of the nations as smoke that vanishes, of the wicked fading like smoke before wind. Isaiah speaks of a people whose worship had become smoke in God’s nostrils. Revelation is saturated with smoke rising from altars, from the abyss, from the bowls of wrath. I am not mechanically mapping those images onto Canadian wildfires. But I am saying that when the air you breathe becomes dangerous, when the invisible substance that sustains your every moment is compromised, something in the created order is bearing witness to a spiritual condition that the visible world alone cannot articulate. The Spirit presses me to pay attention even here. My first response ought to be prayer — and I want to be honest that I said this plainly when I was recording my thoughts, almost matter-of-factly: *I know my God, He could clear this whole wildfire in five minutes. All the smoke gone.* That is not boasting. That is the simple confidence of a son who knows his Father’s power. But notice what that confidence does: it strips away the presumption that the problem is primarily political, primarily Canada’s responsibility, primarily a matter of international environmental liability. If God can clear it in five minutes, then the real question is not about Canada at all — the real question is about the condition of the heart that stands before that God in the moment of smoke. Do I go to Him? Do I ask Him? Do I trust that He is present and capable and interested? Or do I scroll past the reel leaving a comment about reparations and feel that I have discharged my civic duty? There is a posture the Scripture calls for that is deeply countercultural in the West, and it is simply this: intercession as the first and natural instinct of the righteous. Abraham interceded for Sodom. Moses interceded for Israel at Sinai when God’s wrath was kindled. Nehemiah’s first act upon hearing that Jerusalem’s walls were broken was not to file a complaint against the Babylonian Empire — he sat down, wept, fasted, and prayed before the God of heaven. Daniel, in the face of national captivity and spiritual desolation, turned his face toward Jerusalem and interceded. These were not passive men. They were not men who had surrendered the category of justice. But they understood that intercession is not the abandonment of justice — it is the activation of the only power that can produce justice at a level the courts of men cannot reach. The word the Spirit is using with me here is λειτουργία (leitourgia, lay-toor-GEE-ah) — the service of the priests, the public liturgical work, the ministry on behalf of the people before God. In the Old Covenant, this was the designated and ordered role of the Levitical priesthood — to stand between the people and the holiness of God, to intercede, to offer, to minister. In the New Covenant, Peter declares that we are a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for God’s own possession, called to proclaim the excellencies of Him who called us out of darkness. The λειτουργία (lay-toor-GEE-ah) has not disappeared — it has been distributed. Every believer carries the weight of the priestly office. And the priest’s first act in the face of a national crisis is not to calculate liability. The priest’s first act is to take it to the altar. This is not to say suffering does not hurt. I noted, with a cough that interrupted my own recording, that the smoke does my lungs no good either. The pain is real. The inconvenience is real. The health hazard is real. Holy intercession does not require us to pretend that suffering is not happening — it requires us to bring that real suffering into the presence of a real God rather than directing our energy primarily toward extracting something from the nation we hold responsible. There is a difference between lament and demand, and the psalms are full of lament that never becomes demand. Lament says, *Lord, this is hard, this is wrong, this is painful — hear me.* Demand says, *Someone owes me, and I will collect.* The first is priestly. The second belongs to a spirit that, frankly, the wilderness was designed to break. I want to sit with what it means that we are being tested — that the nations are being tested — in this moment. The word for testing in the Hebrew, נָסָה (nasah, nah-SAH), carries the sense of a proving, a lifting up to examine, a trial that reveals what is genuinely present rather than merely assumed. God נָסָה (nah-SAH) Abraham when He called for Isaac on Moriah. He was not finding out something He did not know — He was bringing into full manifestation what was already present in the patriarch’s heart, producing an obedience that would become a testimony and a covenant seal. When the wildfire smoke settles over a nation and the first public response is *compensate us*, God is not surprised. But the test has still been administered. The result has still been registered in the ledger of heaven. What was already present in the heart of that culture has been made fully manifest in the caption of a social media reel. And the question for me — for us — is: when the smoke comes to where we live, what caption does the condition of our heart write? I genuinely believe that one of the most disruptive things the people of God could do in this cultural moment is to be people who, when disaster strikes, are immediately and visibly found in prayer. Not as a performance of piety. Not as a substitute for genuine action and compassion. But as the first and instinctive movement of hearts that know where power actually lives. In a culture that reflexively demands compensation, a community that reflexively moves to intercession is prophetic by nature — they are bearing witness, without a single word of argument, to a different economy, a different King, a different order of things. ## Implications and Challenges The entitlement reflex is not merely a political problem — it has infiltrated the worship culture of the Western Church. We have communities that approach God Himself with the posture of the caption: *what do You owe me for this suffering?* The prosperity frameworks that dominate significant portions of Western Christian media train believers to think of relationship with God primarily in terms of what they are owed. This meditation is a gentle but serious call to examine whether we have absorbed more of that cultural posture than we recognize. The absence of intercession as a first response is itself a form of functional atheism. It is possible to carry doctrinal belief in a sovereign God while living practically as though no one is listening and no one has power to act. The person who scrolls past the reel and comments about reparations without once pausing to pray is not necessarily a cynic — they may simply have been shaped by a formation that never established intercession as the instinctive first gear of the spiritual life. This is a discipleship failure that must be named and addressed with gentleness and seriousness. We must also reckon honestly with the difference between justice and demand. The Scripture is not silent on justice — it cries for it from Amos to Revelation. But the justice the Scripture commends flows through a posture of humility before God, not a posture of extraction from neighbors. When we cannot distinguish between the righteous cry for justice and the cultural reflex of entitlement, we have lost a critical discernment. Both may use the same vocabulary. Only one of them begins on its knees. There is a specific challenge for those of us who engage social media and public commentary as part of our regular life: what is our first action when we see a reel, a story, a report of suffering or disaster? If it is to type a response, share a take, or reinforce an opinion — even a righteous-sounding one — before we have spent even a moment in intercession, the platform has discipled us. The algorithm has formed our reflex. And that formation is at odds with the priestly identity we carry. Finally, the instinct toward prayer must never become a substitute for genuine compassion in action. The meditation is not arguing that we should pray and ignore the suffering of those harmed by smoke-related health crises. The argument is about sequence and posture — that prayer is the first movement, the grounding act, the priestly instinct — and that action flowing from that place of intercession is categorically different from action flowing from entitled demand. The former carries the Kingdom. The latter just carries the culture. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This meditation calls me — and us — to honestly audit the reflexes of our hearts. Spiritual maturity is not measured by doctrinal precision alone but by what rises first when suffering arrives. The mature believer has cultivated, through years of intimacy with God and surrender to the Holy Spirit, an instinct toward intercession that is more ingrained than the cultural instinct toward demand. This kind of maturity is not assumed at conversion — it is formed through consistent practice, through choosing the altar over the complaint every time the smoke comes. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The practical obedience this calls forth is simple and radical: intercede first. When the next disaster appears on the feed, before the comment, before the share, before the opinion — pray. Bring it to the God who can clear the fire in five minutes. Take up the λειτουργία (lay-toor-GEE-ah) of the royal priesthood and stand in the gap. This is not passivity — this is the most powerful form of engagement available to those who know the living God. **For Prophetic Posture:** The saints who will be most prophetically effective in the days ahead are those who have trained themselves to read the conditions of the world as invitations to the altar rather than provocations to the comment section. The prophetic posture is one of watchful intercession — seeing what God may be doing in the testing of hearts, discerning the season, and responding with prayer, compassion, and truth rather than with cultural reflex. We are called to be a people whose first instinct when smoke rises is not *who pays* but *Lord, hear us.* ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one of Your servants, a co-heir of the Kingdom established in the blood of Your Son, standing between the conditions of this age and Your eternal counsel — and I ask You to do what only You can do: clear the smoke from the lungs of this culture, not merely the particulate matter in the air but the heavier, more suffocating smoke of entitlement and prayerlessness that has settled over a people who were designed for intercession. I thank You that You are the God who tests hearts not to condemn but to reveal and to redeem — and I ask You to use this moment, even this small meditation, to plant a deeper reflex of prayer in every heart that receives it. Let the royal priesthood arise in its proper identity, turning first to the altar and then to the world with hands that carry what the altar has given. Let our first words when suffering comes be directed upward before they are directed outward. And when the fires come — whether by nature or by the testing of seasons — let what rises from us be intercession, not demand; worship, not complaint; faith, not fear. You are the Lord who can clear the smoke in five minutes. May we live like we believe that. To You alone be the glory, in the name of Jesus.
