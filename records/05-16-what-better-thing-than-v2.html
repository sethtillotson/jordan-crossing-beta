# 05-16 Conversation Reflection: Kingdom Theology, Homeless Ministry, and Covenant Accountability **Title:** 05-16 at 12:54 — What Better Thing Than to Break Bread and Talk God **Recorded:** 2026-05-16 12:54:21 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Conversation Reflection ## Opening Observation As I sat with this conversation — the kind that wanders from a father and son sharing a drink to dumpster theology to a homeless man’s forty-year prison sentence — I found myself arrested not by any single moment but by the current running beneath all of them. Something holy was moving through the ordinary. My brother called to be honest before he drank, and what followed was a window into the Kingdom that no carefully planned Bible study could have engineered. The Spirit has a way of inhabiting the unguarded hour. **Why This Matters:** This matters because most of the Church has been taught to look for God in the polished and the prepared — in the sermon that was rehearsed, in the devotional that was curated, in the worship service that was staged. But this conversation, stumbling through cheesecake and dumpsters and a homeless man eating somebody else’s discarded wings on a Ybor City street corner, kept brushing up against the Kingdom with a naturalness that the religious mind would miss entirely. If we cannot learn to recognize the movement of God through the ordinary texture of human life — the confessional phone call, the half-eaten meal on a curb, the man forgotten by a system that swallowed forty years of his life — then we will spend our days waiting for a move of God that is already happening all around us in the people everyone else walks past. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, this conversation is unremarkable: two friends catching up, jumping between a disclosure about drinking, a funny moment with a cat stealing cheesecake, some lighthearted speculation about dumpster dining behind the Cheesecake Factory, and eventually a more sober story about a homeless man they once picked up on Christmas. A sociologist might note the class commentary embedded in the food waste discussion. A psychologist might analyze the accountability dynamic that opens the call — one friend disclosing a behavior to the other, almost as a preemptive confession. A casual observer would see two men who clearly care about each other, rambling pleasantly through a Saturday evening, with the conversation eventually touching on harder social realities before signing off with warmth. Nothing extraordinary. Just life. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind, however perceptive it may be about human behavior and social patterns, is not equipped to perceive the layer beneath the layer. It can observe accountability without recognizing covenant. It can appreciate compassion for the homeless without recognizing that what my brother described — offering food and conversation to every homeless man he encounters — is not merely a social impulse but a participation in something ancient and divine. It can note the Christmas story about the man from Ybor City and feel moved without recognizing that what was done for that man was done, in the words of the Lord Himself, unto Him. The natural mind processes information; the Spirit-illumined mind perceives *visitation*. These are not the same thing, and no amount of emotional intelligence or ethical sensitivity can bridge that gap from the human side. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It The conversation opens in a place I want to sit with carefully, because I think the Spirit is doing something here that is easy to flatten into either moral permission or moral condemnation. My brother called me before he drank. He did not call to ask whether he should. He called to be honest — to name what he was about to do in the presence of another person, so that if his own declarations later proved empty, I would have standing to hold him to them. That is not merely social accountability. That is the instinct of a man whose conscience has been shaped by something covenantal. The writer of Proverbs knew that “in the multitude of counselors there is safety” (Proverbs 11:14, NKJV), and the earliest Christian communities understood that confession was not merely private — it was relational, embodied, witnessed. My brother may not have named it as such, but what he was reaching for in that phone call was exactly that: a witness. I am not here to render a verdict on the drink itself. That is between him and the Lord, and I trust the Spirit to be faithful to do what only the Spirit can do in a man’s interior life. What arrests me is the *instinct toward transparency* — the refusal to simply do a thing in secret and let it pass unremarked. There is something genuinely Christlike in that impulse, even when the thing being disclosed is itself contested territory. Jesus did not come to men whose lives were already sorted. He came to the ones who were honestly mid-mess, and the ones who met Him with honesty rather than performance were the ones who left transformed. The woman at the well (John 4) did not sanitize her story. The prodigal son (Luke 15) did not rehearse a polished narrative. They told the truth about where they were, and that truth became the very ground on which grace stood to meet them. Then the conversation shifts — as real conversations always do — and suddenly we are talking about a cat eating cheesecake and the relative merits of dumpster diving behind the Cheesecake Factory. And I want to be careful not to force spiritual architecture onto what was genuinely just a funny, wandering exchange between two friends who enjoy each other. Not every moment of a conversation is a parable. But I notice something: the ease with which two men who clearly love the Lord can laugh together, drift through the absurd, and then — without any formal transition — find themselves speaking with genuine tenderness about the homeless, the forgotten, and the discarded. That movement is not accidental. It is the fruit of a certain kind of heart formation. Men who can laugh freely tend to be men who can also weep freely, and men who can weep freely are the men the Kingdom tends to use at the margins. What my brother said next stopped me cold in its simplicity: *“What better to do with a homeless man than break bread and talk God?”* I have heard entire sermons that contained less Gospel than that single line. The Greek word εὐαγγέλιον (euangelion, yoo-ang-GEL-ee-on) — the good news — was not originally a religious term. It was the announcement of a king’s victory, shouted into the streets, proclaimed to those who had been waiting in uncertainty for word of whether they had a future. And the earliest Christians understood that this proclamation was not separable from the table. The Lord’s own pattern was one of eating with sinners (Luke 15:1–2), breaking bread with disciples (Luke 24:30–31), feeding multitudes with whatever was at hand (John 6:1–14). The meal and the message were never meant to be separated. My brother has apparently internalized this without being taught it as a program. Every time he sees a homeless man, he offers food. Every time. Not strategically. Not as an evangelism methodology. As a reflex of the heart. This is what the letter of James was reaching for when he wrote that “faith without works is dead” (James 2:26, NKJV) — but I want to push past even that framing, because that framing still implies that the works are an *addition* to the faith, a proof one must supply. What I see in my brother is something more integrated than that. The offer of food *is* the faith expressing itself, the way a tree does not decide to bear fruit but simply bears it because of what it is at the root. The story that emerged about the man from Ybor City — picked up on Christmas, shown kindness, finally honest about the small bag of cocaine and the weed that had cost him forty years of his life — this is where the conversation broke fully open for me. Forty years. For a bump of cocaine and a bag of marijuana. In the 1980s. Because of the color of his skin. He was released only because the laws were eventually reconsidered, not because anyone fought specifically for *him*. He emerged from that system old, unemployable, invisible. And on a Christmas day, rather than continuing to drive past, my brother and whoever he was with that evening *stopped*. They brought the man to the beach. They talked with him. They were *curious* about him — not as a project, not as a ministry opportunity to be checked off, but as a human being whose story they genuinely wanted to hear. I said during the conversation — and I stand by it now — that if God has a weakness, it is the outcast. The bent ones. The people whom systems discard and polite society avoids making eye contact with. And this is not sentiment. This is canon. The Hebrew word עָנִי (ʿaniy, ah-NEE) — the poor, the afflicted, the bent low — appears throughout the Psalms and Prophets not merely as a demographic category but as a *theological category*. The עָנִי (ah-NEE) are the particular object of divine attention. Psalm 9:18 declares, “For the needy shall not always be forgotten; the expectation of the poor shall not perish forever” (NKJV). Isaiah 61 — the very passage Jesus stood up to read in Nazareth when He announced what He had come to do (Luke 4:18) — is saturated with the language of the poor, the brokenhearted, the captive, the bound. When Jesus read that scroll and said “Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing” (Luke 4:21, NKJV), He was not announcing a new program. He was announcing that *He Himself* was the answer to the long cry of the עָנִי (ah-NEE). And the man from Ybor City — who went to prison young and came out old for a charge that would barely register as a misdemeanor today — he is exactly that: a man whose life was swallowed by a system and who emerged bent. The fact that he ended up on a street corner where two men would stop their Christmas to hear his story and show him the sea — I do not think that was merely coincidence. I think that was the Kingdom operating through ordinary acts of human kindness in a way that the man who orchestrated it may not have even recognized as divine. The conversation closed with a brief, almost passing exchange about Hannah and Samuel — names from Scripture, figures who are also messengers in the canon — and a quick agreement about the unity of the Body. *“We’re all one body. God bless, brother.”* What might have seemed like a throwaway sign-off was actually a theological summary of the entire conversation: that what happens between brothers in honest phone calls, that what happens on Christmas streets with homeless strangers, that what happens when two friends laugh about dumpsters and then pivot without warning to the theology of the marginalized — all of it is happening within the one Body, animated by one Spirit, moving toward one Lord. The Kingdom is not reserved for the hour when the sermon begins. It is present and active wherever the Spirit has shaped a heart that cannot pass a hungry man without stopping, that cannot do something in secret when transparency is possible, that cannot hear a hard story without leaning in with genuine curiosity. These are not exceptional spiritual gifts. They are the ordinary fruit of an extraordinary indwelling. And the Spirit is pressing on me, even now, to recognize that the conversations I sometimes treat as peripheral to my spiritual life may in fact be among its most generative moments — not because I engineered them to be, but because the Spirit inhabits what is offered honestly. ## Implications and Challenges There is a kind of Christianity that has been domesticated into a purely interior event — private devotions, private repentance, private faith — where the idea that a friend should call before he drinks, and that the drink itself might be worth naming and examining together, would feel intrusive or legalistic. But the witness of Scripture is consistently communal. The early church was not a collection of private believers who happened to attend the same gathering; it was a Body, a household, a people in covenant with one another as well as with God. When my brother called, he was living out something the Western individualist imagination has largely lost: that my choices exist within a relational field where others have a stake in my integrity. There is also a challenge here to the way the Church has often structured its engagement with the poor and homeless. My brother did not describe a program. He described a reflex. And this matters enormously, because programs can be turned off when funding runs out, when enthusiasm fades, or when the numbers do not justify the effort. A reflex cannot be turned off — it is who you are. The formation of disciples who carry the Kingdom instinctively into every encounter with the marginalized is a slower, less measurable work than running a food bank, but it is the deeper work. The Cheesecake Factory dumpster joke, which made us both laugh, was actually concealing a genuine insight: that the world wastes with breathtaking abundance while people go hungry within blocks of that abundance. That is not merely a social observation. It is an indictment that the prophets would have recognized instantly. The story of the man from Ybor City also confronts the comfortable assumption that justice is someone else’s domain — that politics and law are not the Church’s concern, and that we need only preach the Gospel and let the systems sort themselves out. But a man who spent forty years in prison for a quantity of a substance that would today result in, at most, a civil fine is a man whose life was consumed by a system. When the laws finally changed and he was released, there was no one there to help him rebuild. He was simply out — older, unemployable, homeless. The Church that washes its hands of the structures that produce such outcomes, while simultaneously claiming to love the poor, is practicing a deeply inconsistent faith. The prophets were not silent about unjust systems. Isaiah, Amos, Micah, Jeremiah — they named specific structures by name and called the covenant people to account. Finally, the ease and naturalness of the Kingdom language in this conversation is itself a challenge to the stiffness that often characterizes Christian witness. These two men did not modulate into a religious register when they spoke of God and the homeless. They simply spoke. The conversation flowed from cheesecake to accountability to homelessness to Scripture to prayer without any of the forced pivots that so often mark deliberate evangelism. This is what it looks like when the Kingdom has actually taken up residence in the interior life of a person — not as an external obligation to witness, but as a lens through which all of life is already being seen. The implication is that the goal of discipleship is not to teach Christians when to insert the Gospel into conversation, but to form people for whom the Gospel is the native language of their entire perception. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity, as this conversation models it, is not measured by the absence of unresolved areas in a person’s life. My brother called not as a man who had it all together but as a man who understood that the alternative to honesty was far more dangerous than the thing he was being honest about. Maturity is the willingness to be known — to bring what is actual rather than what is polished into relationship, and to trust that covenant love can hold the real. Growing into this kind of maturity means releasing the performance of spiritual tidiness and learning the freedom of transparent living before God and before trusted brothers and sisters in the faith. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The Kingdom calls us to a pattern of life in which the interruption of a homeless man’s story on Christmas is not a detour from our celebration but the *center* of it. Kingdom obedience here means developing — through prayer, through Scripture, through the forming work of the Spirit — the kind of reflexive hospitality that does not need to deliberate about whether to stop. It stops. It breaks bread. It asks questions. It listens to forty-year stories of injustice without looking at the clock. This is not a program. This is formation, and it happens in the ordinary rhythms of a life surrendered. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this conversation calls forth is one of *attentiveness to the ordinary*. The prophet is not only the one who stands before kings; the prophet is also the one who sees what others walk past. To develop a prophetic eye for the Kingdom is to become the kind of person for whom a conversation that wanders from drinking accountability to dumpster theology to racial injustice is not chaos — it is a coherent revelation of where the Spirit is pressing. Learn to stay present in the unguarded conversation. Learn to recognize the Kingdom thread when it surfaces. And when you see a man bent low by what life or systems have done to him, stop. Break bread. Talk God. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one You have called — not as one who has it all sorted, but as one who has been found by You in the middle of ordinary, wandering, honest life, and who has learned enough to know that You inhabit that ordinariness with far more faithfulness than I often recognize. I thank You for the kind of brotherhood that includes honest phone calls, for the kind of formation that produces reflexive hospitality toward the forgotten, for the kind of grace that makes forty-year stories of injustice matter to the people who hear them. I stand in agreement with Your heart for the עָנִי (ah-NEE) — the bent ones, the discarded ones, the ones the system swallowed and the street now holds — and I declare by the covenant blood of Your Son that they are not forgotten by You, that their expectation has not perished, and that the Kingdom You have placed within Your people is actively at work to bring the εὐαγγέλιον (yoo-ang-GEL-ee-on) into the very streets where they sleep. Form in us the reflexes of the Kingdom. Make us people who cannot walk past without stopping. Make us people who break bread and talk God not as a strategy but as a way of being. And in all of it, be glorified — in the honest call, in the shared meal, in the story heard with full attention, and in the Body that holds all of it together as one.
