# 06-14 Call Reflection: Prophetic Word, Lament, and Announcement of New Life **Title:** 06-14 at 11:27 — As It Is in Egypt, So It Is in Me: A Word Born in the Wilderness **Recorded:** 2026-06-14 11:27:17 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation There are moments when heaven interrupts the ordinary without ceremony — no altar, no sanctuary, no extended season of waiting in a prayer room. I am sitting with this call, a brief exchange that began with payment fees and routing logistics, and somehow ended with the trembling announcement of new life. What arrests me most is not the news itself, though the news is staggering — it is the sentence that followed the announcement, the one I almost missed: *“So that was his answer to my lamentations about being in poverty.”* **Why This Matters:** This matters because so many of us are taught, implicitly or explicitly, that God answers in proportion to our preparation — that breakthrough arrives after we have prayed long enough, fasted consistently enough, positioned ourselves correctly enough. But what I witnessed in this conversation is something far more ancient and far more disorienting to that framework: God answering lament with life, answering poverty-language with a covenant promise of increase, in the most unguarded and unscripted moment of an ordinary phone call. The Body of Christ desperately needs to recover a theology of the *unexpected visitation* — the understanding that God is not waiting for us to create the perfect spiritual atmosphere before He moves. He moves through our muddy, half-formed, still-figuring-it-out moments, and He does so on purpose. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, this transcript is simply a casual business call between two men that happened to include a personal life update. The practical mind registers the surface content efficiently: there is a financial transaction being tracked, a fee structure being evaluated, a better payment routing solution being discussed, and then, almost as an aside, a friend shares early news of a potential pregnancy. The natural mind is genuinely happy for the friend, perhaps surprised by the timing of the disclosure, and files it as a warm personal moment embedded in an otherwise logistical exchange. The connection between the prophetic word — *“letting it be in Egypt as it is in me”* — and the pregnancy announcement would likely register as coincidence, or at most as a touching personal association that I made in an emotional moment. The natural mind is comfortable with linear causality: the business conversation happened, a personal update was shared, the friend responded warmly. There is no deeper architecture to it. **The Limitation:** The difficulty is that the natural mind operates exclusively on the visible surface of events, and so it will always reduce a kairos moment to a chronos coincidence. It cannot perceive the threading — that a prophetic word spoken before the announcement was somehow already carrying the freight of the announcement within it, the way a seed carries the tree. To the unilluminated eye, *“letting it be in Egypt as it is in me”* is an opaque phrase that even Brother Samuel admitted he did not fully understand when he wrote it. But the Spirit does not require the vessel to comprehend the word before it lands. The prophetic does not wait for the prophet’s full understanding any more than the burning bush waited for Moses to understand fire. Something was being spoken over a season before the season fully arrived, and what I am sitting with now is the recognition that the prophetic word and the announcement of new life in this call are not separate events — they are one unfolding. The natural mind, working only from what it can measure, will always miss that. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I keep returning to one line, and I cannot move past it without pressing into it: *“So that was his answer to my lamentations about being in poverty.”* There is something deeply Davidic in that sentence. David, who knew the full spectrum of lament — who cried out *“How long, O Lord? Will You forget me forever?”* in Psalm 13 — also knew the turn. He knew the moment when the lament became praise not because the circumstances had fully resolved, but because something landed in the spirit before it landed in the natural. What I hear in that sentence is not triumphalism. It is not a man declaring that his financial situation is fixed. It is a man recognizing that God has spoken into the middle of his wilderness, and the form of that speaking is a child. And that, I find, is deeply scriptural. Life announced in the valley is a recurring pattern in the canon. It is Sarah laughing in her tent, ninety years old and barren, and the LORD saying *“Is anything too hard for the LORD?”* (Genesis 18:14, NKJV). It is Elizabeth, whose womb had been shut, filled with new life at the very moment the forerunner of the Messiah was being formed within her. It is Hannah, weeping so hard at Shiloh that Eli mistook her grief for drunkenness — and yet from that very weeping came Samuel, whose name means *“heard by God.”* The pattern is unmistakable: when God is preparing to do something generative, He often meets the one who is lamenting. Lament is not the opposite of fruitfulness. In God’s economy, lament can be the very soil in which promise is planted. I notice also the prophetic word that was given before this moment — *“letting it be in Egypt as it is in me”* — and I find that phrase doing something unexpected to me as I sit with it. Egypt in Scripture is never a simple symbol. It is the place of bondage, yes — the iron furnace of Deuteronomy 4:20 where Israel was hammered into a people. But Egypt is also the place of preservation. It is where Jacob’s sons found grain in famine. It is where Joseph’s suffering became the salvation of many. And in the New Testament, it is where the infant Christ was hidden from Herod’s slaughter — *“Out of Egypt I called My Son”* (Matthew 2:15, NKJV), Matthew reaching back to Hosea 11:1 and revealing that what was said of Israel as a nation found its fullest fulfillment in the Son of God Himself. So when the Spirit breathed *“letting it be in Egypt as it is in me”* over my season, I am not hearing a word that says *remain in bondage*. I am hearing something more costly and more glorious than that: I am hearing a word that says *what is being formed in you in this place of constriction is being formed on purpose*. Egypt is not the end of the story. Egypt is where the deliverer gestates. Egypt is where what is hidden becomes what is prepared. And now — a child. Potentially. Tenderly. Still early, still trembling with the fragility of early announcement. And yet the announcement has been made. New life has been spoken into the air between two brothers, in a car, on a phone call, somewhere on a highway, an hour from home. I am struck by the geography of this moment: there is no sacred space by conventional definition. Brother Samuel is in a vehicle, Donovan is with him, the call began with Stripe fees and Mercury account routing. And yet heaven broke through. This is precisely how the Gospel moves — not through our orchestration of holy settings, but through God’s sovereign interruption of ordinary ones. The name that came — Elijah, if it is a boy. I cannot pass by that without pausing. אֵלִיָּהוּ (Eliyahu, el-ee-YAH-hoo) — *my God is Yahweh*. It is a name that is itself a declaration, a confession spoken into a life before that life has spoken a single word. The original Elijah was raised up in a moment of national apostasy, when Israel had largely abandoned covenant faithfulness. He was a man of solitude, of sudden appearance, of fire — a man who despaired deeply enough under a juniper tree to ask God to take his life, and yet received bread and water from an angel and the instruction: *“Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you”* (1 Kings 19:7, NKJV). To speak the name Elijah over an unborn child is, whether consciously or not, to invoke the spirit of prophetic endurance — the willingness to be hidden in the Kerith Ravine when no one is watching, to be sustained by what heaven provides rather than what culture offers. There is also something I want to sit with regarding the phrase *“it’s gonna be rough.”* Both men said it. It was not said in despair — it was said in honest brotherhood, the kind of truthfulness that only exists between men who have actually walked together in hardship. And what I find significant is that the roughness was not a contradiction of the blessing. There was no attempt to spiritually bypass the real weight of what bringing a child into a season of financial strain means. This is mature faith. Hebrews 11 is full of men and women who received promises and also received hardship — *“these all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them afar off”* (Hebrews 11:13, NKJV). Faith does not require us to pretend the valley is flat. It requires us to keep walking. What also moves me is the way Brother Samuel received the news. He said, *“I need to go pray on this.”* He said, *“I want to speak to about this properly, not a half-assed conversation.”* There is something deeply honoring in that response — an instinct toward reverence when the holy intersects the ordinary. He did not simply congratulate and move on. He recognized the weight of the moment and chose to steward it with intention. That is the posture of a true spiritual brother: not performing emotional support, but actually making space to bring the thing before God before bringing a full response to his brother. I want to learn from that. I return again to the poverty-lament connection, because I think it deserves further unpacking. Lament in Scripture is not faithlessness. Lament is faith that refuses to be silent. The Psalms of lament — and there are more of them than we often acknowledge — are not failures of trust. They are trust being exercised through the pressure of pain. When I describe my cries about being in poverty as the context in which this answer came, I am standing in a long line of covenant people who discovered that God was listening more carefully than they realized, that what felt like unanswered silence was actually the gestation period of a specific and personal response. *“Before they call, I will answer; and while they are still speaking, I will hear”* (Isaiah 65:24, NKJV). The answer was forming before the lament was fully articulated. And finally — the fee conversation. I do not want to spiritualize away the mundane, because I think the mundane has something to say here too. The fact that this conversation began with Stripe fees and routing logistics and ended with the announcement of a child is itself a kind of parable. We do not get to choose which conversations become sacred. We do not get to designate which ordinary Tuesday phone calls will carry the weight of heaven’s interruption. This means the only defensible posture is to live in readiness — to treat every exchange as a potential site of encounter, not because every conversation will carry that weight, but because we do not know which one will. The disciples did not know, on the road to Emmaus, that the stranger walking with them was the risen Lord. They only knew afterward, when the bread was broken, that their hearts had been burning within them the whole time (Luke 24:32). ## Implications and Challenges The modern Western church has developed an almost liturgical attachment to designated spiritual spaces and programmed spiritual moments. We expect God to speak in the sermon, in the worship set, in the prayer meeting. What this transcript gently but firmly challenges is the assumption that God restricts Himself to our curated containers. The implication is uncomfortable: if we only attend to God in sanctioned moments, we may be consistently missing His voice in the unscripted hours. There is also a challenge here regarding prophetic stewardship. Brother Samuel admitted plainly that he did not fully understand the word he gave when he gave it. This is actually theologically consistent with the nature of the prophetic gift — the prophets themselves searched and inquired carefully about the grace being predicted through them (1 Peter 1:10-11, NKJV). But the modern tendency is often to either over-explain the prophetic word into control or to dismiss an incomplete prophetic word as invalid. This moment invites a more trusting posture: release the word, steward it with humility, and let God be the One who reveals its unfolding. The candor about financial hardship embedded in this conversation is also worth naming directly. The prosperity frameworks that have shaped much of contemporary charismatic Christianity would struggle to hold my statement that *“it’s gonna be rough”* alongside genuine faith and genuine blessing simultaneously. But Scripture does not have that problem. Blessing and difficulty coexist throughout the covenant narrative. To suggest that true blessing insulates us from roughness is to preach a gospel that the apostles would not recognize. The instinct toward prayer before response — modeled by Brother Samuel — confronts the reactive culture of immediate commentary that dominates both social media Christianity and even many small group and pastoral interactions. Not every sacred disclosure requires an instant theological response. Sometimes the most faithful thing a spiritual brother can do is say: *let me bring this to God before I bring it back to you*. That is not evasion. That is honor. Finally, the question of naming — of calling a child Elijah before birth, before gender confirmation, before any certainty — is itself a prophetic act. It is worth asking ourselves whether we are people who speak into potential, who name what is not yet formed as though it is already being shaped by God’s intention. Or have we become a people who only name what is fully confirmed, fully arrived, fully safe? Abraham believed God *“who gives life to the dead and calls those things which do not exist as though they did”* (Romans 4:17, NKJV). Prophetic fatherhood begins before the child is born. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This calls me — and us — toward a more integrated spirituality, one that does not partition life into sacred and secular sectors. Spiritual maturity is the ability to recognize the movement of God in the unsanitized moments: on a highway, in a car, between payment details. It means training the inner ear to remain attentive even when the conversation is about routing numbers. It also means learning to hold lament and expectation in the same open hand, trusting that the God who hears our poverty-prayers is already composing His answer in dimensions we cannot yet see. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this moment calls for is twofold: the obedience of declaration and the obedience of preparation. To speak the name Elijah over a potential life is a declaration. To honestly acknowledge that the season ahead will be rough and to choose faithfulness anyway — that is preparation. Kingdom obedience does not require certainty about outcomes. It requires alignment with what God is doing, even when that alignment is costly, early, and not yet fully formed. **For Prophetic Posture:** This trains us toward a posture of yielded attentiveness: releasing prophetic words we do not fully understand, trusting that God completes what He initiates, watching for the fulfillment in unexpected forms, and treating our brothers and sisters’ sacred disclosures with enough reverence to pray before we respond. The prophetic people of God are not those who have all the interpretation locked in advance. They are those who remain close enough to the Spirit to recognize, when the moment arrives, that this — right here, in this unscripted phone call — is where the word was pointing all along. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one of Your servants, a co-heir of the Kingdom, standing in the New Covenant You have sealed with the blood of Your Son — and I bring this moment to You with thanksgiving and awe. You are the God who answers lament with life, who speaks into Egypt seasons before the deliverance is visible, who hides promises in prophetic words that even the vessel does not fully understand at the writing. I thank You for the gift of brotherhood, for the instinct to pray before responding, for the grace that interrupts logistics with glory. I stand in agreement with what You are doing — with the life that may even now be forming, with the name Elijah already spoken in faith over what is not yet seen, with the declaration that my God is Yahweh even in the rough seasons. Let every word spoken in this call be received and stewarded as a sacred trust. Let lament continue to be heard in Your courts as the prayer it actually is. And let Your people recover the understanding that You are not confined to our sacred spaces — that You move on highways, in cars, through Stripe fee conversations, in the in-between and unguarded moments of real life. Be glorified in this new life if You confirm it. Be glorified in the roughness of the season. Be glorified in every prophetic word that lands before its meaning is fully known. In the name of Jesus Christ, who is the same yesterday, today, and forever — Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Genesis 18:14 — “Is anything too hard for the LORD?” | יִפָּלֵא (yippale, yih-pah-LEH) — to be too wonderful / beyond capacity | Life announced to the barren;
