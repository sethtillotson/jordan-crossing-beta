# 04-25 Call Reflection: Identity Under Assault, Barabbas, Gehenna, and Jonah’s Mercy **Title:** 04-25 at 17:11 — When the Wound Is Still Open, the Fish Brings You Home, and Barabbas Walks Free **Recorded:** 2026-04-25 17:11:33 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture — Phone or Video Call) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I am sitting with the accumulated weight of a single extended conversation that moved through more theological terrain than I initially realized — from raw brotherhood and betrayal, to the sovereign mercy threading itself through a guilty man’s unexpected release, to the question of whether the fire Jesus warned about is a geographic indictment of our pride or the metaphysical architecture of eternity. These three currents ran through one evening, one exchange, one series of voices pressing toward something the Spirit was orchestrating beneath all of it. What I am finding now, as I let it settle, is that these were not three separate conversations. They were three movements of a single revelation — a revelation about identity under assault, about the mercy that arrives through the darkest enclosures, and about the judgment that is never divorced from the Father’s intent to restore. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the men and women who inhabit the Body of Christ are not navigating abstract theological questions in comfortable, detached classrooms. They are navigating them at the edge of their endurance — in the middle of sleepless nights, physical pain, betrayal by those they covenanted with, anger that will not resolve into tidiness, grief that is too large for the emotional categories available to them. The conversation I was part of that evening held all of that simultaneously: a brother named his brokenness aloud, the question of identity under assault surfaced through the image of Barabbas, and the theological architecture of divine judgment was being dismantled and rebuilt in real time through honest engagement with the original text. If the Church cannot speak to people in that condition — cannot meet them in the fire and the fish and the courtyard of apparent injustice — it has very little to offer. This meditation is my attempt to draw out what the Spirit was pressing through that conversation, for the sake of every person who will one day find themselves in a similar simultaneous reckoning. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the perspective of natural reasoning, what unfolded across these hours looks like a group of men in crisis processing their circumstances together — relationally supportive, emotionally cathartic, perhaps admirably honest. The natural mind can appreciate the vulnerability: one man naming his anger and pettiness in real time, another offering the kind of empathetic theological reframing that any perceptive counselor might provide, a third arriving with a single word that redirected the conversation productively. It would explain the estranged wife’s behavior as a predictable response to relational rupture, the call about Barabbas as a clever literary parallel designed to help a suffering person feel less alone, and the discussion of Gehenna as a useful historical correction that removes the punitive edge from an uncomfortable text. The natural mind would locate the value in the honesty, the peer support, the shared Bible reading — and it would frame the theological unpacking of Gehenna and Jonah as intellectually stimulating and emotionally freeing. Sincere. Human. Understandable. But entirely horizontal. **The Limitation:** What the purely horizontal mind cannot perceive is that the architecture beneath all of this is vertical — that the Spirit was not merely providing emotional support resources for troubled people but was pressing, through the cracks that pain had opened, toward something structurally true about who these men are, what holds when everything else is stripped, and what the cross actually accomplished in the courtyard where Barabbas stood blinking in the daylight. The natural mind hears “find yourself in Barabbas” and sees a therapeutic reframing. The Spirit hears it and sees a mirror held up to the guilty man who was released without explanation, whose freedom was purchased at a price he did not negotiate and cannot repay, and who walked out of that story into silence — alive because Another was chosen in his place. Equally, the natural mind hears Gehenna and reaches for the institutional default of eternal conscious torment, because that is what centuries of fear-based theology have trained it to receive. It does not stop to ask what the valley of Hinnom actually was, what a first-century Jewish ear heard in that image, or what a Father whose mercy extends even to the cattle of Nineveh is actually communicating through it. The Spirit-taught mind goes back to the text and asks again — and what it finds is a God whose judgments are always moving toward μετάνοια (metanoia, meh-TAH-noy-ah) — the full reorientation of the mind and will — rather than toward punishment as an end in itself. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I want to begin with the thread that ran beneath all three movements of that evening, because I believe it is the one the Spirit was most insistently pressing: identity under assault. My brother Samuel was pouring out raw grief and fury — the grief of a man who built something on the expectation of covenant and found that the other party had never truly entered one. And as I listened, something in me recognized not just his wound but the ancient strategy running beneath it. Every accusation his circumstances were making against him — you are discarded, you are the villain in her story, you are replaceable, you made the wrong choice — was targeting not his emotions but his identity as a son. This is the enemy’s oldest deployment. It was aimed at the Son of God Himself in the wilderness: “*If You are the Son of God…”* The assault does not always arrive as overt rejection. Sometimes it arrives as a photograph. Sometimes it arrives as a ring worn as a prop next to another man at a prom, curated for an audience of strangers. The message, every time, is the same: *you are not what you think you are.* And the only anchor that holds against that kind of assault is not emotional resilience or time management. It is the revealed, blood-sealed declaration of sonship that nothing created has authority to revoke. What I found myself speaking into my brother’s pain — perhaps haltingly, perhaps imperfectly — was the apostolic declaration that his identity as a son of the Kingdom is not contingent on any of this. I told him he would wake and speak the truth: that he is an eternal priest, a son of the Kingdom of God, and that nothing done out of anger or quickness of heart can ever separate him. Paul writes in Romans 8:38–39 that neither death nor life, nor angels nor principalities nor powers, nor things present nor things to come, nor height nor depth, nor any other created thing shall be able to separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord. The betrayal is a created thing. The photograph is a created thing. The grief, the fury, the sleeplessness, the arm aching on the machine — all created things. None of them carry authority to issue a final verdict on the sonship of a man who is hidden in Christ. I called him an eternal priest that evening, and I stand by that declaration. Peter names it plainly in 1 Peter 2:9: *you are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, His own special people*. The Greek word ἱεράτευμα (hierateuma, hee-eh-RAH-tyoo-mah) — a corporate priestly body, a collective priestly order — is not an honorary title. It is a functional description. Samuel’s pain does not disqualify him from this. His anger does not strip him of this. Even his very human groping for something to take the edge off does not revoke the blood-covenant identity written over him when he was bought. And then, near the end of the call, Samuel asked something that arrested me entirely: *Would you call yourself a son of the house of David? What happened to the house of David? What did they transition to?* He raised it almost in passing, almost experimentally, in the middle of fire and anguish — and I recognized immediately that this was not small talk. This was, in his high-and-hurting state, a prophetic groping toward something ancient and structural about the nature of covenant identity. The house of David — established through the unconditional oath of 2 Samuel 7, the promise given to a man after God’s own heart — was not terminated. It was *fulfilled*. It was raised into something that no Philistine army, no Babylonian exile, no Roman occupation could touch. It transitions into the eternal Kingship of the Son of David, who sits upon a throne that will never end. Luke 1:32–33: *He will be great, and will be called the Son of the Highest; and the Lord God will give Him the throne of His father David. And He will reign over the house of Jacob forever, and of His kingdom there will be no end.* The house of David teaches the grieving man something he urgently needs to hear: covenant lineage survives catastrophe. When that house was threatened by Saul’s spear, by Absalom’s rebellion, by Athaliah’s massacre of the royal seed in 2 Kings 11 — more than once it seemed the line would be severed. And yet one child survived. One heir was hidden. The covenant could not be extinguished because it was not anchored in human faithfulness. It was anchored in God’s oath. And that means Samuel’s identity is not the wreckage this season is trying to convince him it is. It is a co-inheritance with the One who survived every assault — συγκληρονόμοι (synklēronomoi, sün-klā-ro-NO-moy) — joint-inheritors, sharing the inheritance of the One who sits on that eternal throne. Then the conversation turned — as the Spirit’s economy always turns — toward the one name that arrived through my brother Leon with quiet, undeniable authority: *Barabbas*. He had been drawn into the call, and he arrived with a single directive that neither of us had been moving toward: *read every passage about Barabbas and find yourself there*. I recognized immediately the kind of prophetic economy I have come to know: not many words, not elaborate exposition, but a specific scriptural address delivered with precision. The Spirit does not waste syllables. And so we opened Matthew 27, and we began to read. The name Βαραββᾶς (Barabbas, bah-RAB-bahs) is an Aramaic compound — *bar*, son, and *abba*, father. Son of the father. And standing across from him in that courtyard is the actual Son of the actual Father, the Messiah. The irony is not accidental. It is architecturally divine, embedded in the text before a single word of theology is spoken over it. Barabbas, the Gospels tell us, was a notorious prisoner — λῃστής (lēstēs, lay-STEES) in Mark’s account: a robber, an insurrectionist, a murderer in Luke’s telling. He is not a sympathetic figure. He belongs in custody. He deserves what is coming to him. And then, without his asking, without his repentance on record, without a single word attributed to him, the crowd shouts his name and he walks free. The text never follows him. He simply disappears into the daylight, a free man who perhaps never fully understood the price of his freedom. And that is precisely the point. Barabbas is not a character to feel sorry for — he is a mirror. Every man who has ever stood guilty in the courtyard of his own choices, who has carried the weight of what he has done and become, who deserves the accumulation that is surrounding him — stands in that courtyard as Barabbas. And he walks free. Not because the verdict was wrong. But because Another stepped into his place and bore it completely. That is the gospel in its most unvarnished form, and it is the gospel Samuel needed to hear not as theology but as reality: the exchange in that courtyard was not only historical. It was personal. It was made for every man who ever stood guilty and walked free because a Lamb bore his sentence. The brother who had been asking about prayer posture earlier in that same evening — what should I actively be thinking about when I come to God in prayer? — received an answer that, in retrospect, is connected to everything else: *bring nothing*. Not a question. Not an agenda. Not a theological inquiry. Enter the secret place — what Jesus calls in Matthew 6:6 the ταμεῖον (tameion, tah-MAY-on), the inner room, the hidden chamber — with the sole expectation of receiving the gift of the Father’s full presence. And what I noticed is that the moment that instruction landed, the interior erupted — not with theological objection but with the confession of petty thoughts, of anger still running hot, of the gap between the invitation to stillness and the reality of what was actually inside. That eruption was not a derailment. It was the very curriculum. The flesh surfaces precisely when the Spirit begins to press toward genuine encounter. And in naming it — giving the ugliness an honest name rather than performing peace he did not have — something opened. This is precisely the dynamic the Psalter was written to capture. David did not arrive at the Psalms through abstraction. He arrived through murder, adultery, exile, betrayal, and the unraveling of his own kingdom. The lament tradition — constituting nearly a third of the entire Psalter — is not an embarrassment. It is the Spirit’s authorized language for the soul under siege. Psalm 22 opens with desolation — *My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?* — and arrives at proclamation. The pattern is not avoidance of the wound. It is traversal of it. And Samuel, sitting in what he described as the Thanos gauntlet of simultaneous suffering — physical, relational, financial, emotional — was being given canonical permission to pray ugly, to pray angry, to pray confused, and to expect God to be large enough to receive all of it. The question of Gehenna arrived that night almost as a challenge — someone had pulled up a reference describing it as hell, as a place of punishment, and I had to stop there. Γέεννα (geenna, gheh-EN-nah) in Koine Greek is not a theological abstraction. It is a geography. It is the valley of Hinnom, outside Jerusalem’s walls — in the Old Testament associated with the pagan practice of child sacrifice to Molech, and by the first century the city’s perpetually burning refuse heap. Every Jewish listener who heard Jesus say “Gehenna of the fire” in Matthew 5:22 would have had a visceral, concrete image: the smoldering dump you could smell from the city walls. When Jesus says a man who calls his brother a fool is liable to “the Gehenna of the fire,” He is not mapping out a doctrine of eternal conscious torment. He is saying: your contempt for your neighbor will make your life indistinguishable from burning garbage. Your pride, your anger, your dismissal of the human being across from you, will consume you from within the way fire consumes refuse. And then — and I love this, because the pivot is so precise — He immediately says: before you bring your gift to the altar, go be reconciled to your brother first. The most sacred religious act available to a first-century Jew, and Jesus subordinates it to the reconciliation of brothers. The altar gift without the reconciled brother is γράμμα (gramma, GRAHM-mah) — the letter — without ἀγάπη (agapē, ah-GAH-pay) — covenantal, self-giving love. It is religious performance without covenantal substance. The chief priests in Matthew 27 are the living exhibit: the most letter-literate people in the first-century world, orchestrating the destruction of the Word made flesh, quoting chapter and verse while condemning the Author of the canon. The fruit of γράμμα (GRAHM-mah) without πνεῦμα (pneuma, NYOO-mah) — the Spirit — is a community that is well-versed in the text and deeply deceived about its Author. This is the question my brother pressed that I found myself answering before I had finished forming the thought: if there is no hell in the sense we have been taught, what did Jesus save us from? And the Spirit pressed the answer through clearly: He saved us from a wasted life. He saved us from ourselves. He saved us from the condition of Barabbas — guilty, violent, rebellious — walking free without the dignity of understanding what just happened on his behalf. He saved us from becoming the chief priests, who had every resource the Kingdom had entrusted to them and used it to destroy the very One those resources were meant to announce. And He saved us from Jonah — sitting outside Nineveh in bitter, shade-seeking self-righteousness, waiting for the destruction of people he should have loved. It was in this movement of the conversation that Jonah arrived, almost by accident. But nothing in a Spirit-led conversation is accidental. The book of Jonah is one of the most devastating portraits of divine mercy in all of Scripture, and it is devastating precisely because the mercy runs further than the prophet’s resentment can track. The belly of the great fish — the three days in darkness, in the deep, in what Jonah himself calls Sheol in his prayer — is not a punishment. It is a womb. A gestation period in the dark that ends not in death but in being vomited onto the exact shore he needed to reach. The Hebrew word for what God prepares is מָנָה (manah, mah-NAH) — appointed, designated, sovereignly arranged. God מָנָה (mah-NAH) the fish. He also מָנָה (mah-NAH) the plant, the worm, and the scorching east wind. Every comfort and every discomfort in Jonah’s story is a sovereign appointment. None of it is punishment for its own sake. All of it is μετάνοια (meh-TAH-noy-ah) — a divinely engineered turning, the full reorientation of the mind and will toward what is true and what is required. And this is precisely what Jesus names explicitly in Matthew 12:40: the three days in the fish’s belly are a sign of the Son of Man’s burial and resurrection. Which means the darkest enclosure of your disobedience — the place that feels like death, the deep darkness where you are certain you have forfeited your calling — is not your tomb. It is your womb. If you will pray from within it with thanksgiving and surrender, the God who מָנָה (mah-NAH) the fish will also מָנָה (mah-NAH) the shore. One of the brothers on the call put it beautifully, even through exhaustion: it is like a son living in his father’s house, eating his father’s food, sleeping under his father’s roof, and then becoming enraged when the father adjusts the thermostat. That image is so exact it almost hurts. Jonah touched the thermostat when God said “Go to Nineveh” — and he went to Joppa instead. And God did not strike him dead. God prepared a fish. The discipline of the Lord — παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah), fatherly training, the structured education of a son by a father who intends to produce maturity — is not cruelty. It is חֶסֶד (hesed, HEH-sed), covenantal lovingkindness, wearing a more uncomfortable garment than we usually assign it. The book of Jonah ends with a question from God that the text never answers in Jonah’s words. *Should I not pity Nineveh, in which there are more than one hundred and twenty thousand persons who cannot discern between their right hand and their left — and also much livestock?* I have sat with that final line more times than I can count. The cattle. God explicitly mentions the cattle in His defense of His mercy toward Nineveh. This is not an afterthought — it is the fullest Old Testament disclosure of חֶסֶד (HEH-sed)’s scope, extending beyond political or covenant relationships into the created order itself. And it lands, that question, as an indictment of every theological system that maps the Father’s mercy too narrowly — that draws the circle of redemption precisely at the edge of what we find personally believable or emotionally satisfying. The question is left unanswered in the text because it is left for the reader. For Jonah. For Samuel. For every one of us who has ever sat on a hillside waiting for the destruction of people we were supposed to love, and found our shade-plant withering. And what I said to my brother at the close of that night is what I still believe now: wait for your plant. Wait for your worm. And when you finally reach the place where you just want it to be over — expect to hear Him. What threads all of this together — the sonship, the Barabbas exchange, the cracked-open heart, the belly of the fish, the valley of Hinnom reinterpreted — is a single, continuous revelation that the Spirit was pressing through every movement of that long evening: the Kingdom of God is built through men whose interior life has been genuinely restructured by encounter, not by information. The chief priests had the most information. Pilate had political information. The crowd had enough information to make a different choice. What was missing in all of them was the willingness to let the text do what it was designed to do: press them past the management of appearances into the genuine encounter with the living God who will not be domesticated by religious habit. What I watched happen in that conversation — through the anger, through the pettiness named aloud, through the prophetic word about Barabbas, through the late-night unpacking of Gehenna and Jonah — was precisely that pressing. The Spirit was doing what He always does in the cracked-open places: entering through the aperture that pain had made, pressing truth into the marrow rather than merely the mind, and restructuring something from within. Brother Leon said it with precision before he stepped off the call: *there is going to be a crack in your heart, and that is when He finds you.* The vessel that cannot crack cannot be filled. The crack is not damage to be repaired. It is the aperture through which the living God enters with His fullness. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation presses upon me is the habit of arriving at prayer — and at brotherhood — with an agenda. We come to the secret place with our lists, our petitions, our journals open and ready. We come to our brothers with theological frameworks pre-loaded, ready to deploy. And there is a place for structured intercession and prepared truth. But if those are the only modes we know, we have never truly entered the ταμεῖον (tah-MAY-on), and we have never truly sat with a brother in his actual fire. The posture this conversation returned me to is one of full arrival with empty hands — genuinely still before God and before the suffering person across from me, willing to receive what the Spirit chooses to give rather than performing what I came prepared to offer. Samuel’s confession of pettiness in the middle of the call, and Brother Leon’s single word arriving without elaboration, both modeled this in different ways. The one laid himself bare. The other brought only what he was given. Both were more useful than any amount of pre-packaged spiritual wisdom. Second, the translation of γέεννα (gheh-EN-nah) as “hell” is not a minor lexical dispute — it is a theological load-bearing error that has reshaped the gospel for nearly two millennia. When the primary motivation for following Christ becomes fear of punishment rather than reverential awe of a Father who will appoint a fish, a worm, a shade-giving plant, and an unanswered question at the end of a book just to reach one rebellious prophet — the entire shape of discipleship is distorted. It produces people who comply rather than people who are transformed. It produces Jonahs who complete their mission half-heartedly and then sit on hillsides resenting the mercy they were used to convey. The remedy is not more programs or curriculum reforms — it is slower, more honest engagement with the whole counsel of God, including the geographic and historical realities behind the images, the original-language texture of the words, and the canonical portrait of a Father whose חֶסֶד (HEH-sed) consistently outruns our mapping of it. Third, the appearance of the chief priests in Matthew 27 is a permanent warning about the gap between theological literacy and spiritual formation. These were not ignorant men. They had memorized the Torah. They knew the prophets word for word. And they used that knowledge to orchestrate the destruction of the One the entire canon was pointing toward. The possession of theological information is not evidence of spiritual formation. The academy is not the same as the anointing. And the capacity for deeply sincere religious community to choose the wrong thing — for deeply sincere theological reasons — while the Spirit is pressing toward something else, is not merely a first-century problem. In our generation, the distortion tends to be softer: a gospel of self-actualization, a theology of comfort, a spirituality of agreement and affirmation that never summons anyone toward μετάνοια (meh-TAH-noy-ah). The false prophecy of our age does not always announce itself loudly. Sometimes it simply substitutes Barabbas — the life of self-managed autonomy — for the Lamb, and the crowd applauds. Fourth, Pilate’s hand-washing is a warning to every person who knows the right thing and refuses to do it because the social cost is too high. Pilate was not deceived about Jesus. He was cowardly about Jesus — and there is a particular and terrible spiritual danger in the person who has seen enough to know better and still capitulates to the crowd. His wife received a dream. His conscience was active. The evidence before him was unambiguous. And he washed his hands and handed Jesus over anyway. The blood is not cleansed by the ceremony of denial. We carry the weight of what we know and refuse to act on, and the liturgy of plausible deniability does not relieve us of it. Fifth, what Samuel asked near the end of that call — almost in passing, almost experimentally — about the house of David and what it transitioned into, was not peripheral. It was prophetically central. A man who knows he carries the inheritance of the greater Son of David is not a man who can be permanently destabilized by a prom photograph or a curated ring. Identity forged in revelation — in the kind of cracked-open encounter with the living God that this conversation was pressing toward — is categorically more durable than identity forged through emotional processing alone. The question was a prophetic fragment groping toward the surface, and the Spirit was pressing it through the wound for a reason. We need to develop ears for those fragments — to catch them in the fire, hold them, and bring them back to the person when the storm has quieted enough to study them properly. Finally, the concept of severe mercy deserves more serious theological attention in our communities than it currently receives. We have inherited, in much of contemporary Western Christianity, a subtle version of prosperity theology that frames suffering as a sign of spiritual failure or insufficient faith. But the canon teaches the opposite: that the deepest formation happens under the heaviest pressure, that the most faithful servants often carry the longest silences, and that the Father’s παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) is the mark of sonship, not its absence. If we cannot preach this truthfully — if we cannot say to a man in the middle of his Jonah darkness that the fish is mercy and the shore is real — we will continue producing disciples who collapse when the east wind comes, because no one told them the wind was part of the design. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity is not the achievement of emotional stability or the accumulation of theological comprehensiveness — it is the deepening capacity to bring what is actually inside us into the presence of the One who is actually large enough to hold it, and to remain there until He speaks. Samuel’s question about the house of David was not fully formed, but it was pointing in the right direction: *who am I, actually? What am I made of? What cannot be taken?* Maturity is the slow process of allowing those answers to come not from our circumstances but from the Word and the Spirit — even, especially, when the circumstances are screaming something else entirely. The man who can confess his pettiness aloud and the man who can receive a single prophetic word without elaboration and the man who can wait for his plant and his worm with his face toward the wind — these are all, in their different moments, demonstrating the same thing: a soul being genuinely restructured by encounter rather than merely informed by content. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this meditation calls forth is specific and immediate. Reconcile with your brother before you bring your gift to the altar — whatever form that altar takes. Read the overlooked passages: Barabbas, Lamentations, Jonah’s hillside, the minor prophets who hold the canonical vocabulary for the dark seasons the modern church rarely names. Find yourself in the figures you would rather observe than inhabit. Enter the ταμεῖον (tah-MAY-on) with empty hands. Stay in the courtyard of the Barabbas story long enough to feel the weight of the exchange before rushing to the resurrection. Refuse to grant the enemy’s framing — which says that the wound is the final word — and insist instead that the One who makes all things new has not yet spoken His final word over this situation, over this territory, over these men and their fractured covenants and their fishbelly darknesses. Kingdom obedience in this season looks like intercession, presence, honest prayer, and the refusal to spiritually abandon a brother because his pain is messy and his language is unfiltered. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation establishes is one of communal availability and empty-handed waiting. Brother Leon modeled it: he questioned whether he had heard correctly, received the same word a second time, delivered it plainly, and stepped back. That is prophetic precision in its most humble and effective form — not protecting his own spiritual comfort, not waiting for a more prepared moment, but being the kind of person who can be summoned into another’s crisis and arrive with a word rather than a performance. We are being trained, in seasons like these, to become that kind of people: those who develop ears for the prophetic fragments that surface in the most unguarded moments, who can catch them, hold them, and bring them back when the storm has quieted. A community that learns to hear in the fire — that cultivates the discipline of the ταμεῖον (tah-MAY-on) while also remaining accessible to the crisis of brothers — is the prophetic community the Kingdom is pressing us to become. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You now as one of Your servants, a co-heir of the Kingdom under the blood of Your Son, standing between the heavens and the earth and making agreement with Your will and Your word over every life that was pressed together in that conversation — and over every life that will encounter this reflection in whatever season they are carrying. I thank You that the covenant You have established cannot be unraveled by a photograph, a ring deployed as a weapon, a sleepless night, a belly of grief, or a valley of smoldering refuse. I thank You that You מָנָה (mah-NAH) the fish, the plant, the worm, and the wind — that every sovereign appointment in the darkest seasons is an act of חֶסֶד (HEH-sed) and not a withdrawal of it. I thank You that You did not require Jonah to perform peace before You carried him to the shore, and You will not require Samuel to perform peace before You carry him to his. I thank You that the house of David did not end in exile — that it was raised into the eternal throne of the Son of David, and that every man and woman who is hidden in Christ is a συγκληρονόμοι (sün-klā-ro-NO-moy) — a co-inheritor — of what that throne holds. I ask that the crack in every heart present in that call — the crack that pain has made, the aperture that grief has opened — would become exactly what it was designed to be: the place through which Your fullness enters rather than the wound through which identity is stolen. Silence every lying verdict. מָנָה (mah-NAH) the shore for every man still in the belly. Let the unanswered question at the end of every Jonah story land with the full weight of Your mercy: *Should I not pity them?* — the city, the cattle, the estranged, the enemy, the brother sitting in his anger on the hillside waiting for a destruction that Your heart has already turned from. And where I fell short in that conversation — where I moved past grief too quickly, where I was distracted, where I offered words when I should have simply remained — fill those gaps with what only You can give, because You are faithful, the line holds, the throne is eternal, and You are not content to lose what You have made. To the Son, through the Son, to the Father, by the πνεῦμα (NYOO-mah) who makes all encounter possible — be glory now and in the age to come.
