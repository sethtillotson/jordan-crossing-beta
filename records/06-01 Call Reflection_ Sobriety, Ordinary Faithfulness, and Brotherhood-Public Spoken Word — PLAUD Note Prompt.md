# 07-28 Reflection: The Sacrament of Small Things in Everyday Marriage

**Title:** 07-28 at 22:31 — The Sacrament of Small Things

**Recorded:** Not provided

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture)

**Classification:** Call Reflection

## Opening Observation

As I sit with this small, ordinary exchange between my wife and me — a scattering of hot dogs, cats, roast beef, tiredness, and tender goodbyes — something in me hesitates to call it insignificant. The Spirit keeps pressing a quiet question: what if the daily grind, the mundane affection, the silly banter over lunch under five dollars, is not the backdrop to a holy life but part of its very substance? There is a line near the end where one of us says these small moments make “the daily grind of the work and the life feel so good and peaceful,” and I cannot let it go.

**Why This Matters:**

So much of discipleship gets imagined as the mountaintop, the fast, the vision, the great commission moment. But the overwhelming majority of a faithful life is spent in exchanges exactly like this one — checking out at a gas station, tending an animal, telling a tired spouse “I love you” for no particular reason except that we should be saying it. If I cannot find God here, in the domestic liturgy of ordinary love, then I have misunderstood where He chose to dwell. This matters because the enemy of a maturing soul is not usually great sin; it is the quiet contempt for small, unglamorous faithfulness. The Body of Christ is built not primarily in cathedrals but in kitchens, cars, and casual “love you, bye” exchanges that reveal whether covenant love has become second nature.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

Read through natural reasoning, this is simply a couple of tired people making small talk at the end of a long day. One is constipated and dehydrated, headed to work; the other has settled for a cheap gas-station lunch instead of prepping healthier food. There is mild domestic friction — a warning about spending, a nod that the hot dogs aren’t healthy, a cat who bit someone and “should be in kitty jail.” A psychologist might note the affection regulation, the playful teasing as a bonding mechanism, the way couples soften fatigue through shared silliness. Nothing here demands interpretation beyond ordinary human companionship and the low-grade stress of adult life.

**The Limitation:**

Yet the natural mind, for all its accuracy about the surface, has no category for the sacred weight of the ordinary. It can measure the calories in a hot dog but cannot weigh the covenant in a “why not, we should be saying it.” It sees fatigue and errands and mild irritation, but it cannot perceive that these are the actual materials God uses to sanctify two people bound together. The flesh always hunts for significance in the dramatic and overlooks the holiness hidden in repetition — the same blindness that could stand before a carpenter’s son and see only a tradesman from Nazareth. What is spiritually discerned is not the presence of something extraordinary, but the presence of the Extraordinary One within the unremarkable.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

As I listen back to this exchange, the first thing that arrests me is how much of it is play. My wife jokes about her poop, teases me that if I loved her I would want to see it, and turns the cat’s antics into a little song — “she’s a sassy girl in a squashy world.” The Spirit reminds me that joy is not the opposite of holiness; it is often its fruit. “A merry heart does good, like medicine” (Proverbs 17:22), and I notice that this laughter is medicine precisely for the fatigue and the dehydration and the work that waits. God is not offended by the lightness of a marriage that can still be silly after years together. He is the one who fills the mouth “with laughter” and the tongue “with singing” (Psalm 126:2).

Then I notice the tenderness folded inside the teasing. “I thought you love me!” she says, half-playing, and later, almost surprised, “I guess I love you long, why not?” And I answer, “Yeah? Why not, we should be saying it?” Something in me recognizes that this is not throwaway speech. Words carry creative weight in the economy of God, because we are made in the image of the One who spoke worlds into being. The Hebrew דָּבָר (dabar, dah-VAR) — word — means far more than sound; it means word-as-event, speech that accomplishes. When I say “I love you” without occasion, I am not merely reporting a feeling; I am building a reality, reinforcing a covenant, speaking a small creation into the room.

I am seeing that the marriage bond itself is being quietly honored here. There is a moment where she thanks someone “for loving me,” and the whole tenor of the call is safety — the freedom to be tired, constipated, silly, mildly annoyed at the cat, and still deeply held. This is a shadow of the greater mystery. Paul takes the ordinary institution of marriage and declares, “This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church” (Ephesians 5:32). Every unglamorous act of a husband loving a wife, of a wife trusting a husband, rehearses the gospel in miniature. The Spirit presses me: the way I love Gladys in these small, tired moments is a sermon whether or not anyone else ever hears it.

I cannot ignore how tired she is — “a little bit tired,” “I’m going to work,” “I have to drink more water, I’m so dehydrated.” And I feel the Lord’s gentleness toward the weary. He does not despise the worn-out saint who still gets up and goes to the job. He is the one who said, “Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest” (Matthew 11:28). The Greek there, κοπιάω (kopiaō, ko-pee-AH-oh), means to labor to the point of exhaustion. There is dignity, not shame, in a life poured out through daily work. The gospel does not rescue us from the ordinary grind; it fills the ordinary grind with a Presence that makes it bearable, even peaceful.

That word peaceful stops me. “It makes the daily grind of the work and the life feel so good and peaceful.” Here is a testimony hidden in a throwaway line. The peace of God is not the absence of pooping cats and cheap lunches and looming shifts; it is a possession that operates within them. εἰρήνη (eirēnē, ay-RAY-nay) — peace — in the biblical sense is not merely calm but wholeness, the ordered flourishing of shalom. And Jesus says His peace is unlike the world’s: “not as the world gives do I give to you” (John 14:27). The world gives peace by removing pressures; Christ gives peace that persists beneath them. What I hear in that line is a soul that has learned, however unconsciously, to be content.

I am also convicted, gently, by the frugality thread. “I love when lunch is less than five dollars,” I say, and she counters that maybe I should food-prep, that buying hot dogs all the time isn’t healthy or wise. There is wisdom in her correction, and I notice how the Spirit uses a spouse as a means of grace — “iron sharpens iron” (Proverbs 27:17). But I also hear the deeper contentment beneath my delight in a cheap, filling meal: “having food and clothing, with these we shall be content” (1 Timothy 6:8). A jalapeno cheddar hot dog and a dollar-twenty-five soda become, in the Kingdom, an occasion for gratitude rather than complaint.

Even the cat’s discipline preaches. “He attacked Nona. That’s why I’m mad at him. He should be in kitty jail.” I smile, but I notice the moral instinct underneath — that harm has consequences, that love and correction coexist, that even in a household there must be order. This is a faint echo of the God who is both merciful and just, who disciplines those He loves (Hebrews 12:6) not from cruelty but for restoration. Order in the small household is a fractal of order in the household of God.

And I keep returning to the animals themselves — Sassy, Nanya, the busy little creatures underfoot. “She’s very busy,” we say of the cat, and there is delight in simply watching a creature be what God made it to be. I am reminded that “the earth is the LORD’s, and all its fullness” (Psalm 24:1), and that a home full of small living things is a small Eden entrusted to us to tend. Adam’s first vocation was to name and keep the creatures. When I greet the kitty — “Good kitty” — I am, in a tiny way, still keeping the garden.

What the Spirit is ultimately unveiling is this: incarnation. The God who could have remained distant chose to enter the ordinary — eating, sleeping, laughing at weddings, walking dusty roads, telling tired friends He loved them. “The Word became flesh and dwelt among us” (John 1:14). If my Lord sanctified the mundane by living it, then my mundane is not the enemy of my spiritual life; it is the very field of it. The gas station, the roast beef, the shared exhaustion, the “love you, bye” — these are not distractions from holiness. They are, when offered up, the holiness itself.

Finally, I hear the goodbye stretch on — “Okay, bye… love you… bye… why not, we should be saying it.” The reluctance to hang up is its own small parable. Love does not want to let go. And I think of how the Father is toward us: slow to release us, delighting to hear from us, never treating our small daily words as a bother. If a tired married couple can linger over a goodbye, how much more does the Lord linger over the prayers of His children, even the distracted, weary, four-word ones.

## Implications and Challenges

By God’s kindness, this ordinary call is an invitation to see rightly — so let me receive its challenges as grace, not scolding.

**The idol of the significant.** Much of modern Western Christianity is addicted to impact, platform, and the dramatic testimony. This exchange quietly rebukes that hunger. The saint who cannot love faithfully in a boring Tuesday has not yet learned the shape of the cross, which was, in its own way, a long obedience through unglamorous suffering.

**The suspicion of joy.** Some pious instincts treat laughter as unspiritual and silliness as immature. Yet Scripture fills the mouth of the redeemed with singing. A marriage that can still play is displaying a fruit of the Spirit, not a lapse from seriousness. I am challenged not to sanctify grimness and call it reverence.

**The neglect of ordinary words.** We say “I love you” less than we should, saving affection for occasions. But דָּבָר (dah-VAR) builds worlds. The challenge here is to become lavish with covenant speech — to say it “long, why not,” because words spoken in love are never wasted.

**The gospel of productivity.** The pressure to food-prep, to optimize, to eat healthier, to work harder is not evil, but it can quietly enthrone efficiency over gratitude. The challenge is to hold wisdom (she is right that hot dogs aren’t a diet) and contentment (I am right to give thanks for a cheap, filling meal) together, without letting either become an idol.

**The despising of weariness.** A culture that shames tiredness or a spirituality that treats exhaustion as faithlessness both miss the tender heart of Christ toward the κοπιάω (ko-pee-AH-oh), the labor-worn. The challenge is to bring my fatigue to Him openly, and to be gentle with my tired spouse as He is gentle with me.

And so the correction lands softly: God is not waiting for me on some future mountaintop. He is already here, in the kitchen, in the car, in the goodbye — asking only that I notice Him.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

Maturity is not measured by how spiritual my extraordinary moments feel but by how much of Christ has soaked into my ordinary ones. I grow when I stop waiting for a “real” spiritual life and begin offering the gas station, the roast beef, and the tired goodbye as worship. The mundane is the classroom of sanctification.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

Obedience here looks like small faithfulness: saying “I love you” without occasion, receiving my spouse’s correction with humility, tending the creatures and the home God gave me, giving thanks over a cheap lunch. “He who is faithful in what is least is faithful also in much” (Luke 16:10). The Kingdom is advanced in these hidden acts.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

To watch and wait is to develop eyes that see God in the unremarkable — to discern the sacred weight of a laugh, a leftover, a linger over goodbye. The prophetic saint is not only one who sees the future; he is one who sees the present truly, recognizing Immanuel in the middle of an ordinary day and testifying that He was there.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I thank You that You are not far off, but near — nearer than the hot dog on my lap or the tiredness in my wife’s voice, dwelling in the small liturgy of our shared days. As one of Your servants, standing between heaven and earth, I make agreement with Your will that our home be a small Eden and our marriage a living parable of Christ and His Church. Teach me to say “I love you” long and often, to give thanks over cheap and simple things, to be gentle with the weary, and to find Your εἰρήνη (ay-RAY-nay) not beyond the daily grind but planted inside it. Thank You for laughter, for a sassy cat, for roast beef and jalapeno cheddar, for a spouse who corrects and cherishes me, and for the reluctant sweetness of every goodbye. Let me tend the ordinary as holy ground, for You have filled it with Yourself. In the name of Jesus, the Word made flesh who dwelt among us, amen.
