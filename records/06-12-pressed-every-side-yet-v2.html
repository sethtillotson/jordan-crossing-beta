# 06-12 Call Reflection: Pressed On Every Side, Yet Not Crushed — Samuel & Spiritual Endurance **Title:** 06-12 at 10:38 — Pressed On Every Side, Yet Not Crushed **Recorded:** 2026-06-12 10:38:46 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation Listening back to this call with my brother Samuel, I am struck by how much Kingdom weight passed through what sounded — on the surface — like ordinary banter between two friends navigating life on a shoestring. Somewhere between the absurdity of a crack-sales business plan and the genuine tenderness of a man driving across the country to clean out his late grandfather’s home, I heard something the Spirit would not let me pass over: a man pressed on all sides, and yet — still holding. That phrase I spoke almost in passing, referencing Paul’s declaration in 2 Corinthians 4:8, was not accidental. The Spirit has a way of surfacing the most precise word in the middle of the most unlikely conversation. **Why This Matters:** There is a theology of limitation that the Western church has largely abandoned. We have traded the spiritually formative pressure of *not enough* — not enough money, not enough options, not enough comfort — for a gospel of perpetual increase and uninterrupted flourishing. But Scripture does not promise the absence of the press; it promises survival through it. If the saints cannot find God in the involuntary, the inconvenient, and the stripped-down seasons, they will only ever know Him in the comfortable ones — and that is a dangerously shallow discipleship. What passed between Samuel and me on this call, beneath the humor and the chaos, was a quiet testimony to something the Spirit uses constantly: ordinary pressure as the context for extraordinary faithfulness. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind listening to this conversation would see two young men doing what young men do — commiserating about money problems, cracking jokes to cope, discussing AI tools and business metrics, navigating the small frustrations of technology and traffic and grief. A psychologist might note the humor as a healthy coping mechanism, a way of processing financial stress and interpersonal instability without descending into despair. A career counselor might focus on Seth’s practical decision to speak with his manager and begin exploring closer job options — a sensible, self-preserving pivot. A sociologist might observe the transatlantic friendship as a product of digital connectivity, two men from different economic and cultural contexts finding common ground through shared entrepreneurial ambition and casual candor. None of these observations are wrong. They simply see the surface. They account for what is visible without perceiving what is being woven underneath. **The Limitation:** The natural mind is not equipped to recognize the moment when the Holy Spirit uses a man rolling a joint at thirty miles per hour, and another man involuntarily sober due to an empty wallet, as the occasion for a genuine theological confession. It cannot hear the kairos weight in a half-laughed reference to being *pressed in on all sides, yet not crushed.* The Spirit moves in the ordinary and the undignified — in the same way He hovered over what appeared to be formless void before the first word of creation was spoken. To see only the chaos of this call is to miss the hovering. To see only the humor is to miss the intercession. There is a kind of spiritual perception that must be cultivated over time — a trained ear that hears covenant language even when it surfaces inside a joke, and recognizes the hand of God even when it is moving through the mechanics of a cracked phone that will not ring. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I keep returning to that moment when I said it — almost reflexively, almost as though the Spirit reached into the conversation and placed it there without ceremony: *pressed in on all sides, yet not crushed.* Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 4:8–9, “We are hard-pressed on every side, yet not crushed; we are perplexed, but not in despair; persecuted, but not forsaken; struck down, but not destroyed.” I was not preaching. I was not even teaching. I was half-laughing about being broke and involuntarily sober. And yet the Spirit used that exact frame — Paul’s most concentrated summary of apostolic endurance — to name what I was actually living. That is how the Spirit works. He does not wait for the pulpit. He speaks through the middle of the mundane. The Greek word Paul uses for *hard-pressed* is θλίβω (thlibō, THLEE-bo) — meaning to compress, to crowd in upon, to press from all sides as if squeezed through a narrow place. It is the same root behind θλίψις (thlipsis, THLEEP-sis), the word often translated *tribulation* or *affliction* throughout the New Testament. What I am noticing now, sitting with this, is that Paul does not describe a season of θλίβω (THLEE-bo) as a failure of faith or an absence of God’s favor. He describes it as the normative atmosphere in which the life of Christ is made visible through the clay jar of a human body. The press itself is the context for the revelation of treasure — as Paul makes clear in verse 7: “But we have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the excellence of the power may be of God and not of us.” I am not in a season of flourishing by any external measure. I am commuting over a hundred dollars’ worth of gas per week on a job that may not be sustainable. I am looking for work closer to home. I am financially stretched thin enough that even small habits have gone quiet. And yet — something in me recognized the press without collapsing under its name. That recognition is itself a fruit of the Spirit’s work. When Paul says *yet not crushed,* the Greek carries the weight of a man who has been squeezed but not obliterated — who has felt the walls close in but has not been swallowed. I know that feeling now in a way I could not have understood in a more comfortable season. What strikes me further is that Samuel’s intercession — offered almost comically as an invoice from him and God to be paid later — was nevertheless real. He said plainly that he and God had been intervening where they could. That is covenant language wearing casual clothes. The Greek word for intercession in Romans 8:26–27 is ὑπερεντυγχάνω (hyperentynchanō, hoo-per-en-TOON-khan-oh) — to meet with, to appeal on behalf of, to stand in the gap between one person and another. Samuel was doing that. He had been praying. He had been thinking about my situation. He had offered counsel — rest first, then consider your options — that was confirmed independently by my manager at work. That double confirmation landed on me in the moment, and I said so: “that’s confirmation enough for me.” The Spirit is not wasteful. When He confirms a word through two separate and unrelated witnesses, that is not coincidence. That is the principle of Deuteronomy 19:15 operating in the relational fabric of daily life. The moment when Samuel revealed they were driving to his grandfather’s house to clean it out — and I had not realized his grandfather had passed — was a sudden and unguarded window into grief. Samuel carried it lightly in the conversation, but grief carried lightly is still grief. Donovan was in the car. They had both taken the week off. These are men doing the quiet, unglamorous work of honoring the dead — going to clear a house, to close a chapter, to carry boxes and make decisions in rooms that smell like someone who is no longer there. There is something deeply covenantal about that task. In the Hebrew tradition, the care of the dead and the honoring of the household of the departed was not a burden to be outsourced — it was a sacred duty. Samuel and Donovan driving across the country to do that work together is a picture of covenant friendship that is far more weighty than it appeared in the moment I stumbled over it. I want to sit with the word that my manager and Samuel both used — rest. Rest first. Before strategy, before the job search, before the next move — rest. The Hebrew word for rest in the Old Testament is often נוּחַ (nuach, NOO-akh) — to settle, to remain, to come to a place of quiet after movement. This is the same root behind the name Noah — the one who brought relief to the earth after the flood. And the Sabbath rest given to Israel was not merely a weekly pause in productivity; it was a covenantal declaration that YHWH was the source of provision, not human striving. When two witnesses — one a spiritual brother, one a secular manager — both say *rest first,* the Spirit is saying something I need to hear: the next chapter begins not with more effort, but with a regrounding in the One who sustains. Even my involuntary sobriety — which I named with some self-deprecating humor — may be part of that regrounding. What I have been stripped of, the Spirit may be using to clear the frequency. The conversation drifted into a genuinely substantive exchange about artificial intelligence tools — which systems are best for which tasks, how the mixture-of-agents approach works, what the cost structures look like. On one level, this is simply two men who care about their work talking shop. But I am noticing something underneath it: both Samuel and I are actively building, even in the press. Neither of us has abandoned the vision. Neither of us has gone quiet. Samuel is still trying to get his website live; I am still writing volumes of books; we are still comparing notes on the tools that help us go further faster. There is something apostolic in that posture — the ability to continue building while θλίβω (THLEE-bo) is at work. Paul did not stop writing from prison. He did not stop planting from a jail cell. The press does not have to produce paralysis; it can produce clarified focus. Samuel’s business metrics — the ad split test, the cost per acquisition, the meeting-booking ratios, the projected ROI — were shared with genuine precision and real optimism. He has done the work. He knows the numbers. And he framed it correctly: the most important thing now is stabilization, finding the rhythm that makes the results repeatable. That is wisdom. And I find myself receiving it not merely as business counsel, but as a spiritual metaphor: the Kingdom does not only advance through breakthrough moments. It stabilizes through consistent, faithful repetition — through the kind of daily obedience that is not dramatic but is deeply formative. Stabilize the rhythm. Then scale it. That applies to intercession, to Scripture study, to discipleship, to every pattern of faithfulness that the Lord is building into this season. The call ended the way it began — warmly, practically, with a genuine blessing exchanged. “God bless, brother. Enjoy your day.” These are words that can become hollow through repetition, but between men who have actually been praying for each other, they carry weight. When Samuel said “be out the trenches soon,” I heard it as both an encouragement and a prophetic declaration — spoken casually, but not without the Spirit’s breath behind it. The trench is real. The press is real. The commute and the expense and the job search and the stripped-down season are all real. And God bless, brother — spoken by a man who has been standing in the gap — is not a pleasantry. It is a benediction. What I am walking away from this call holding is this: the most formative seasons are not always the most visible ones. They are often the ones that look like two guys on a call, laughing about crack business plans while one of them is driving to honor a dead grandfather and the other is figuring out how to afford gas. The Kingdom does not suspend itself for dignified circumstances. It advances through faithful people who keep building, keep praying, keep speaking the Word into one another’s lives — even when the phone almost didn’t ring, even when the wallet is empty, even when the grief is real and the house needs to be cleared. Pressed on every side. Not crushed. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation presses upon me — and upon any who will read it — is the temptation to suspend spiritual attentiveness until circumstances become more “suitable” for God to move. I nearly missed the weight of my own words in the middle of a joke. The Spirit does not wait for the atmosphere to become reverent before He speaks. He speaks in ordinary conversations, through casual phone calls, in the middle of traffic and grief and empty wallets. If I train myself to only hear God in structured devotional times, I will miss the rhema words He plants in the middle of the mundane. We must cultivate ears that hear across the full range of daily life, not only in the cathedral moments. A second challenge is to the prosperity-adjacent assumption that financial pressure is a sign of spiritual misalignment. This call was saturated with the reality of limited resources — and yet it was also saturated with intercession, covenant faithfulness, confirmed counsel, and the genuine advance of Kingdom vision. The absence of financial abundance was not evidence of divine absence. Some of the most spiritually alive seasons in Scripture were materially lean ones. Elijah under the juniper tree. Paul in the Mamertine prison. The disciples without bread in the boat. Scarcity is not always discipline; sometimes it is the very context the Spirit uses to strip the noise and clarify the call. Third, this call gently challenges the way we treat intercession as an optional add-on rather than a covenantal function. Samuel had been praying. He said it casually, wrapped in humor, but the reality was unmistakable — he had been standing in the gap. And the fruit of his intercession showed up as confirmed counsel, matched independently by another voice. The church needs to recover the understanding that prayer for one another is not a spiritual nicety; it is a covenantal act with measurable spiritual consequence. When the saints pray for one another with genuine intent, the Spirit coordinates confirmation, clarity, and direction in the lives of those being prayed for. Fourth, I want to name the grace that was present in Samuel’s willingness to still engage the call in the middle of his own grief. He was on his way to clean out a dead man’s house. He had every reason to be unavailable, distracted, or simply closed. And yet he picked up, he laughed, he counseled, he shared his metrics, he spoke blessing at the end. Grief does not have to produce spiritual withdrawal. Sometimes the most faithful thing a grieving person does is remain present to their brothers and sisters even while carrying loss. Samuel modeled that without fanfare, and it is worth honoring. Finally, there is a challenge here regarding the tendency to compartmentalize — to have a spiritual conversation and a business conversation and a personal conversation as separate, unrelated categories. This call refused that compartmentalization. Theology bled into business planning. Grief bled into laughter. Practical counsel bled into prophetic confirmation. The Kingdom does not compartmentalize. The Spirit moves across the whole of life, not just the labeled sacred portions. Those who learn to let these categories bleed together — who can receive confirmation in a phone call and pray over a financial spreadsheet and grieve and build and bless all in the same breath — are developing something rare: an integrated, Spirit-saturated life. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This meditation calls us to develop what might be called *covenantal attentiveness* — the practiced spiritual discipline of noticing the Spirit’s movement across the whole texture of ordinary life. Spiritual maturity is not measured by how eloquently we speak in structured settings, but by how faithfully we hear and respond to what the Spirit is doing in the unscripted, unguarded, and inconvenient moments. This means slowing down enough to receive confirmed counsel when it comes — from a brother’s prayer and a manager’s wisdom on the same day — and treating that convergence with the reverence it deserves rather than letting it pass as coincidence. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The immediate call to obedience this reflection surfaces is the call to *rest before the next move.* Two witnesses have spoken it. The Spirit has underlined it. Obedience here is not passivity — it is the active trust that says *I will not force the next chapter through striving when the Lord is calling me to settle before I advance.* This is נוּחַ (NOO-akh) as a Kingdom act. Additionally, the continuing faithfulness to build — to write, to plan, to compare tools, to stabilize rhythms — even inside the press, is the right obedience. Do not abandon the vision in the valley. Carry it faithfully through. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation cultivates is one of *expectant endurance* — the ability to hold the word of the Lord over a situation that has not yet resolved, without collapsing under the delay. Samuel’s declaration — “be out the trenches soon” — was received as a word, not merely a farewell. The prophetic community must learn to speak blessing and declaration into one another’s lives not only in formal settings, but across the ordinary channels of relationship. The phone call, the text, the casual sign-off — these are the vessels through which prophetic words often travel. Stay awake to them. Receive them. Let them anchor the soul during the press. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You with thanksgiving for the brother You placed on this call with me — for the man who was driving toward grief and yet remained present enough to speak life. I thank You that You do not wait for dignified circumstances before You move. You spoke through the casual and the comic, and underneath it all, I heard the Word: *pressed, but not crushed.* Lord, I receive that word now with full intentionality — not as a cliché but as a covenantal declaration over this season. I confess that the involuntary sobriety of limited resources is not a punishment but a pruning, and I yield to whatever stripping is necessary to keep my frequency clear before You. I receive the confirmed counsel to rest before the next move, and I trust that the next chapter will not be missed because I paused to settle. I pray for my brother Samuel — for the grief of clearing his grandfather’s house, for the faithfulness of the friendship he carries, for the business You are building through him. I stand in agreement, as a servant of the Living God, between heaven and earth, that what has been spoken in intercession over this season will be fulfilled — that the trenches will give way to the open field, that the press will produce the treasure, and that every thread of this ordinary season will be woven by Your hand into something that glorifies the name of Jesus. I pray it as one who holds the authority of the New Covenant, as a son of the Kingdom, as a co-heir with Christ — not by my own worthiness but by the blood of the Lamb who was pressed and crushed so that we would not be. To Him be glory and honor in this season, and in every season to come.
