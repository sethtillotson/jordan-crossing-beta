# 08-19 Call Reflection: Integrity and Keeping One’s Word in Business **Title:** 08-19 at 04:51 — Let Your Yes Be Yes: Integrity as the Currency of the Kingdom **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation A young brother from the other side of the world called me with a knot in his chest — money paid, work paused, promises repeated but never landing, and beneath it all the quiet dread of a Christian man who does not want to sell others something he is not sure of himself. I heard the ache of a promise deferred, and I heard him name the very thing that was troubling him with startling clarity: he does not want to mislead people. What arrests me is how the whole conversation kept circling back to a single ancient standard — let your yes be yes. This was not a business call about a lead-gen offer. This was a call about integrity, and about whether a man’s word means anything at all. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the integrity of our speech is not a business skill layered on top of our faith — it is the faith itself made visible in the marketplace. Jayden was not merely worried about fifteen hundred dollars; he was wrestling with whether the people around him could be trusted, and whether he himself would be made complicit in a pattern of deferred promises. When a brother says “as a Christian myself, it’s hard for me to even do that when I’m not sure where I stand,” he is testifying that his conscience will not let him separate his commerce from his Christ. For those of us who lead, teach, disciple, or run anything, this is a summons: the saints are watching whether our yes holds weight. Discipleship is not only what we say in the seventy percent faith-based conversations; it is whether the thirty percent business conversations bear the same fruit. The world learns whether Jesus is real partly by whether His people keep their word. And endurance in obedience means picking up the ball when a brother drops it, rather than letting a good man’s hard season become an excuse that wounds those depending on him. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation Read through natural reasoning, this is a straightforward business dispute with a compassionate edge. A customer paid into a mentorship-and-placement program, the placement never materialized, and after nearly three weeks of “we’ll get you on this offer” he is understandably frustrated and considering going back to his job. The manager running the operation, Samuel, is going through difficult home circumstances, which explains the slippage, and the natural resolution is managerial: apply pressure, set a firm timeline, or issue a refund. The emotional layer — Jayden’s fear of being scammed from across the world, his reluctance to sell something he doubts — reads as ordinary consumer anxiety and workplace ethics. In this frame, the kindest and most efficient move is to fix the operations, honor the guarantee, and keep a talented salesman from walking. It is a story about accountability, cash flow, and a leader stepping in to stabilize a wobbling venture. **The Limitation:** All of that is true, and none of it reaches the floor of what actually happened on that call. The natural mind can measure the dollars and diagnose the broken process, but it cannot see why a young man’s refusal to mislead strangers is a form of worship, or why keeping one’s word is a matter of bearing the divine image rather than merely maintaining a good reputation. It treats integrity as reputational capital — useful because it is profitable — and so it cannot perceive that integrity is holy because God Himself cannot lie. It hears “let your yes be yes” as a proverb about being reliable, when Jesus spoke those words to unveil a whole way of being in which speech is sacred and oaths are unnecessary because the whole life is transparent. The flesh optimizes the outcome; the Spirit examines the heart from which the words proceed. What Jayden was really guarding was not his investment but his conscience, and that is territory the natural mind does not know how to map. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I keep returning to the sound of his voice — apologizing, twice, three times, for even raising the concern. “I do apologize for bothering you with this stuff.” He is from the completely opposite side of the world, one o’clock in the afternoon his time, having put fifteen hundred Australian dollars into something, having taken a third week off work now, and the thing he keeps softening with apology is the simple, honest question: where is the work? There is something tender and disarming in a man who is being let down and still worries that his honesty might come across as confrontational. He does not want to be difficult. He wants things to work out. He wants to keep the connections and build on them. And underneath the courtesy is a real fear, plainly spoken: “Well, crap, am I going to get scammed?” What arrests me is the sentence he almost buried in the middle of it all — that as a Christian, he finds it hard to sell the mentorship to others when he himself is not sure where he stands, because he does not want to be misleading people. He was being asked, “just for the time being,” to sell to others the very promise that had not yet been kept to him. And his conscience recoiled. That recoil is the fear of God alive in a young man. He would rather go back to his ordinary job than pass along to a stranger the same deferred hope that was aching in his own chest. That is not consumer anxiety. That is a man refusing to become a link in a chain of unfulfilled words. The moment we both named it, the whole conversation found its center. “Let your yes be yes and your no be no.” I said it, he agreed, and it was as if we had both touched the load-bearing beam of the entire matter. That phrase is not mine and it is not merely wisdom about business; it is the Lord’s own teaching. In Matthew 5:37, Jesus says, “But let your ‘Yes’ be ‘Yes,’ and your ‘No,’ ‘No.’ For whatever is more than these is from the evil one.” He is teaching that the disciple has no need to swear oaths, because the disciple’s ordinary speech should already carry the full weight of truth. The reason we reach for oaths and guarantees and “I promise, this time for real” is precisely because our plain word has lost its density. Jesus is calling His people to a speech so honest that it needs no reinforcement. The word beneath this is worth sitting with. When James takes up his brother’s teaching, he writes in James 5:12, “let your ‘Yes’ be ‘Yes,’ and your ‘No,’ ‘No,’ lest you fall into judgment.” The Greek there for the doubled affirmation is ναί (nai, NYE) — the simple “yes,” the true affirmation. James warns that when our nai stops meaning nai, we do not merely disappoint a customer; we fall ὑπὸ κρίσιν (hypo krisin, hoo-PO KREE-sin) — under judgment. The unkept word is not a small thing in heaven’s accounting. It is the kind of thing that draws us into the sifting of God, because it is a small betrayal of the God whose every promise is Yes. And that is the deepest current here, the one the natural mind never reaches. Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 1:20, “For all the promises of God in Him are Yes, and in Him Amen, to the glory of God through us.” Every promise God has ever made finds its Yes in Christ. This is who our God is — a God who does not defer, who does not say “next week, next week, next week” and let the weeks pile up into silence. His faithfulness is His nature. So when Jayden guards his own yes, when he refuses to sell a promise he cannot personally underwrite, he is unknowingly imaging the God whose Yes is unbreakable. The reason integrity is “the number one currency for business,” as I put it to him, is far older and deeper than commerce: it is because we are made in the image of the God who keeps covenant to a thousand generations. There is another thread the Spirit is pulling, and it is the harder one. Samuel is a good man in a genuinely hard season — home-life burdens, real weight, the kind of thing that would slow anyone. Jayden said he sympathizes, and so do I. But then Jayden said the thing that must be honored: “Just because I’m going through life, does that mean the work stops or the world stops?” I told him what I learned in the fire of my own divorce, when I was running fifteen businesses at once — the world did not stop for me. People had sympathy, they did not slap me with fees, they understood. But after four months, love itself required them to say: you still have responsibilities; there are people who depend on you. That is not hardness. That is what Galatians 6:2 and 6:5 hold together in tension — “Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ,” and yet “each one shall bear his own load.” We carry each other’s crushing weights; we do not carry each other’s daily duties. Compassion that never calls a brother back to his own load is not mercy; it is a slow abandonment of the people that brother is responsible for. Here the ecclesial tension opens. A hard season is real, and a hard season is not a license. The gospel does not make excuses; it makes atonement and then it makes men new. When we allow a leader’s suffering to become an unspoken permission to let promises slide, we are not being gracious to him — we are actually withholding from him the honor of being called up into his calling. The most loving thing I can do for Samuel is not to cover the pattern but to name it gently and help him meet it. Proverbs 27:6 says, “Faithful are the wounds of a friend.” I told Jayden I would get on Samuel’s butt about it, and I meant it as an act of brotherhood, not accusation. To let a good man quietly become a man whose word no longer lands would be the real betrayal. The Kingdom mystery hiding in this ordinary phone call is that God was using a frustrated young salesman to preach the gospel back to the whole operation. Jayden had no idea he was doing theology. He thought he was asking about a lead-gen PPA offer. But the Spirit was speaking through his conscience, saying: the way you conduct this business is a testimony about the character of God. The refund I offered, the timeline I promised to chase down, the call I said I would make to Samuel when he woke fresh — all of that only matters if it is actually done. My own yes, given on that call, is now on the scales. The revelation is that God had positioned this conversation as a test of whether the men leading would themselves live the standard the young man was clinging to. And so this lands on me, personally and directly. I told him, “If we say we’re going to do something, we’ve got to make sure that we do it.” I meant it, and now the Lord will watch whether I mean it. I have “seven more hours of work” and “a ton of stuff going on in my life” — I said as much — and the temptation of every busy man is to let a promise made in a hard moment quietly evaporate under the pressure of everything else. But I am the one who said it is my responsibility to pick up the ball if Samuel drops it. The Spirit is pressing me here: do not become, toward Jayden, the very pattern Jayden was grieving. My yes to him about calling Samuel is a small covenant, and small covenants are exactly where a man’s fidelity to God is proven or exposed. He who is faithful in little is faithful in much. For the Body, this is a plain and searching word. We are quick to compartmentalize — the faith conversations over here, the business conversations over there, as though Jesus is Lord of the seventy percent and merely a consultant on the thirty. But there is no such division in the Kingdom. Colossians 3:23 says, “And whatever you do, do it heartily, as to the Lord and not to men.” The way we treat a brother’s investment, a customer’s expectation, a client’s time — this is worship or it is a lie, and there is very little in between. The beloved brethren who work and trade and build together are meant to be a community where a man from the opposite side of the world can send his money and his trust and not lie awake wondering if he is being scammed. That is a witness to the watching world of a different economy altogether. What moves me most is that Jayden closed the call not with a threat but with a blessing — “God bless you, man, I can’t wait to eventually meet you in person.” Even in his frustration, he was reaching for fellowship, for the connection, for the hope that these are good people worth staying bound to. I answered him honestly: for that to happen, we’ve got to fix whatever’s going on here. That is the heart of it. Reconciliation and fellowship are not sustained by warm feelings alone; they are sustained by kept promises. The unity of the brethren rests on the trustworthiness of the brethren. If we want to meet one another face to face with joy, we must first be people whose word can be trusted from across the world. ## Implications and Challenges **The compartmentalized gospel must fall.** I described my talks with Samuel as thirty percent business and seventy percent faith and home life, “kind of like discipleship.” But this call revealed that the business thirty percent *is* the discipleship, not a break from it. Modern Western Christianity has grown comfortable with a faith that speaks fluently in prayer meetings and falls silent in the invoice, the contract, the deadline. Yet Jesus made no such division — He located our integrity precisely in our everyday “Yes” and “No.” The challenge here is to stop treating our commerce as a neutral zone and to let Christ be Lord over the terms of every deal we make. **Compassion without accountability is a counterfeit mercy.** Samuel’s hard season is real, and both Jayden and I honored it sincerely. But there is a soft, culturally acceptable distortion that treats any personal struggle as an automatic suspension of responsibility, and it is not the mercy of God — it is a slow harm to everyone depending on the struggling man. Galatians 6 holds burden-bearing and load-carrying together without contradiction. The loving move is not to cover the pattern of deferred promises but to name it as a friend, as Proverbs 27:6 says the faithful wounds of a friend do. To spare Samuel the correction would be to abandon both him and Jayden. **The unkept word is a spiritual matter, not merely a professional lapse.** James 5:12 warns that when our yes stops meaning yes, we fall under judgment — ὑπὸ κρίσιν (hoo-PO KREE-sin). We tend to file broken promises under poor time management or unfortunate circumstances, softening them into something forgivable and small. But Scripture treats the collapse of plain speech as a serious thing, because it distorts our witness to a God whose promises are all Yes and Amen. The challenge is to feel the weight heaven assigns to a deferred promise, and to repent of the casualness with which we let our commitments slide. **A young believer’s tender conscience is not weakness to be managed but fire to be honored.** Jayden was asked to sell the mentorship “for the time being,” and his conscience recoiled because he would not mislead others. In a results-driven culture, that hesitation could be read as unhelpful scruple. But it is the fear of God, and it is precisely the kind of conscience the Body should protect and celebrate, never override. When we ask people to sell what we have not yet delivered, we are asking them to wound their own conscience for our cash flow, and that is a grievous thing. **My own yes is now on the scales, and busyness is no excuse.** I promised to call Samuel, to chase the timeline, to figure out a solution “so we don’t feel this way.” I also mentioned seven more hours of work and a life full of demands. The most common way good men break their word is not by malice but by letting the pressure of everything else quietly dissolve a promise made in a sincere moment. Luke 16:10 says he who is faithful in little is faithful in much. The challenge is intensely personal: I must not become, toward this brother, the very pattern he was grieving. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity is measured in the density of our word. A growing disciple is one whose plain “yes” carries the full weight of a signed oath, because his life has become transparent before God and man. This call invites us to examine every place where our speech has grown light — the promise we soften, the timeline we keep pushing, the “we’ll get to it” we no longer intend to honor. To mature in Christ is to become a person whose commitments cost us something to make and therefore mean something to receive. It is to let the God whose every promise is Yes reshape our mouths until our nai truly means nai. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience here is concrete and unglamorous: make the call, keep the promise, name the pattern in love, and honor the refund if the value cannot be delivered. It calls forth leaders who pick up the ball when it is dropped rather than letting a brother’s hard season wound the people depending on him. It requires us to refuse the compartment that keeps Jesus out of our contracts and to do our business heartily, as to the Lord. And it means protecting the tender conscience of a young believer rather than asking him to bruise it for our benefit. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture trains us to hear the Spirit speaking through an ordinary frustrated phone call — to recognize that God was preaching the gospel of His own faithfulness through a young salesman who only thought he was asking about work. The saints who watch and discern will learn to see the sacred standard hiding inside the mundane transaction, and to testify to a God whose Yes never defers. It teaches us to wait on the Lord for how to speak correction — I chose to catch Samuel “fresh awake and feeling good” — so that our faithful wounds heal rather than merely cut. And it forms in us a fidelity so reliable that a brother from the opposite side of the world can trust his money, his time, and his hope to our word. ## A Closing Prayer Father, You are the God whose every promise in Christ is Yes and Amen, and I come as Your servant and co-heir, standing between the heavens and the earth on behalf of these two brothers. I thank You for a young man whose conscience would not let him mislead a stranger, and I ask You to guard that fire in Jayden and let it never grow cold. I lift up Samuel in his hard season — carry the burden that is too heavy for him, Lord, and at the same time call him back with kindness to the load that is his to bear, that his yes would once again mean yes. Search me, Father, for I made a promise on that call, and I ask that You would not let the weight of my own busy life dissolve the word I gave. Make me faithful in this little thing, that I might be trusted with much. Where there has been a pattern of deferred promises, let there be repentance and repair, and let no one in this venture fall under judgment for a light word. Teach us as beloved brethren to let Jesus be Lord of our commerce as fully as our prayers, so that the watching world would see in us a different economy — an economy of kept covenants and honest speech. Let the connections Jayden longs to build be built on the rock of trustworthiness, and grant that we might one day meet face to face with joy because our word held. To You be the glory, God who does not lie, God whose faithfulness endures to a thousand generations. Amen. claude-opus-4.8 ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |--------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Matthew 5:37 | ναί (nai, NYE) — yes / true affirmation | Jesus locates integrity in plain speech; the disciple needs no oath because his ordinary word already carries full truth. | | James 5:12 | ὑπὸ κρίσιν (hypo krisin, hoo-PO KREE-sin) — under judgment | The collapse of a trustworthy “yes” is not a minor lapse but draws one under God’s sifting; kept speech is a spiritual, not merely social, matter. | | 2 Corinthians 1:20 | ναί (NYE) | Every promise of God finds its Yes in Christ; human integrity images the covenant-keeping character of God Himself. | | Galatians 6:2, 6:5 | — | “Bear one another’s burdens” and “each shall bear his own load” held together: we carry each other’s crushing weights, not each other’s daily duties. | | Proverbs 27:6 | — | “Faithful are the wounds of a friend” — loving correction of a struggling brother is brotherhood, not accusation. | | Colossians 3:23 | — | All work is to be done heartily as to the Lord, abolishing the divide between “faith” conversations and “business” conversations. | | Luke 16:10 | — | Faithfulness in the little promise proves and forms faithfulness in the great; small covenants are where fidelity to God is tested. |
