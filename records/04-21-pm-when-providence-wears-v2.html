# 04-22 Call Reflection: Spiritual Discernment in a Business Opportunity with Brazilian Associates **Title:** 04-21 at 12:33 PM — When Providence Wears a Phone Call and a Brazilian Accent **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I am sitting with a brief, almost unremarkable phone call — caught mid-flight, mid-travel, mid-day — and yet something in me will not let it pass without reflection. A man named Samuel reached out, introduced two men from Brazil, floated a business opportunity, and invoked the name of God over the whole of it. What strikes me is not the proposal itself, but the layering: providence language wrapped around commercial ambition, faith invoked in the middle of a pitch, and a quiet, almost instinctive sobriety in my own responses that I now find worth examining. There is something here worth pressing into — not to inflate a phone call into prophecy, but to ask what a discerning spirit does when enthusiasm and earnestness arrive together at the door. **Why This Matters:** The saints are not called to be suspicious of every opportunity, nor are they called to be naive. The Body of Christ moves constantly in a world where business, money, relationship, and Kingdom language all occupy the same space at the same time — sometimes legitimately, sometimes not, and often somewhere in the complex middle. How we hold conversations like this one, how we respond to the invoking of God’s name over financial proposals, how we remain open without becoming reckless — these are not peripheral concerns of the disciple. They are central to what it means to walk in wisdom, to be in the world but not shaped by its reflexes, and to honor the name of God by not lending it to things prematurely or carelessly. If the saints do not learn to hold enthusiasm at arm’s length while keeping their hearts genuinely open, they will oscillate between cynicism and credulity, and neither posture serves the Kingdom. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the vantage point of natural reasoning, a call like this one is straightforward enough to categorize: a friend with an entrepreneurial spirit reaches out, introduces a couple of eager young men, floats a promising-sounding business model, and asks for a consultation. The numbers mentioned — twenty-seven thousand in a day — are the kind of figures designed to quicken the pulse and bypass the slower rhythms of careful thought. Natural reasoning would process this along two predictable lines: either excitement, because the numbers are compelling and the connection feels warm and relational, or defensiveness, because the combination of urgency, unfamiliar names, and large financial figures carries the pattern of things that often disappoint. The world’s wisdom, in this case, would likely land on either enthusiastic agreement or protective skepticism, and it would reach whichever conclusion quickly, driven more by temperament and past experience than by anything deeper. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind, even at its most cautious and experienced, is still operating from a framework that treats discernment as essentially a risk-assessment function — a calculation of probabilities, a reading of social cues, a pattern-matching exercise drawn from the memory of past outcomes. What it cannot do is perceive what the Spirit is actually doing beneath and around a moment like this. It cannot distinguish between an open door and a test of openness. It cannot hear what God may be forming in the character of the one receiving the call — the patience, the sobriety, the willingness to say *we’ll see* rather than *yes* or *no* before the time. It cannot recognize that the invocation of God’s name over a business opportunity is itself a moment requiring theological attention, not just social grace. The senses trained by human experience alone will miss the deeper question the Spirit is asking through an exchange like this one. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with this conversation, the first thing I notice is my own voice. There is a steadiness in it that I do not want to overlook. When Samuel says, *“God put Matthew and Lucas in my path for a reason — trust this,”* my response is not agreement and it is not dismissal. It is simply: *“We’ll see. We’ll find out.”* That is not unbelief. That is the posture of a man who has learned, however partially, that not every invocation of divine purpose is a word from the Lord — and that genuine faith does not require instant confirmation of what someone else has declared prophetically over a business arrangement. I am pressing into that instinct here, because I think it is more theologically significant than it first appears. The Book of Proverbs, that great collection of Spirit-breathed wisdom for navigating the ordinary intersections of life and decision, offers a word that feels immediately relevant: *“The simple believes every word, but the prudent considers well his steps”* (Proverbs 14:15). Prudence here is not coldness. It is not the refusal to be generous or open. It is the trained capacity to let a thing unfold before calling it proven. The Hebrew word beneath *prudent* is עָרוּם (*arum*, ah-ROOM), meaning shrewd or sensible — the same word used of the serpent in Genesis 3, but redeemed in Proverbs into a faculty that belongs rightly to the righteous. Wisdom reclaims what the enemy distorted. The saint is to be shrewd — not toward manipulation, but toward truth. What I notice in myself during this call is that I am not cold toward Samuel or toward Matthew or toward Lucas. I greet Lucas warmly. I mention that I once lived with people from Brazil. There is genuine human warmth in the exchange. And yet I do not absorb Samuel’s enthusiasm as my own. I do not echo his certainty. I hold open the door — *“Sure, I can do a conversation”* — without walking through it as though the room has already been decorated. That balance, I am coming to understand, is not a small thing. It is the narrow road between the ditch of suspicious withdrawal and the ditch of undiscerning openness, and it requires the work of the Spirit to walk it steadily. There is also something worth sitting with in the fact that God’s name is invoked early and often in this call. Samuel says, *“God bless your flight,”* and *“Godspeed,”* and *“God put Matthew and Lucas in my path for a reason.”* I do not question Samuel’s sincerity. But I am aware that the invocation of divine purpose over a business opportunity is one of the more common ways that the line between genuine faith and motivated reasoning becomes blurred — not always through dishonesty, but through the very human tendency to baptize what we are already excited about. The Scripture warns about this pattern with some gravity: *“Do not be rash with your mouth, and let not your heart utter anything hastily before God”* (Ecclesiastes 5:2). The Preacher’s caution is not against prayer or trust in divine providence. It is against the presumption that enthusiasm equals confirmation. This is where the theological tension of the call becomes clearest to me. Samuel is operating from faith — or something he is calling faith. And I genuinely hope it is real. But faith in the New Testament sense is not the same as confidence in a promising outcome. The Greek word πίστις (*pistis*, PIS-tis) — faith, trust, conviction — is fundamentally relational and covenantal in its force. It describes the kind of trust that has an object: trust *in* the character and word of God, *in* the promises of Christ, *in* the witness of the Spirit. When faith is decoupled from that specific object and applied instead to a business arrangement or to one’s instinct about a set of associates, it begins to function differently — not as the substance of things hoped for, but as the emotional energy of things desired. I do not say this to condemn Samuel. I say it because I am aware of this temptation in myself as well, and because the distinction matters enormously for how the people of God navigate the marketplace. The mention of Brazil — and my instinctive response that I once lived with people from Brazil — is a small moment, but it is the kind of human connection that the Spirit often uses to soften a guard that needs to remain gentle rather than hard. Relational familiarity is not the same as relational knowledge. The men I used to room with were not these men. The connection is associative, not substantive. And yet it does something in the moment — it creates a warmth, a small bridge of shared humanity, that is real and good and should not be discarded. What I am learning is that I can honor that warmth without letting it do the work of discernment. Relationship and wisdom are not enemies, but neither is relationship a substitute for wisdom. Something else arrests me here, and it is the figure that gets dropped almost as an afterthought at the very end of the call: *“The opportunity we have — twenty-seven thousand in one day.”* I note the timing. It comes just as I am trying to close the conversation. It comes after the relationship has already been established, after the goodwill has already been built, after God’s name has already been invoked over the venture. That sequencing is worth observing — not because Samuel is necessarily calculating, but because this is a recognizable pattern. The Spirit is pressing me to notice that urgency introduced at the moment of departure is often designed, consciously or not, to bypass the slower, quieter process of discernment. The Proverbs speak directly to this: *“He who is hasty in his feet sins”* (Proverbs 19:2, emphasis on the latter clause). The man who rushes toward opportunity without waiting for understanding will miss the step he cannot afford to miss. I am also struck by my own consistent refrain throughout this call: *“We’ll see.”* I said it at least three times. In context, it functions as a verbal posture — a posture of genuine openness held together with genuine reserve. And I find that this posture has deep scriptural roots. The patriarch Jacob, wrestling through the night with the angel of God, did not release his grip until he received the blessing — but he also could not force the blessing before its time. The disciples on the road to Emmaus walked with a stranger for miles before their eyes were opened to recognize the risen Christ. The entire pattern of the Kingdom, from Genesis through Revelation, is one in which the faithful are called to *wait and see* — not passively, not fearfully, but with eyes open and feet still, trusting that what is real will be confirmed and what is false will be revealed. My *“we’ll see”* is, I think, a small echo of that larger posture. There is a word in the letter of James that I cannot shake loose from this reflection. James writes: *“If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask of God, who gives to all liberally and without reproach, and it will be given to him”* (James 1:5). The context of that promise is the testing of faith — the experience of trials that produce patience and completeness. James does not offer this promise in the context of smooth and exciting opportunities. He offers it in the context of pressure, uncertainty, and the need to discern what is genuinely from God and what is the noise of circumstance. I am aware, sitting here, that a consultation is not a commitment, and that wisdom begins not with a yes or a no but with the willingness to ask God what is actually present in a moment like this one. The figure of the newsletter industry — the high-payment risk environment, the ad spend, the business model — is not itself spiritually loaded. Commerce is not sin. The marketplace is not outside the reach of God’s governance. Throughout Scripture, men and women of faith operated in trade, agriculture, negotiation, and entrepreneurship without compromise. What the Scripture cares about is not whether the saints are in business but whether they are in it with integrity, with eyes clear, with hands clean, and with a heart that has genuinely sought the Lord’s counsel before moving. The Proverbs do not tell the merchant to leave the marketplace. They tell him to use honest scales. I find myself arriving, at the end of this reflection, at something simpler than I expected: I am grateful for the instinct that said *“we’ll see”* rather than *“yes”* — and I am aware that that instinct is not self-sufficiency. It is a mercy. The Scripture tells us that the steps of a good man are ordered by the Lord (Psalm 37:23), and I believe that includes the conversations that come at inconvenient times, mid-flight, between connections, with strangers on a phone introduced by a trusted friend. God is not absent from any of this. The question is not whether He is present, but whether I am awake enough to perceive what He is doing in it — and humble enough to wait for the answer before I move. ## Implications and Challenges The modern Western church has, in many streams, developed an enthusiastic theology of open doors — a reflexive tendency to interpret opportunity as divine affirmation. When something feels good, when the numbers are compelling, when a brother invokes God’s blessing over a proposal, the cultural pressure is to receive all of that as confirmation and move. This call gently but firmly challenges that reflex. Open doors require discernment, not just courage. The Spirit who opens doors also gives wisdom to test what is behind them. There is a particular challenge embedded in the mixing of spiritual language with commercial urgency. When the name of God and the figure of twenty-seven thousand dollars occupy the same breath, the disciple must be careful not to allow the spiritual framing to bypass the practical and prayerful scrutiny that any significant commitment deserves. God’s name does not automatically consecrate every venture it is associated with. Reverence for His name means we do not use it as rhetorical currency. The instinct toward relational trust — *“I trust these guys”* — is not wrong in itself. Trust is a gift and a virtue. But trust that outpaces knowledge is not faith; it is optimism. The saints are called to grow in their capacity to hold both warmth and wisdom simultaneously, refusing to let enthusiasm do the work that prayer, discernment, and time must do. The consultation agreed to in this call is wise precisely because it creates space for that slower process rather than collapsing it. There is also a word here for those who find themselves on Samuel’s side of conversations like this one — those who feel genuine excitement about a venture and want to bring others in. The invocation of divine purpose over a business opportunity is a serious act. It is not wrong to believe that God orders steps and opens doors. But the declaration that “God put these people in my path for a reason” carries the weight of prophetic speech, and prophetic speech carries accountability. The saints would be better served by inviting God’s discernment alongside their enthusiasm rather than presenting the invitation as already having been confirmed from above. Finally, there is grace in the small things. I notice that I stayed kind throughout this call — warm with Lucas, respectful toward Samuel, genuinely open without being swept. That is not a minor thing. The fruit of the Spirit does not disappear in business contexts. Sobriety and love are not opposites, and the saint who can hold both simultaneously — saying *“we’ll see”* with a smile and a genuine heart — is one who has been formed in something real. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity is not the ability to say yes boldly or no sharply. It is the capacity to remain genuinely open while keeping the slower, quieter rhythms of discernment intact — to let warmth and wisdom occupy the same posture, to honor the humanity of every person we encounter without surrendering the stewardship of our own calling and clarity to their enthusiasm. This call is a small training ground for exactly that kind of maturity. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Kingdom obedience in a conversation like this one looks like: honoring the commitment to show up for the consultation, praying genuinely between now and then, asking the Lord what is actually present in the opportunity, and remaining free to say yes or no when the time comes — without pressure, without fear, and without the need to perform either openness or caution for anyone else’s benefit. It means holding the name of God with enough reverence that we do not invoke it prematurely over outcomes we haven’t yet discerned. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture here is one of watchful patience. The prophet does not race toward the word before it comes. He waits in the place of prayer, keeping his senses submitted to the Spirit rather than to the energy of the room. In practical terms, this means that between this call and the consultation, there is a genuine invitation to seek the Lord — not for a dramatic vision, but for the quieter kind of confirmation that comes when the Spirit bears witness to the truth of a thing or gently presses a check into the heart of the discerning. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I thank You that You are present in every conversation — including the ones that happen mid-flight, between connections, with people I have just met. I thank You that the instinct to hold open a door without walking through it prematurely is itself a gift of Your grace, a small mercy from the Spirit who leads into all truth. I ask now, as Your servant and as one who has agreed to give counsel, that You would grant genuine wisdom for what lies ahead — not the wisdom of self-protection, not the wisdom of cynicism, but the wisdom that You give liberally, without reproach, to those who ask in faith. Lord, I hold this opportunity before You and ask You to reveal what is genuinely present in it. Where there is something You are building, let it be confirmed. Where there is something that does not bear the weight of Your purpose, let it be quietly set aside without harm to anyone. Let my words in the consultation that follows be seasoned with grace and rooted in truth, and let me honor every person in that room as a bearer of Your image, whatever the outcome. May Your name be kept holy in every marketplace conversation I enter, and may the fruit of this particular moment — whatever it is — serve Your Kingdom and not merely my comfort or gain. To You be all glory, all honor, and all wisdom, now and always.
