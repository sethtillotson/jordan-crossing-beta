# 07-22 Personal Meditation: Covenant Love, Medical Waiting, and Faithful Presence **Title:** 07-22 at 12:08 — Waiting in the Threshold: Love, Uncertainty, and the Steadiness of Presence **Recorded:** 2026-07-22 12:08:00 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Personal Meditation ## Opening Observation There are moments in marriage that do not announce themselves as sacred, yet the Spirit arrests me in the middle of them anyway. This brief, tender exchange between Gladys and me — barely a minute of morning words, a medical result still half-unread, a question hanging in the air — holds within it something I cannot simply let pass unremarked. The words are small; the weight underneath them is not. I am sitting with what it means to love someone who is standing at the edge of an answer they have not yet fully received. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ is not assembled only in sanctuaries. The covenant of marriage is itself a living theological theater, and the daily movements within it — the groggy morning check-in, the anxious reading of a medical portal, the simple “are you okay?” — are not beneath the notice of the Holy Spirit. What forms us as disciples is not only the mountaintop revelation or the formal prayer meeting; it is also the unguarded moment beside the one we have covenanted to love, when the ground feels slightly uncertain and neither of us has all the information yet. How we inhabit those threshold moments says everything about what we actually believe about God’s sovereignty, about the nearness of His care, and about the vocation of presence that covenant love demands. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind looks at this exchange and sees almost nothing worth examining. Two people talking briefly about a pending test result — one reading, one checking in, both saying their goodbyes with easy affection. The psychological lens might note mild anxiety on one side and considerate space-giving on the other. A counselor might commend the communication: Seth doesn’t press, Gladys doesn’t dismiss. Everything resolves into a manageable social moment — slightly tense, quietly navigated, quickly concluded. There is no dramatic breakdown, no crisis, and so the natural mind moves on, satisfied that nothing of consequence happened here. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind does not know how to read threshold moments. It can measure the words exchanged, but it cannot perceive the interior weight of “I don’t know” spoken twice by a woman waiting on medical news, nor can it comprehend what it costs a husband to hold space rather than fix, to say “I’ll let you read” rather than press for reassurance he wants as much as she does. The natural mind processes this as efficient conflict avoidance, not as the daily discipline of covenantal love — the kind Paul calls *agapē* precisely because it is not merely affectionate feeling but a chosen, sustained, sacrificial orientation toward another. What is actually happening in this moment is invisible to the reasoning of the flesh; it belongs to a different order of seeing entirely. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I am seeing, as I sit with this small recorded exchange, that the covenant of marriage was always meant to be one of God’s primary classrooms for the disciplines of waiting, of not-knowing, and of faithful presence. Gladys and I are standing — or more accurately, she is standing and I am approaching — at a threshold that has not yet resolved. There is a test result. There is a second part still pending. There is an “I don’t know” that is honest rather than evasive, and I notice how much the human heart wants to rush past that “I don’t know” into something certain. I wanted an answer too. The Spirit is pressing me here: what does it look like to love someone well precisely in the season before the answer comes? The Scriptures do not avoid this territory. The whole of the Psalmic tradition is populated with prayers offered in the middle of unresolved situations — laments that do not wrap neatly, cries that hang in the air awaiting the dawn of God’s reply. Psalm 130 opens in the depths, *mimma’amaqqim* — out of the deep places — and the entire psalm is a sustained posture of waiting upon the Lord before the resolution is visible. “My soul waits for the Lord more than those who watch for the morning” (Psalm 130:6, NKJV). The psalmist does not pretend certainty. He waits. And the waiting itself is presented as an act of trust, not a failure of faith. What arrests me in our exchange is the simplicity of the love being practiced. I did not fill the silence with false comfort. I did not say, “It’s going to be fine.” I asked if she was okay, I received an honest non-answer, and I chose to create space rather than fill it with my own anxiety dressed as encouragement. There is something profoundly biblical in that restraint. Ecclesiastes tells us there is “a time to keep silence, and a time to speak” (Ecclesiastes 3:7, NKJV), and the wisdom of the covenant is learning which time this is. Sometimes love sounds like “I’ll let you read.” Sometimes presence does not require words at all. And yet I am also confronted by the simplicity of what was said at the close: “Okay, love you, bye.” Four words of blessing and release. This is not a trivial thing. The Hebrew concept of *shalom* (shalom, shah-LOHM) — peace, wholeness, the completeness of covenant well-being — was not reserved for grand priestly pronouncements. It lived in the daily utterances of a covenant people toward one another. When I say “love you” to Gladys as she sits with uncertain medical news, I am not merely performing social habit. I am, whether I am conscious of it or not, pronouncing something over her — something that aligns with God’s own declaration over those He loves. I am a husband made in the image of One who does not withdraw His love when the news is still pending. The HPV test result — the waiting, the second part still outstanding — carries its own particular weight for a wife and mother. There is vulnerability in medical waiting that strips away pretense. The body becomes suddenly legible, suddenly mortal, suddenly asking questions that daily routine usually permits us to defer. I am thinking of how Paul writes to the Corinthians about the body as the temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:19, NKJV), and I feel the pastoral gravity of that truth when it touches the actual, physical body of someone I love. Her body is not merely her own concern or mine. It is the dwelling of the Spirit of God. And so the care we extend in these moments — even a simple “are you okay?” — participates in a larger reverence. I am also noticing how Gladys’s “I don’t know” is itself a form of honesty that the Spirit honors. We live in a culture, and sometimes a Christian subculture, that pressures people to perform certainty and positivity even when the internal reality is genuinely uncertain. But the biblical witness consistently honors the honest lament, the frank admission of not knowing, over the performance of a peace that has not yet arrived. Job’s friends failed him not because they came to sit with him but because they eventually could not tolerate his honest not-knowing. Gladys’s “I don’t know” is, in its own quiet way, a refusal to perform — and that kind of honesty is a form of integrity before God as much as before me. The short exchange ends with “bye bye bye” — and I find in those three words a kind of covenantal rhythm that is worth attending to. The repetition is warmth. It is the language of two people who have said this goodbye ten thousand times and will say it ten thousand more, God willing. There is a comfort in repetition that the world tends to pathologize as routine but that the Scriptures understand as covenant faithfulness. The daily return to one another — morning after morning, call after call — is itself an enacted parable of God’s own faithful return to His people. “His mercies are new every morning” (Lamentations 3:23, NKJV) is not merely a devotional sentiment; it is a declaration about the structure of covenant love. Every morning is a new bye-bye and a new returning. As I hold this in prayer, I am drawn to the Song of Solomon — not as allegory only but as genuine celebration of the embodied covenant relationship between a husband and wife. The Beloved and the Shulamite speak to one another in short, charged exchanges, filled with longing, checking in, reaching across distance. “I sought him, but I did not find him” (Song of Solomon 3:1, NKJV) describes a momentary absence that is both real and theologically resonant. The love that undergirds the seeking is what makes the absence briefly grievous and the return sweet. Our brief morning check-in lives in that same register — small in scale, but native to the same covenant territory. There is something I am finding particularly holy about the fact that Gladys was reading the results. Not waiting for me to find them for her. Not asking me to interpret them. Reading. There is agency and dignity in that. And my role in that moment was not to manage her process but to accompany it at the appropriate proximity. This is the art of marriage in its most grounded form — knowing when to draw near and when to hold the space that allows the other to be exactly where they are. I think of Boaz, who instructed his workers not to rebuke Ruth but to allow her to glean, and to drop handfuls from the harvest on purpose (Ruth 2:15–16, NKJV). His love operated at the right distance — close enough to protect, spacious enough to honor her agency. That is the image I want to carry into how I love Gladys through uncertain seasons. The pending nature of the second test result is not something I can resolve by will, prayer formula, or pastoral confidence. I can pray. I can trust. I can speak life over her body and our household. But I cannot — and should not try to — collapse the space of holy waiting by demanding a resolution before God provides it. Abraham waited. Hannah waited. Simeon waited. The whole of the covenantal narrative is threaded through with seasons of “not yet” that are not evidences of God’s absence but instruments of His formation. And if that is true in the grand arc of redemptive history, I must believe it is equally true in the smaller but no less sacred arc of a Tuesday morning in our home while a medical portal loads the second part of a test. What I carry away from this exchange, saturated now by Scripture and the Spirit’s pressing, is this: faithful love does not require complete information. It requires presence, attentiveness, the restraint to not fill every silence with noise, the courage to say “I don’t know” when that is the honest answer, and the daily returning that covenant makes possible. ἀγάπη (agapē, ah-GAH-pay) — covenantal love — is not proved in the dramatic moments alone. It is proved and refined and deepened in exactly these threshold-morning exchanges, where two people choose one another again before the day has even declared itself fully. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation presses on me — and perhaps on those who read it — is the deep cultural pressure to perform wellness and certainty even in the presence of the people who most deserve our honesty. Gladys said “I don’t know” twice, and I received it. But how often do we — inside Christian marriages and communities — train one another, implicitly and explicitly, to always have an encouraging word ready, always land on the positive note, always close the loop into confident faith? There is a kind of faith-language that becomes a subtle demand for the other person to stop being uncertain before they are ready. The Word does not ask that of us. It asks for truth in the inner parts (Psalm 51:6, NKJV). Let us recover space for honest not-knowing within our closest covenants. A second challenge is the tendency to under-theologize the ordinary. I could have listened to this recording and thought, *nothing happened here — just morning small talk.* But the Spirit does not categorize our lives that way. Every covenant exchange is potentially pregnant with the character of God being formed in us or resisted. The question “are you okay?” asked by a husband to his wife reading uncertain medical results is a moment of pastoral ministry. Let the Church recognize that the most important ministry many of us will ever exercise happens inside our own homes, in the unglamorous daily returns. Third: the body matters. The pending HPV result is not a spiritually neutral detail to be politely passed over. The body of a covenant wife is under the canopy of covenant prayer and care. I am challenged to pray specifically and scripturally for Gladys’s physical body — to bring her before the Lord not only in the grand intercessory moments but in the daily acknowledgment that her health, her wholeness, her *shalom* (shah-LOHM) is a matter of Kingdom concern. Fourth: the discipline of appropriate proximity — knowing when to draw near and when to hold space — is a spiritual skill that must be developed, not assumed. I got it reasonably right in this exchange, but I notice it required restraint of my own desire for reassurance. Love that creates space for the other often costs the self something. That cost is worth naming and honoring rather than treating as automatic. Fifth, and finally: covenant faithfulness is built on repetition more than on singular heroic moments. “Bye bye bye” is a liturgy. Daily check-ins, daily I-love-yous, daily returning — these are the stones by which the altar of a marriage is built. Let us not despise the small stones because they are not dramatic. They are the substance of what endures. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This meditation calls me to receive ordinary threshold moments as genuine formation ground. The Spirit is not only present in the clearly supernatural. He is present in the uncertain waiting, in the honest “I don’t know,” in the restraint of not filling silence with false comfort. Growing up into Christ means developing the spiritual attentiveness to recognize what is actually happening in our quietest moments — and to respond with the character of the One whose love does not require the outcome to be certain before it is given. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Practically, this calls me to pray specifically over Gladys’s health — to stand in covenant intercession for her body as the temple of the Holy Spirit. It calls me to continue creating space for her honest processing rather than rushing past uncertainty. And it calls me to receive “I don’t know” from the people I love as a gift of trust, not a problem to be solved. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture here is one of patient, watchful accompaniment. The prophets of old were not only those who declared great words; they were also those who sat with God’s people in the space between the promise and its fulfillment. I am learning to inhabit that space with Gladys — not passive, not anxious, but watchfully present, praying, trusting, and ready to receive the next word when it comes. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You from within the covenant You authored and ordained — as a husband, as a servant, as one who stands between the heavens and the earth in the name of Jesus Christ, Your Son, who came in the flesh and was raised in glory. I bring Gladys before You in this moment of waiting — the second test still outstanding, the full answer not yet in hand — and I declare over her body what You have already declared: she is Yours, she is loved, she is held in the hands of the One who knit her together in her mother’s womb and has not ceased to watch over her since. I thank You that You are not distant in the threshold moments. I thank You that ἀγάπη (ah-GAH-pay) is Your nature before it is our instruction. I ask for her *shalom* (shah-LOHM) — not the surface quiet of everything being fine, but the deep covenantal wholeness that flows from being known by You. Let her body be well. Let her heart be at rest. Let this household be a place where honest waiting is never met with shame and where Your faithfulness is as familiar as morning. I agree with Your will, Lord — whatever it brings — and I trust that You are good in it. To the One who sits enthroned above every medical report, every uncertain result, every unresolved morning: be glorified.
