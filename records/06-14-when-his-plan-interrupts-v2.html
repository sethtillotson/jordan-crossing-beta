# 06-14 Conversation Reflection: Surrender, Covenant, and Unexpected Pregnancy Announcement **Title:** 06-14 at 11:18 — When His Plan Interrupts Yours: A Meditation on Surrender, Covenant, and the Life Being Knit Together **Recorded:** 2026-06-14 11:18:16 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Conversation Reflection ## Opening Observation Something holy landed in the middle of the ordinary — between the cats and the chicken and the phone scrolling and the tired bones — and I am only now beginning to understand the weight of what was captured here. Gladys and I were not sitting in a prayer room when God answered years of longing; we were in our apartment, surrounded by our cats, talking about moringa trees and hospital bills and a subscription she accidentally purchased for a period tracker she no longer needs. And somehow, in all of that unscripted mess, the Lord made His intentions known. What arrests me most is not merely the news of the pregnancy itself, but the moment within the conversation where I heard myself say what I have said to disciples many times — and realized I was saying it to my own wife, and to myself. **Why This Matters:** The condition of the modern believer is one of persistent, well-intentioned self-direction dressed in the language of faith. We bring our plans to the altar, sincerely, and we ask God to bless them — and we genuinely believe that is prayer. We construct the architecture of our futures, we build the business model, we map the timeline, we visualize the outcome, and then we lay it before the Lord and say, *consecrate this.* What this conversation lays bare — tenderly, humorously, with cats wandering through the frame — is the difference between that transactional posture and the terrifying, glorious reality of a God who does not co-sign our plans but who authors His own. This matters for every disciple who has ever prayed earnestly for one thing and received another. It matters because the gap between what we asked for and what He gave is often not a failure of prayer — it is the very answer. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the standpoint of natural human reasoning, what happened here is simply an unexpected pregnancy during a season of financial uncertainty, modest income, incomplete insurance coverage, and personal unreadiness — a circumstance that would reasonably call for practical contingency planning, perhaps some anxiety about timing, and a reassessment of personal goals. The mind that operates within visible cause-and-effect frameworks would hear Gladys say, *I specifically asked for my business, for my plans, my business, my whatever* — and nod sympathetically. Of course you planned. Of course you set goals. Of course you expected the blessing to look like the blueprint you drew. That is simply what intelligent, motivated people do: they set trajectories and seek support for those trajectories. From this vantage point, the pregnancy is a disruption — welcome or unwelcome depending on one’s emotional reserves at the moment — to an otherwise organized forward motion. The natural mind is not cruel in this reading; it is simply insufficient. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind, however compassionate its framing, cannot perceive what is moving beneath the surface of this moment, because it has no category for a God who answers lament with life, who turns the ordinary act of breathing and sleeping and misreading body signals into a moment of covenant announcement. The mind that reasons only from the visible cannot distinguish between a plan that failed and a plan that was never God’s to begin with. It cannot hear the difference between a blessing denied and a blessing redirected. And it most certainly cannot recognize that the tiredness Gladys felt, the phone she could not put down, the spotting that frightened her, the test she took at night when she should have waited until morning — all of it was already inside the sovereign knowledge of a Father who had been knitting something together in the darkness long before either of us had adjusted our expectations to include it. The flesh sees interruption; the Spirit sees appointment. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with what was captured in that conversation, I keep returning to the moment Gladys said something that struck me as almost a confession of the deepest spiritual habit — not just hers, but mine, and if I am honest, the habit of nearly every disciple I have ever walked with. She said, *I specifically asked for my business, for my plans, my business, my whatever.* And I heard in that sentence the entire anatomy of transactional religion: the sincere heart, the genuine prayer, the real faith — all of it pointed at a destination the Lord had not authored. I have taught this principle many times. I have said it to Samuel. I have pressed it into the conversations of brothers who came to me with their great ideas wrapped in the language of prayer. And here I was, in my own living room, watching the Lord demonstrate it in my own household. What I said to Gladys in that moment was not a rebuke — it could not be, because I was not standing outside the same lesson. I said to her: *What you’re doing is creating a plan in your will, and then going to the altar and asking the Lord to consecrate your plan.* And what He did instead was tell us His plan. That is not a theological abstraction. That is a living word that came through Isaiah the prophet and has never ceased being the posture of heaven toward the ambitions of the earth: *“For My thoughts are not your thoughts, nor are your ways My ways,” says the LORD. “For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are My ways higher than your ways, and My thoughts than your thoughts”* (Isaiah 55:8–9, NKJV). What I could not have anticipated is that the verse would stop being a text I meditated on and become a fact I was living inside of. There is a Greek word that helps me see this more clearly. The New Testament uses the word βούλημα (boulēma, boo-LAY-mah) to describe the deliberate, settled purpose or will of God — the kind of will that is not up for negotiation and does not require our endorsement. Peter uses a related form in 2 Peter 3:9 when he says the Lord is *“not willing that any should perish.”* That settled, sovereign intention is what was operating behind every detail of this season — the moringa tree that kept growing, the subscription Gladys accidentally bought for a period tracker, the test she almost did not take, the body that began announcing what the Lord had already decided. The βούλημα of God does not wait for our readiness. It proceeds according to His own counsel. And yet — and this is where I find the tenderness that keeps me from reducing this to cold theological mechanics — He did not override our humanity. He did not bypass our jokes about naming the baby Jasmine Rice, or our wonder at Sassy the cat being our *practice*, or Gladys worrying about her nipples and her insurance and her HCG levels. He entered all of it. He was present in the unguarded apartment conversation, in the sleepy Sunday afternoon, in the Elijah moment I referenced almost accidentally when I told Gladys not to feel guilty for resting. *“Arise and eat”* — the angel said it twice to Elijah under the juniper tree (1 Kings 19:5–7, NKJV), and what strikes me now is that the divine word came not in the throne room but in the wilderness, while the prophet was exhausted and afraid and done. God’s announcements often arrive in the juniper-tree moments, not the mountaintop ones. I am seeing something in the threading of this conversation that I did not plan to find. In the middle of talking about Samuel’s divorce situation — where I counseled him simply to wait, to not make it about winning, to let the ball stay in the court where God had already placed it — I was unknowingly rehearsing the same word for myself. *Wait on the LORD, and He will bring you your salvation* (Psalm 27:14, NKJV). Samuel was learning to wait on the Lord in his legal situation. Gladys and I were being pressed into the same wait in a different arena — not waiting for a divorce to finalize but waiting for the plans of God to arrive on their own schedule, regardless of our insurance status, our EIN filings, our business ideas, or our readiness to parent. The counsel I gave to my covenant brother was the counsel the Spirit was already positioning for my own household. The Hebrew word קָוָה (qāvāh, kah-VAH) — translated *wait* in many psalms — carries within it the image of twisting strands together, of being drawn taut toward something. It is not passive waiting; it is directional, expectant, tethered waiting. It is the tension of a cord that has not yet reached its anchor. When I told Samuel, *just wait, just sit on it, just pray and just be patient* — I was describing qāvāh without using the word. And now I see that what is happening in my own home is exactly that: a cord being drawn taut. Something is being knit. In the womb, yes — literally — but also in the fabric of this household. A covenant is being tightened. The Spirit is pressing me to notice something about the specific details Gladys named when she described what she had prayed for. She did not pray for a child. She prayed for her business, for her plans. And God gave her a child. This is not God being cruel or dismissive; this is God being God in the most thoroughgoing sense — the One who *“has done great things”* (Joel 2:21, NKJV), who *“gives life to the dead and calls those things which do not exist as though they did”* (Romans 4:17, NKJV). He is not required to bless the architecture we build in our will. He is free — gloriously, terrifyingly free — to hand us something we did not request and that turns out to be more than we would have chosen for ourselves if left to our own wisdom. What strikes me is that Gladys herself began to arrive at this understanding inside the conversation. She said, almost under her breath: *Why am I asking for a blessing? What if this is the blessing?* That is a prophetic moment hiding inside domestic dialogue. I do not want to over-spiritualize what was an honest, slightly bewildered, very human question. But there is something in the willingness to let the unexpected thing become the asked-for thing — to let God redefine the blessing in real time — that is very close to the posture of Mary when Gabriel appeared: *“Let it be to me according to your word”* (Luke 1:38, NKJV). Not a performance of surrender, but a real recalibration of expectation in the presence of an announcement one did not see coming. I also cannot move past the detail about Samuel — about how a year ago I could not trust him, and now he is a covenant brother who is true to his word, who sends money when he says he will, who has walked through real fire and remained. I find this to be one of the quiet testimonies embedded in this conversation: that restoration is real. That a man who had stuff from his childhood, who went through betrayal and divorce proceedings and a season of spiritual inconsistency, can — through the sustained patience of covenant friendship and the grace of God — become someone dependable. The Greek word for this kind of tested, proven faithfulness is δόκιμος (dokimos, DOH-kee-mos) — the word used in James 1:12 for the one who endures testing and is found approved. Samuel is becoming δόκιμος (DOH-kee-mos). And I was given the privilege of witnessing it, and of being one of the voices that kept calling him forward. There is a thread connecting all of this that I want to name carefully: the theme of *things being knit together in the dark.* The baby being knit in the womb — which I said aloud in the conversation, referencing what I knew about early fetal development. But also: a covenant friendship being knit back together over a year of tested ground. A marriage being knit more deeply by news that arrived before either person felt ready. An understanding of God’s sovereignty being knit into the daily fabric of a couple who talks about cats and moringa trees and hospital billing and still somehow arrives at *“who knows? Maybe this is the next billionaire of the ground.”* The Spirit knits. The human eye sees scattered threads; the Father sees the completed garment. I want to be careful not to overstate what I know. I do not know the outcome of this pregnancy. I do not know the financial path forward. I do not know whether Gladys will deliver at Essentia or elsewhere, whether the sliding scale will cover what we need, whether my EIN filings will lead to the business provision we need before the baby arrives. I know none of that. What I do know — what this conversation has pressed into me with a weight I was not entirely expecting — is that the God who authored the pregnancy without our consultation is also the God who has already authored provision. He does not create appointments without creating supply. The One who said *“Before I formed you in the womb I knew you”* (Jeremiah 1:5, NKJV) to Jeremiah was not speaking only to a prophet; He was speaking to a principle: His foreknowledge precedes our formation, and His provision precedes our awareness. ## Implications and Challenges The most direct challenge this conversation raises — one I want to name with grace because I am not exempt from it — is the habit of consecrating self-generated plans rather than receiving divinely originated ones. This is not a fringe problem; it is deeply woven into the motivational culture of modern Western Christianity, where vision boards and five-year plans and prophetic declarations of personal goals are regularly offered up to God as though He exists to fund the infrastructure of our ambitions. The correction is not that planning is sinful. The correction is that the posture must be reversed: *“Commit your way to the LORD, trust also in Him, and He shall bring it to pass”* (Psalm 37:5, NKJV) — meaning, the commitment precedes the plan, and the plan emerges from the listening rather than the listening being appended to the plan. There is also a real challenge embedded in what I said about Samuel’s divorce situation that extends beyond that circumstance: the challenge of not making spiritual situations about winning. The moment any of us picks up a lawyer — metaphorically or literally — to enforce our rights in a situation where God has not yet spoken His final word, we tend to shift from participants in His story to managers of our own outcomes. The wisdom I gave to Samuel — *as soon as you make this about winning, you’ve already lost* — is the wisdom of Proverbs 20:22 (NKJV): *“Do not say, ‘I will recompense evil’; wait for the LORD, and He will save you.”* Waiting is not weakness. It is the refusal to preempt the Lord. A third challenge, one that emerged quietly in the conversation’s texture, is the modern believer’s complicated relationship with rest. Gladys felt guilty for being tired. I felt the need to explain why we were being lazy on a Sunday. And I found myself citing Elijah under the juniper tree — not as a clever proof text but because it genuinely applied. The Western Christian ethos tends to treat rest as spiritual failure, as productivity withheld, as trust not yet muscular enough to overcome tiredness. But the angel did not rebuke Elijah for sleeping. He said, *“Arise and eat, because the journey is too great for you”* (1 Kings 19:7, NKJV). God’s response to exhaustion is not condemnation — it is food, twice, and permission to sleep again before the next leg begins. There is also a challenge to the way we receive unexpected covenant gifts. The modern instinct, when something arrives before we feel ready, is to immediately calculate cost: the insurance cost, the birth cost, the childcare cost, the lifestyle adjustment cost. And I want to be clear — those calculations are necessary and responsible, and I am not suggesting we ignore them. But the Spirit presses me to hold those calculations inside a larger framework: the God who assigned the pregnancy has already seen the invoices. The practical details belong in the hands of a Father who feeds sparrows and clothes lilies (Matthew 6:26–28, NKJV). The math does not have to terrify us. It has to be submitted. Finally, there is a gentle but real challenge in what this conversation models about covenant marriage. Gladys and I did not have a composed, liturgical response to learning we were pregnant. We talked about cats. We argued playfully about baby names. She said she was tired and did not want to do household chores. I referenced Elijah because our cat wandered into my line of sight. This is covenant marriage in its actual texture — not the Instagram version, not the conference platform version, but two people in an apartment on a Sunday, sitting inside news that is bigger than both of them, and finding that the Lord is somehow present in the ordinary weight of it. That is worth naming, because many couples believe spiritual life and domestic life are parallel tracks. They are not. They are one road. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This calls us to examine every prayer we bring to the altar and ask honestly: *Is this the Lord’s plan I am asking Him to fulfill, or is this my plan I am asking Him to validate?* The difference between those two postures is not always obvious from the inside. It requires stillness, it requires the willingness to hear *no*, and it requires what David described in Psalm 131:2 (NKJV) — a soul quieted and calmed, like a weaned child with its mother. Spiritual maturity does not mean we stop having plans. It means we hold them loosely enough that when the Lord sets them aside and hands us something else, we are not shattered — we are simply recalibrated. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this conversation calls for is the obedience of waiting in the right direction — qāvāh (kah-VAH), tethered expectation — rather than forcing outcomes. For Samuel, that means not weaponizing the legal process. For Gladys and me, that means trusting provision ahead of the visible evidence of it, and walking toward the clinics and the insurance processes and the HCG monitoring without letting the cost conversation become louder than the covenant reality. Kingdom obedience in a season of unexpected appointment often looks less like a dramatic act and more like a series of small, steady, surrendered steps taken in the direction the Lord indicated. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this season demands is one of consecrated receptivity: the willingness to let God’s announcement rewrite the prayer request. Mary did not argue with Gabriel. She asked a clarifying question, received the answer, and then said *yes*. That is the posture — not passive, not naive, but genuinely open to a future that looks different from the one we designed. And for those who walk alongside us as covenant community, the prophetic call is to help one another distinguish between *my idea that I am asking God to bless* and *what I actually heard from God* — the exact question I have pressed into Samuel, and the exact question the Lord pressed back into me through this very conversation. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who has sat inside this moment and recognized Your fingerprints all over what looked, on the surface, like an ordinary Sunday afternoon. You are the Author of the life being knit together in Gladys right now, and You are the Author of the word that fell out of my own mouth before I fully understood that I was preaching it to myself. I ask You to do in us what You have been doing without our cooperation: continue the knitting. Knit the faith where fear has been hiding. Knit the provision into the gaps we can see and the ones we cannot. Knit the marriage more tightly through this season of shared weight and shared wonder. Knit Samuel into the fullness of what You are making him, and let our covenant friendship be a long testimony to what restoration looks like when two people refuse to quit on each other. As a servant of Yours, one who has been given to watch and speak and agree with what You are doing between heaven and earth, I set my agreement here: Your plan is better than ours. Your timing is not late. Your βούλημα (boo-LAY-mah) stands over every invoice, every clinic visit, every frightened night, every stubborn business idea, every moringa tree, every unread message from a father whose phone is off-grid. You are God over all of it, and we are not afraid — or we are choosing faith over fear, which is the closest thing to courage we have. Be glorified in the small apartment, in the tired Mama, in the cats who will become reluctant big sisters, and in the child You have already named. We receive it. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |--------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Isaiah 55:8–9 | הִגְבִּיהוּ (hig-BEE-hoo) — the verb of elevation used for God’s ways being “higher” | The gap between divine and human thought is not a gap of degree but of kind;
