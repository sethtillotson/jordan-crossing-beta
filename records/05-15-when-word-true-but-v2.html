# 05-15 Theological Reflection: Teaching Posture, Spiritual Authority, and the Danger of True Doctrine Delivered Wrongly **Title:** 05-15 at 09:33 — When the Word Is True but the Way Is Wrong **Recorded:** 2026-05-15 09:33:27 **Source Discernment:** A — Personal Spoken-Word Revelation **Classification:** Theological Reflection ## Opening Observation Something has been pressing on me about the way we handle the Word of God in public teaching — not whether the content is accurate, but whether the posture behind it is faithful. James issued a sobering warning that I cannot shake: *“Let not many of you become teachers, knowing that we shall receive a stricter judgment”* (James 3:1, NKJV). The burden I am carrying is not about false doctrine in the classic sense, but about something subtler and perhaps more dangerous — true doctrine delivered in a spirit that wounds the very people it was meant to heal. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Body of Christ is not merely a classroom, and those who stand before the brethren are not merely lecturers dispensing accurate information. The manner in which truth is communicated carries its own spiritual weight. When teaching is wrapped in urgency language, fear-inducing frameworks, or an authoritative tone that implicitly elevates the teacher above the student, the very truth being proclaimed can become a stumbling block. The Lord Jesus warned with the gravest solemnity that causing one of His little ones to stumble would be better remedied by a millstone around the neck than by continued life on earth (Matthew 18:6). If that is the standard of accountability He set, then the *how* of teaching is not a secondary concern — it is a Kingdom matter of the first order. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural standpoint, the concern I am raising would be dismissed as stylistic preference or pastoral oversensitivity. The world’s framework for evaluating communication is largely pragmatic: if the information is correct and the outcome is measurable, the method is justified. A teacher who says *“seven signs you must enter an immediate emergency fast”* would be evaluated by whether people fasted, whether results followed, and whether the teacher’s credibility was enhanced. The natural mind does not perceive the invisible architecture of fear, hierarchy, or conditioned expectation that such language builds in the hearts of listeners. It measures success by response rate, not by the spiritual condition of those who responded — or those who tried and failed to produce the expected outcome, and were quietly broken by that failure. **The Limitation:** What the natural mind cannot see is that truth, when clothed in the wrong spirit, does not arrive as truth. The vessel carrying the Word shapes the atmosphere in which the Word lands. A heart already burdened with shame or striving receives *“you must enter an immediate emergency fast”* not as an invitation into intimacy with God, but as one more impossible demand from a God they fear cannot be pleased. The natural mind evaluates content; the Spirit-formed mind evaluates *formation* — what is actually being built in the inner person of the listener. Only the Spirit can discern whether a teaching, however factually correct, is building faith or manufacturing performance, cultivating sons or producing servants afraid of the master’s rod. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I am sitting with James 3:1 again, and I notice that James does not say *“let not many of you become false teachers.”* He says *“let not many of you become teachers”* — full stop — *“knowing that we shall receive a stricter judgment.”* The gravity of that word *stricter* in the Greek, μεῖζον (meizon, MY-zon) — greater, heavier, more weighty — tells me that the standard applied to those who teach is not the same standard applied to those who learn. Teachers are measured not only by whether their doctrine is sound, but by the whole of their influence on the flock. This is not an invitation to silence the gifted; it is an invitation to sober self-examination for any person who presumes to stand before God’s people and say, *“Thus saith the Lord.”* The thing I keep returning to is this: the facts can be correct and the way can still be wrong. This is not a comfortable tension to sit with, but it is a biblical one. The Pharisees had Scripture memorized; they knew the law with precision. Yet Jesus said their teaching placed on men’s shoulders burdens *“heavy and hard to bear”* while they themselves would not move them with one of their fingers (Matthew 23:4). The problem was not the law — the law was holy, just, and good (Romans 7:12). The problem was the *spirit* in which the law was administered, the hierarchical distance it created between the custodians of truth and the struggling people beneath them. I am seeing that same pattern at work today. When a teacher employs language like *“you must enter an immediate emergency fast”* — and I want to be careful here, because fasting is real, fasting is scriptural, and the call to fast may sometimes be genuinely urgent — the weight of that language does something to the listener before the listener ever opens their Bible. The word *immediate* forecloses discernment. The word *emergency* invokes panic. The word *must* bypasses the student’s direct relationship with the Spirit of God and substitutes the teacher’s authority for the Spirit’s leading. What was meant, perhaps, as an earnest call to spiritual discipline arrives as a summons issued by someone who has positioned themselves above the congregation — and the congregation, already hungry for direction, receives it as law. There is something the Spirit is pressing on me here about what happens *after* — after the listener obeys the teaching, after they fast the prescribed fast or follow the prescribed method, and the expected outcome does not materialize. The teacher spoke of expected outcomes. Those words matter. When a teacher’s language induces expectation — *if you do this, this will happen* — and that expectation is not met, the listener does not conclude that the teacher was mistaken. In their spiritual formation, shaped by a culture that defers to the teacher-as-authority, they conclude that *they* failed. That *they* did something wrong. That God did not show up *for them* specifically. The stumbling is not dramatic; it is quiet, interior, and devastating. A stone of offense was laid not by heresy, but by an unrealized promise attached to a technique. The Greek word for stumbling block is σκάνδαλον (skandalon, SKAN-dah-lon) — it refers to the trigger of a trap, the mechanism that causes a snare to spring shut. Jesus used this word when He spoke of the millstone. It is the same word used in Romans 14 when Paul addresses the stronger brother whose liberty causes the weaker to fall. What strikes me is that a σκάνδαλον (SKAN-dah-lon) does not have to be placed intentionally to do its damage. A teacher may place a trigger in the path of a student with the best of intentions — and it will still spring shut. The damage is real whether the intent was harmful or not. This is precisely why the stricter judgment James speaks of is so weighty. I am also pressing into what it means that we are *all* students of the one Teacher. Jesus Himself said in Matthew 23:8, *“Do not be called ‘Rabbi’; for One is your Teacher, the Christ, and you are all brethren.”* This is not an abolition of the teaching gift — Paul lists teachers plainly in Ephesians 4:11 as gifts given by the ascended Christ to the Body. But it is a radical leveling of the relational ground on which teaching must take place. Every person who teaches in the Body of Christ is simultaneously a student. The moment a teacher forgets that they, too, sit at the feet of the one Teacher, their instruction begins to take on the character of authority it was never meant to carry. The hierarchical boundary between teacher and student that I have been observing is not merely a stylistic problem — it is a theological corruption of the nature of the Body. There is a Hebrew concept that illumines this for me: the role of the rabbi in ancient Jewish tradition was not to produce followers of the rabbi, but to produce students of Torah who would surpass the rabbi in their love and knowledge of God. The posture was inherently self-emptying. What I observe in much modern Christian teaching is the inverse — the teacher’s personal experience, personal method, and personal vocabulary become the framework through which the student is expected to encounter God. *“This is what worked for me”* becomes *“this is what will work for you.”* But the Spirit of God, רוּחַ (ruach, ROO-akh) — breath, wind, Spirit — blows where He wills (John 3:8). He is not domesticated by methodology, and He does not owe the student the experience the teacher promised. This leads me to what I believe is the deeper wound: when teachers universalize their particular testimony. There is something beautiful and legitimate about saying, *“God met me when I fasted — let me tell you what I found.”* That is testimony. That is the language of Revelation 12:11, the word of one’s testimony, the overcomer’s witness. But testimony becomes tyranny when it crosses the line into prescription — when *“God met me here”* becomes *“God will meet you here, and if He doesn’t, something is wrong with you.”* The teacher’s particular καιρός (kairos, KY-ros) — appointed time, divine moment — cannot be legislated into the student’s journey. God is sovereign over the timing and the means of encounter. I am also sitting with Paul’s words in 1 Corinthians 3:6-7: *“I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the increase. So then neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase.”* The teacher’s role is to plant and water. The teacher’s role is never to guarantee the harvest. When teaching language implies that following the prescribed method will produce the promised outcome, it has subtly relocated God from the role of sovereign giver to the role of vending machine — insert the right spiritual currency, receive the predicted result. This is not faith; it is a form of spiritual mechanics that may wear the language of faith but bypasses the living God entirely. And yet — and this is where I want to land with both honesty and grace — I do not believe most of the teachers I am reflecting on intend this. The urgency in their voices often comes from genuine love. The authority in their language often comes from genuine conviction. They have seen something work; they want to share it with passion. The sin, if sin is even the right word in every case, is often a failure of *epistemic humility* — a failure to remember that their portion of the light is not the whole of the light, and that the Spirit of God may lead another believer by an entirely different path to an entirely different encounter with the same God. Humility in teaching is not a softening of conviction; it is a precision of scope. It says, *“Here is what the Lord has shown me — and He may show you differently, but He is faithful, and He will show you.”* What has been 250 years of American Christianity, in many ways, but the progressive institutionalization of personal encounter into transferable technique? The revivalists packaged the altar call. The Pentecostals packaged the tarrying room. The charismatics packaged the prayer method. Each packaging began with a genuine move of the Spirit, and each became, over generations, a mechanism that people could step into hoping to produce the same result — often without the Spirit who first produced it. I am not condemning the tradition; I am mourning the drift. What was once a testimony has become a template, and the template now mediates between the student and the living God rather than introducing the student to Him. ## Implications and Challenges **First, the culture of Christian celebrity teaching must be examined with sober eyes.** When a teacher’s platform grows large enough, the implicit authority of their voice can override the discernment of individual believers in the congregation. The listener, rather than testing every word against Scripture and bringing it to the Spirit in prayer, simply complies — because surely the teacher with the large following, the produced content, and the confident delivery must know. This is not the Berean posture (Acts 17:11). The size of a platform was never listed among the gifts of the Spirit. **Second, we must recover the language of invitation over the language of imperative in teaching.** There is a meaningful difference between *“I want to invite you to seek the Lord in fasting”* and *“you must enter an immediate emergency fast.”* Both can be honest expressions of a genuine burden. Only one honors the listener’s direct relationship with the Spirit. This is not a call to weak or indecisive communication — it is a call to language that leads people *to* God rather than to the teacher’s method. **Third, we must take seriously the pastoral responsibility for what happens when the expected outcome does not arrive.** A teacher who induces expectation and then disappears into the next video, the next conference, the next series — leaving no pastoral infrastructure to receive those who tried and failed — has sown without any intention of reaping. The broken, quietly stumbling believer who followed the seven steps and found nothing is a real person, and they deserve shepherding, not just content. **Fourth, humility in teaching is not optional — it is structural.** The teacher who does not regularly, publicly, and genuinely position themselves as a fellow student of the one Teacher will, over time, produce a congregation that relates to them rather than to Christ. The teacher’s personality, vocabulary, and experiential framework will become the lens through which the congregation interprets Scripture. This is a form of spiritual dependency that does violence to the priesthood of all believers (1 Peter 2:9) and to the direct access every saint has to the Teacher through the indwelling Spirit. **Fifth, the grace note in all of this is that the gift of teaching is not being condemned — it is being called higher.** The teacher who fasts in secret, who sits at the feet of Christ before they stand before people, who speaks from encounter rather than from method, who says *“this is what the Lord showed me, and I trust He will show you”* — that teacher is a gift to the Body that multiplies rather than diminishes faith. The call is not to silence the teaching gift. The call is to crucify the flesh that surrounds it. **Sixth, the American church in particular must reckon with how deeply pragmatism has shaped our teaching culture.** If it produces measurable results, we assume it is from God. If it fills seats or generates responses, we double down. But the Spirit of God is not accountable to our metrics, and the Kingdom of God does not run on our timelines. We would do well to recover the ancient wisdom: *“Let not many of you become teachers.”* ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This reflection calls me — and every person who has ever taught, mentored, or discipled another believer — to a posture of consistent, deliberate humility. Maturity in the teaching gift looks like increasing awareness of the gap between *my encounter* and *God’s sovereignty*, and an increasing willingness to hold that gap openly before those I teach. It means repenting of the subtle pride of universalized testimony and returning always to the posture of a fellow student, still learning, still growing, still surprised by the Spirit. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedient response to this word is to examine our own teaching language — whether in formal ministry, in friendship discipleship, in small group leadership, or in the content we share online. Wherever we have spoken in ways that induced expected outcomes rather than directed people to the living God, we can return and reframe. Wherever we have positioned ourselves above rather than alongside those we teach, we can humble ourselves. The Kingdom of God is structured around a single Teacher, and those He gifts as teachers serve the community best when they remain most transparently under His instruction themselves. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic community especially must heed this word. Those who move in the gifts of the Spirit, who speak words of knowledge, who call the Body to fasting and prayer and urgent spiritual engagement — they carry a particular responsibility to keep their language clean of manufactured urgency and empty of promised outcomes that only God can guarantee. The prophetic voice is not an authoritative override of the believer’s personal walk with the Spirit; it is a confirmation, an invitation, a witness alongside the Spirit’s work already in motion. The watching posture — the posture of faithful endurance, of abiding rather than performing — is the posture that keeps the prophetic voice clean and the Body healthy. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who is also under the weight of James 3:1 — as one who speaks, who teaches, who reflects publicly on Your Word — and I ask first for the grace of genuine humility, the kind that is not performed but that comes from truly resting in my place as a student before You, the one Teacher. I thank You that Your Word is not returned void, that even where it has been packaged in human urgency and imperfect methods, You have remained sovereign over every encounter, every καιρός (KY-ros), every moment of genuine meeting between Your Spirit and a hungry heart. I bring before You, Lord, those who have been quietly stumbled — who fasted and found silence, who followed the method and found no promised result, who carried the shame of believing themselves to have failed when in fact no teacher could guarantee what only You can give. Restore them, Father. Let them know that You were never absent, that the silence was not rejection, and that You are still the God who meets His people in the wilderness as much as on the mountaintop. And for those of us who teach, who speak, who share — burn away every trace of hierarchical pride, every universalized testimony that has crowded out Your sovereignty, and grant us the courage to speak as fellow students: awed, accountable, and always returning to the feet of Your Son, in whose name alone we dare to open our mouths. May Your rûaḥ — רוּחַ (ROO-akh) — blow through the teaching rooms of this generation and replace every manufactured atmosphere with the holy weight of Your actual presence. To the one Teacher, Jesus Christ, be all glory, all honor, and all authority — now and unto the ages.
