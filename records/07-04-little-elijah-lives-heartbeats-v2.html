# 07-04 Call Reflection: Little Elijah’s Heartbeat, Covenant Theology, and the Interior Exodus **Title:** 07-04 at 04:30 — Little Elijah Lives: On Heartbeats, Holy Framing, and the Interior Exodus **Recorded:** 2026-07-04 04:30 (approximate, based on Seth’s stated time during the call) **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture / Phone or Video Call) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to this early-morning call with my brother Samuel — at four-thirty in the morning on the Fourth of July, while my wife rested on the couch under the AC and the whole house was quiet — I found myself sitting inside one of those rare moments where the sacred and the ordinary are completely indistinguishable from each other. We had just come from the ER. Medicine had no explanation for the bleeding. And yet there was a heartbeat — flashing like light on the ultrasound monitor — and a child the size of a peanut, clinging to life at seven weeks and five days. What I am seeing, even now as I sit with this memory, is that God was already speaking before I had language to receive it. The heartbeat that looked like flashes of light on a screen was not incidental imagery. It was a living parable pressed into the moment by the hand of the One who knits life in secret places. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Body of Christ desperately needs a theology of *the ordinary moment held inside the holy one* — a framework for recognizing that God does not only speak in the spectacular. He speaks through ultrasound monitors at odd hours of the morning. He speaks through the framing of a single phrase — *just for a season* — that becomes the very thing holding a soul together when the worst seems to be happening. He speaks through conversations between brothers across continents, where business talk and theology and baby names and geopolitics all tumble together, and somewhere in the middle of it all a word gets spoken that is genuinely prophetic in its weight. If we only expect God at church, in formal prayer, or during curated worship experiences, we will miss the greater portion of what He is continuously saying. The saints must be trained to hear Him everywhere, because He is speaking everywhere — and little Elijah’s heartbeat, flashing like light in the dark of an ER examination room, is proof enough of that. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, hearing the account of this call, would sort it into the category of ordinary human experience — a couple had a pregnancy scare, went to the emergency room, received reassuring news, and the father called a friend to share his relief. The conversation that followed would be read as the meandering small talk of two young men: geopolitical speculation, jokes about British culture, business strategy, complaints about cities they dislike, and general life commentary. A secular observer might find warmth in the friendship, offer congratulations on the pregnancy, and perhaps note the genuine tenderness Seth shows in the way he speaks about his wife’s exhaustion and his wonder at seeing the ultrasound. The natural mind is capable of recognizing goodness here. It can celebrate friendship, new life, and resilience. What it cannot do is perceive the architecture underneath — the reason the heartbeat appeared as flashes of light carries meaning beyond biology, the reason the phrase *just for a season* is actually a spiritual technology, the reason the exchange about stepping into the Jordan before the waters part is not merely a motivational metaphor but a living theological principle with blood and history behind it. **The Limitation:** The difficulty is not that natural wisdom is entirely wrong about what it sees — it is that it stops too soon. It sees the surface of the water but cannot perceive what moves beneath it. The man who has not been born of the Spirit cannot receive the things that belong to the Spirit of God because they are spiritually discerned — not because God is being secretive, but because the instrument required to receive that frequency has not yet been calibrated by the new birth. What looks like a relieved father talking to a friend at four-thirty in the morning is, to eyes that have been opened, something far more layered: it is a testimony being built in real time, a theology of endurance being forged in the fire of an unexplained bleeding, a word about the interior exodus being spoken in the middle of a conversation about a business contact who talks about wanting ten thousand dollars a month but will not insert the effort. The natural mind cannot hold all of that at once, because it has not been taught by the One who authored all of it. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I cannot stop returning to the image of the heartbeat on the ultrasound screen — those little flashes of light, pulsing in the darkness of the womb. The radiologist confirmed it: those flashes *are* the heartbeat. What I saw was not metaphor. It was biological reality. And yet I feel the Spirit pressing me here to let that image carry its full theological weight, because Scripture does not allow us to speak of *light in darkness* as merely poetic. John opens his Gospel not with a genealogy but with a cosmology: *“In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God”* — and then, almost immediately, *“In Him was life, and the life was the light of men. And the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it”* (John 1:1, 4–5). The Greek word translated *comprehend* or *overcome* is καταλαμβάνω (katalambanō, kah-tah-lahm-BAH-noh) — to seize, to overtake, to suppress. The darkness tried and failed. And what I witnessed on that monitor was a tiny, pulsing demonstration of that same axiom: life pressing through unexplained darkness, flashing light that the threat of loss could not comprehend or overtake. I am also noticing something about the name we have been carrying for this child — Elijah. We named him Elijah before we knew whether he would survive the night. And that name is not a small thing. אֵלִיָּהוּ (Eliyyahu, eh-lee-YAH-hoo) — *my God is Yahweh*. It is a declaration of covenant fidelity, a name that announces whose side you are on and who your God actually is. The historical Elijah was a man whose ministry was marked precisely by moments where life returned from apparent death, where heaven answered fire, where a still small voice was more authoritative than earthquake or wind. That little boy — fourteen millimeters long, seven weeks and six days into his existence — carries a name that is already a declaration before he has drawn a single breath of outside air. There is something prophetic in that. Names in Scripture are not decorative. They are destinational. I am sitting with something else now — the word that came out of my own mouth almost involuntarily when I thought we were losing him: *“Blessed be the name of the Lord.”* I said it because of the framing. *Just for a season.* And I want to press into that framing with theological seriousness rather than treating it as merely a psychological coping mechanism, because I think the Spirit is revealing something here about how covenant people are meant to hold suffering. Job said it first, and he said it from ruins — *“The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD”* (Job 1:21). He did not say it because the pain was absent. He said it because his theological orientation was prior to and larger than the pain. The framing held him. And what I am finding is that the phrase *just for a season* is not a minimization of loss. It is a placement of loss inside the right container — the eternal perspective of One who wastes nothing and whose plans do not miscarry even when pregnancies seem to. There is a word that emerged in the conversation that I have not been able to set aside — the phrase *interior exodus*. I used it in the context of describing a brother who speaks the right language about wanting change, but whose feet are still dry on the bank of the Jordan. And the distinction I was pressing toward is critical: the exterior exodus, the visible departure from Pharaoh’s house, from wage slavery, from the monotony of a life that does not match what God has spoken — that exodus is frightening and real and costly. But the interior exodus, the deep restructuring of what a man actually *believes* is possible, is the harder journey. Israel left Egypt in a day. It took forty years to get Egypt out of Israel. The geography changed immediately. The theology did not. I am pressing deeper into this Jordan image now, because it carries extraordinary weight in the canon. Joshua 3 records that the priests were instructed to step into the flooded Jordan *first* — before the waters parted. *“And as those who bore the ark came to the Jordan, and the feet of the priests who bore the ark dipped in the edge of the water — for the Jordan overflows all its banks during the whole time of harvest — that the waters which came down from upstream stood still”* (Joshua 3:15–16). The miracle was contingent upon contact. The parting was not promised to those standing at a safe distance waiting for proof. It was activated by feet in floodwater. This is not a theology of recklessness. It is a theology of faithful motion. The man who prays for God to move without himself moving has confused prayer with passivity. He is treating God as what I called in the conversation a transactional vending machine — insert request, receive result — rather than as the covenant partner who says: *you move in the direction I have spoken, and I will part what you cannot part in your own strength.* And here the Spirit presses me to name what is actually wrong with the way *manifestation* language has crept into the vocabulary of otherwise sincere believers. It is not that desire is wrong. It is not that prayer is wrong. It is that when a person has been taught, even implicitly, to *call things into existence* through the force of focused thought or spoken declaration disconnected from obedient action, they have absorbed a doctrine that is ancient and pagan in its origin — the idea that the universe is raw material waiting to be shaped by the sufficiently evolved human will. This is not the Gospel. This is Gnosticism with a faith vocabulary layered over it. The New Covenant does not invite us into a relationship with a universe we can manipulate. It invites us into a relationship with a Person who acts according to His own sovereign purposes and who often partners those purposes with the practical, costly, unglamorous obedience of His people. As I said in the call: the road is narrow for a reason. It is unsexy. It requires the kind of sustained, daily, unsexy effort that the person who loves the *idea* of transformation will not long endure. I am also struck by what I observed about my parents in the conversation with Samuel — together since they were eighteen, now in their sixties, a marriage built before either of them had language for what they were building. And without making more of it than the moment supports, I notice that their covenant endurance is now reaching forward in time. The grandchild they are about to receive — little Elijah, seven weeks and six days old, flashing light on a monitor — is a fruit of their faithfulness. Covenant is generational. Proverbs confirms that *“a good man leaves an inheritance to his children’s children”* (Proverbs 13:22). The inheritance is not only financial. It is a model. It is a demonstration. It is the living proof that a man and a woman can choose each other every day for forty-plus years and still be choosing each other. That is a theological statement in a generation that does not believe such things are possible. There is also a small and gentle word here about wisdom and preparation — the bug-out bag, the pregnancy pillow, the inspection, the things that I initially dismissed as *middle-class people stuff*. And I want to be honest rather than self-congratulatory: there is a spirit of poverty not only in the bank account but in the imagination, and it sometimes masquerades as spiritual contentment or working-class authenticity. Wisdom builds and prepares. Proverbs 24:27 says *“Prepare your outside work, make it fit for yourself in the field; and afterward build your house.”* The ant, in Proverbs 6, stores in summer for winter without being commanded. Preparation is not faithlessness. It is the practical outworking of stewardship. The bag in the corner of the room is not a failure to trust God. It is what trust in God looks like when trust has legs. And finally I want to sit with something that ran underneath the entire conversation like a current — the quality of the friendship itself. Samuel and I did not speak only of theology. We laughed. We joked about swans and selfie sticks and the guards at the palace and ancient laws about carrying salmon. We talked about cities and business and e-bikes and AI parenting guides. And yet even in that lightness, the most important things kept surfacing — the baby, the heartbeat, the blessing of the Lord, the Jordan, the interior exodus, the fire that must match the confession. I think there is something the Spirit would say here: *this is what covenant brotherhood actually looks like*. It is not all solemn. It is not all weighty. It is the full texture of two lives pressing into each other with enough trust that the sacred and the ordinary are not kept in separate rooms. David had Jonathan. Paul had Timothy. I have brothers like Samuel — across an ocean, on a different time zone, awake with me at four-thirty in the morning to say: *“Man. Little Elijah lives.”* That is enough. That is more than enough. ## Implications and Challenges The theology of *just for a season* is a genuine gift, and it deserves more serious examination than it typically receives in Western Christianity, which tends either toward toxic positivity — *everything will be fine* — or toward a kind of anxious, unprocessed grief that does not know where to put suffering. The framing *this is for a season* is not denial. It is not minimization. It is the placement of the present moment inside the larger narrative of a God whose purposes are not derailed by what looks like disaster. The Church needs to teach this. Not as a coping strategy, but as a theology of time rooted in the biblical understanding that God inhabits eternity and has promised that all things — even this — are working together for good for those who love Him and are called according to His purpose (Romans 8:28). Suffering held inside that frame does not disappear, but it stops being the final word. The infiltration of manifestation theology into Christian prayer culture is a challenge that calls for clarity and compassion in equal measure. Many people who use this language are not being deceptive. They have genuinely absorbed it from environments that dressed New Age thought in scriptural language. The correction must begin with teaching what covenant relationship actually means — not a technique for bending reality to our will, but a life of alignment with the will and Word of the One who created reality. Prayer is not leverage. It is conversation. It is the posture of a child before a Father who already knows what we need, and who invites us to ask not because He needs to be informed but because the asking forms us into the kind of people who can receive the answer rightly. The distinction between the exterior and interior exodus is perhaps the most pastorally urgent insight in this conversation. Many people in the Church have made visible departures — from addiction, from destructive relationships, from dead religion — without completing the interior journey that those departures were meant to inaugurate. They have crossed the sea but they are still eating manna with an Egyptian imagination. The work of discipleship is not the single dramatic exit. It is the long, unglamorous reconstruction of the inner man — what Paul calls the renewing of the mind (Romans 12:2) — so that the new life on the outside has a corresponding architecture within. Without that interior transformation, the old Egypt reasserts itself. The work must go both ways. The image of hype without heat — a phrase Samuel used to describe his associate’s enthusiasm that does not translate into sustained effort — is a diagnostic worth applying carefully and humbly to ourselves before we apply it to others. It is possible to love the language of the Kingdom and still be unwilling to do the unsexy work of building it. It is possible to speak prophetically about your future and still choose comfort over the discipline that the future requires. The narrow road is not narrow because it is cruel. It is narrow because it requires the kind of sustained intention that a wide path never demands. This is not a message of condemnation. It is an invitation to honest inventory: *where does my confession outpace my obedience?* Finally — and this is both a challenge and a comfort — the Body of Christ needs to reclaim the theology of covenant friendship. We have, in much of contemporary Western Christianity, reduced fellowship to meeting attendance and reduced brotherhood to social media encouragement. What Samuel and I share — this kind of friendship where I call at four-thirty in the morning to say *little Elijah lives* and he receives that with genuine tears of relief — this is closer to the New Testament model of κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-noh-NEE-ah) — the deep participatory sharing of life, not merely information or activity. The Church does not need more programs. It needs more brothers and sisters who are genuinely inside each other’s lives, who know the name of the baby and the fear behind the ER visit and the scripture that held things together through the night. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity in the Spirit means learning to frame every season — including the terrifying ones — inside the larger architecture of God’s covenant faithfulness. It means developing a theology of *blessed be the name of the Lord* that is not performance but posture, not denial but declaration. It means letting the heartbeat on the monitor teach us what we already know but so easily forget: that life is authored by the One who defeated death, and that light flashing in darkness is not an accident — it is a sign. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The Jordan principle demands motion before the miracle. If God has spoken a direction over your life — over your family, your business, your calling, your restoration — then the obedient response is not to wait at the bank for the waters to part on their own. It is to put your feet in. The parting happens in the contact. Obedience is not the reward for answered prayer. It is often the condition under which answered prayer arrives. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic community must refuse both the passivity of waiting for God to do everything and the presumption of believing we can manufacture outcomes through the right verbal formula. We are not creators. We are stewards and heralds. Our posture is one of attentive alignment — watching for what the Father is doing, moving when He moves, speaking what He speaks, and holding the tension of *not yet* with the confidence of those who know that the same God who caused a fourteen-millimeter peanut to flash light in a dark room can cause anything He has promised to come to life on schedule. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You from this threshold — the fourth day of July, four-thirty in the morning, having just come from an ER room where medicine had no answer and You did — and I want to say *thank You* before I say anything else. Thank You for little Elijah’s heartbeat. Thank You that those flashes of light on the monitor are a living word from You, a sign written in biology and read by the Spirit, declaring that the darkness has not comprehended the life You placed in that womb. I stand as Your servant, as a co-heir of the covenant made in the blood of Your Son, and I make agreement with what You have already spoken: this child belongs to You, this family belongs to You, and what You have begun, You will complete. I pray for every brother and sister who reads this word — that the framing of *just for a season* would become not a platitude but a fortress in their minds, a genuine theological resting place when the bleeding makes no sense and the medicine offers no explanation. I pray against the infiltration of manifestation theology in the Church, and I invite the Holy Spirit to restore the saints to covenant prayer — honest, relational, costly, and full of the kind of faith that puts feet in floodwater before the waters have parted. Let the interior exodus be completed in every life that the exterior exodus has already begun. And let the Church rediscover the holy, ordinary, laughter-filled, midnight-call beauty of true covenant brotherhood — that κοινωνία (koy-noh-NEE-ah) that cannot be manufactured by programs but only grown by two people who actually let each other in. To You be all glory, in the name of Yeshua, the flashing light that all darkness has failed to overtake.
