# 08-16 Meditation: Renaissance Fair as a School of the Kingdom — Arrow, Armor, and Discernment

**Title:** 08-16 at 13:13 — A Renaissance Fair Made Holy: The Arrow, the Armor, the Whisper, and the Whole Animal

**Recorded:** 2026-08-16 13:13:04

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture)

**Classification:** Personal Meditation

## Opening Observation

I set out to record a family afternoon at the Renaissance festival in West Fargo, and what I carried home was an arrow, a weight of armor, a whispered warning over a tarot booth, and a fur trader’s confession that nothing is wasted. Between goats fed first and Bengal kittens I half-wanted to bring home, between a stunt performer’s plea for laughter and a gnome-fairy’s word about stickers, between armored combatants and a young alligator calmed by a hand on his belly, the Spirit kept pressing questions into me that would not let the day be only fun. What does it mean to commit, to aim, to be covered without being crushed, to discern the voice that steals the straight path, and to bring the whole animal of my life to a God who wastes nothing? An ordinary afternoon of festival play became, without my permission, a school of the Kingdom.

**Why This Matters:**

Discipleship is not built primarily in the sanctuary but in the unremarkable hours where we are being formed without noticing, and this single afternoon proved it many times over. The instructor’s word to me, “when you do it, you got to commit,” is the same word the Spirit speaks over every disciple tempted toward half-drawn obedience. The armored man’s honest account of bearing ninety pounds of steel exposed how the covering that saves us is itself a burden we must be conditioned to carry. The warning that rose through my own lips over the tarot booth named the enemy’s oldest strategy — the true fact wielded to redirect a life — while the makers of swords and stamps and pelts preached stewardship, refining, and the grammar of a gift that marks whose Kingdom we belong to. This matters because the Kingdom is unveiled in the small, and those with ears to hear will find their whole lives being catechized by moments they thought were only recreation. The Body of Christ must learn to hear the Spirit in the fairground and the checkout line as surely as in the pulpit.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

To the natural mind this is simply a pleasant, forgettable outing: a thirty-two-year-old man takes his family to a fair, fumbles an archery lesson, laughs when the string catches his lip, watches paid performers work the crowd for tips, sees young men in a troupe called the Twin Cities Wyverns hit each other in armor for Venmo donations, holds a baby alligator before it grows too large, hears a fortune-teller play to a crowd, and buys a few hand-carved souvenirs before heading home. The archery is a novelty, the axe throwing a photo opportunity, the armored combat a controlled-violence spectacle with an ambulance on speed dial, the tarot booth harmless entertainment or skilled cold-reading, and the crafts a charming inefficiency now that machines do it faster and cheaper. The gnome’s monologue about stickers is improvised whimsy, the fur trader’s thrift is mere survival skill, and the whole thing is nostalgia, showmanship, and the marketing of wonder on a hot summer day. There is truth in this reading, and no scorn is owed to it, because the men were genuine, the danger was genuine, the crafts were genuine, and the joy was genuine.

**The Limitation:**

But the natural mind is trained to file such hours under “leisure” and move on, never suspecting that the God who spoke through mustard seeds and lost coins is still speaking through hay bales, brigandines, tarot cards, and squirrel pelts. It can measure the pounds of armor but not the parable of covering; it can catalog the illegal strike zones but not hear the whisper of covenant restraint in them; it hears “not the full truth” and shrugs, assuming any truth is good and more information always better, never suspecting that accurate detail can be the very bait on a hook. It hears the performer begging for applause and feels mild pity, but does not recognize the ache of a soul made for a glory that tips cannot supply; it watches a gator’s belly rubbed calm without perceiving the astonishment that a bone-plated creature has a place beneath the armor where a hand still reaches. The flesh perceives surfaces and enjoys them, but the things of the Spirit are discerned only by the Spirit, and here the Spirit is speaking through soft fur and cold iron, snapped bowstrings and whispered omens, about how God makes, marks, refines, and keeps His own. The flesh sees a fair; the Spirit sees a field ready for harvest.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

I keep returning to the bale and the bow. I am a right-handed man, my wife’s purse of the day set down on the table, being told to take the arrow so that the nock faces me — one finger above, one finger below, aim at the tip. The stranger’s coaching is patient and exact: don’t use your thumb, give it a little push up, tuck that up and pull this one back. And then the release, and the string snapping against my own lip, and the laughter of my family. Fourteen years since I last did this, back in high school with hay bales, and my body half-remembers and half-fails. There is something tender in being coached again as an adult, in being made a beginner in front of the ones I am meant to lead.

What arrests me is the instructor’s earlier word, spoken almost as a throwaway: “when you do it, you got to commit.” It lands in me like a stone dropped into a deep well. So much of my obedience has been a half-draw — the bow lifted but never fully pulled, the arrow nocked but never released with the whole strength of my back. I hear in that offhand coaching the summons to a wholeness of intention I have often withheld, the difference between a man who dabbles and a man who looses the arrow with everything in him.

The first Scripture that opens to me is Paul’s own archer’s language: “I do not run with uncertainty. Thus I fight: not as one who beats the air” (1 Corinthians 9:26). Paul knew what it was to aim. The Hebrew for sin sharpens this. חָטָא (chata, khah-TAH) means to miss, to fail of the mark, to fall short of a goal one was aiming at. It is an archer’s word before it is ever a courtroom word. To sin, in this ancient hearing, is not first to break an abstract rule but to loose the arrow of one’s life and watch it sail past the glory of God. Standing at that bale, being told that if I shot over the top my turn was done and I would have to walk find the arrow, I felt the weight of חָטָא (khah-TAH) as something almost physical — the wasted shot, the walk of shame to recover what flew wide.

And the target itself, in the Greek, is σκοπός (skopos, sko-POSS), the mark toward which the runner strains: “I press toward the goal for the prize of the upward call of God in Christ Jesus” (Philippians 3:14). Between חָטָא (khah-TAH) and σκοπός (sko-POSS) lies the whole drama of sanctification — the arrow of the human life either loosed toward the upward call or wasted in the air. The coach’s rule that a shot over the bale ends the turn but sends you walking after the arrow is a small parable of grace and consequence together: there is a limit, there is a discipline, and there is mercy enough to send you retrieving rather than casting you off the field. The canon deepens this when I remember that God Himself is imaged as an archer, and His Servant as a polished arrow: “He has made Me a polished shaft; in His quiver He has hidden Me” (Isaiah 49:2). The Messiah is the arrow the Father draws to full commitment and looses at the mark of the world’s redemption — no half-draw there, no beating the air.

Then I wandered from that archery bale toward a field where young men strapped sixty to ninety pounds of steel and padding to their bodies to hit each other as hard as they legally could. I keep returning to a young man named Nathan describing the helmet — a full wooden fort around the head, shock padding, three millimeters of steel, strapped under the chin so it cannot be removed. He said a quarter to half of the entire weight rides on the head, and that if the kit is well made and strapped to the body it helps, because the load can be balanced onto the shoulders rather than crushing the neck. He had worn one gambeson so long the matting was coming out, all that stringy material spilling from the padding. What arrests me is that the covering is itself a burden — armor as something you must condition your body to bear, something fitted precisely or it will hurt you as surely as the enemy will. The protection and the affliction are woven into the same garment.

The Scripture every believer half-remembers opens here: “Put on the whole armor of God, that you may be able to stand against the wiles of the devil” (Ephesians 6:11). But I notice now what I never noticed before — Paul says *stand*, four times in that passage, not conquer, not advance. “Having done all, to stand” (Ephesians 6:13). The armored men spoke of the same thing: the whole contest comes down to three points of contact, and if your knee touches, you are out. The entire art is staying on your feet. The word for that armor is πανοπλία (panoplia, pan-op-LEE-ah) — the *full* armor, the complete panoply, not a single piece but the entire integrated kit. Nathan showed me this without knowing it, explaining how everything must be attached to everything else — the arms to the gambeson, the shoulders to the brigandine, the brigandine to the helmet by the timing strap. A single unstrapped piece becomes a soft spot, an open place where the enemy is permitted to land. I have too often worn my faith piecemeal, a helmet of salvation without the belt of truth, and wondered why the blows still reached my soft spots.

For those soft spots were the whole subject of his instruction. He explained with real care what is legal — that you may strike the back of the thigh, the kidneys, the open places, that you may even break bones — but three strikes are forbidden: the groin, the back of the neck, and the back of the knee. He grew sober speaking of them, saying a man could be shut off, paralyzed, that a boxer had recently died from such a blow. Even in combat there is covenant. And I hear in this the ancient wisdom that the tongue of the brethren is also a weapon: “no man can tame the tongue… with it we bless our God and Father, and with it we curse men, who have been made in the similitude of God” (James 3:8–9). There are places you do not hit a fellow saint — the shame he confessed in weakness, the fear that would paralyze him, the tender back of the knee where his whole ability to stand is rooted. “There is one who speaks like the piercings of a sword, but the tongue of the wise promotes health” (Proverbs 12:18). Yet a tension opens I cannot smooth over: those men were competitors, but we were never given each other to defeat. “We do not wrestle against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers” (Ephesians 6:12). How often the Body has taken its panoply, forged to withstand the devil, and turned it upon itself.

Then in the reptile tent the Kingdom mystery unveiled itself where I did not expect God to be. A young alligator, its back covered in what the keeper called armor plates that are actually bone, grown into the skin, a creature built to be impenetrable. And yet, when he grew rambunctious, the way to calm him was to rub his belly, the soft underside beneath the plating, and the keeper said plainly, “I know he can feel that.” The hardened, armored creature is reached not through his armor but beneath it. This is exactly how the Lord deals with the hardened: “I will take the heart of stone out of your flesh and give you a heart of flesh” (Ezekiel 36:26). He does not shatter the armor of the resistant soul with a hammer; He finds the soft underside and lays His hand there. And Christ Himself came not plated but pierced, not covered but exposed, that He might reach the soft underside of every hardened man.

But the afternoon turned again, and darker, when we drifted from grass-crazed servals and Bengal kittens to a booth where cards were being turned and a voice was interpreting a stranger’s life. Something in me would not stay a spectator. I heard myself telling my family, plainly, that they know everything you’ve done, that they never sleep, that they’ve watched you your whole life — and I remember the small “Oh no” that answered me. What arrested me was the precision of the danger I named: it may be true, but it’s not the full truth, and it’s meant to steer you off track from where you were going — God’s will. I did not say the whispers were false. I said they were partial, and aimed. The horror is not inaccuracy; the horror is accuracy in service of misdirection. This opened the garden, where the serpent does not begin with a bald lie but with a fact bent slightly: “You will not surely die. For God knows that in the day you eat of it your eyes will be opened” (Genesis 3:4–5). The serpent is נָחָשׁ (nachash, nah-KHASH) — a word carrying the sense of a serpent but also the whispered enchantment of an omen.

The canon is unambiguous about this channel: “There shall not be found among you anyone who… practices witchcraft… or one who interprets omens” (Deuteronomy 18:10–12), and the very next breath gives the alternative: “The LORD your God will raise up for you a Prophet like me… Him you shall hear” (Deuteronomy 18:15). The prohibition is the mercy that clears the air so we can hear the one Voice. In Acts, a slave girl with a spirit of divination — πύθων (python, POO-thone) — followed Paul crying *true words*, yet Paul, greatly annoyed, cast the spirit out, because the source poisons the stream. The issue was never truth versus falsehood but sonship versus surveillance. When my Father knows me — “O LORD, You have searched me and known me… You are acquainted with all my ways” (Psalm 139:1–3) — it is covenant love that anchors me. When the נָחָשׁ (nah-KHASH) knows me, that same knowing becomes a weapon to unmoor me. Satan is ὁ κατήγωρ (ho katēgōr, ho kat-AY-gore), “the accuser of our brethren… who accused them before our God day and night” (Revelation 12:10). When I said “they never sleep, they’ve watched you your whole life,” I was describing the ceaseless dossier of the accuser, whose accusations were nailed to a cross.

Later that same afternoon a man showed us two swords, appropriately weighted, one unsharpened and safe, another a mono-steel blade that would “cut someone” and *sings* through the air. Discernment is exactly this: two objects that look alike, one dulled and safe, one that will open you up, and only the one who knows steel can tell the difference by more than appearance. We must become a people who can tell the singing blade of the accuser from the dull toy of harmless fun — who know that a voice can be accurate and still be a knife. And the sword-maker told the story of the Ulfberht blade, steel so pure, forged in a clay-sealed crucible where no air could enter, that it could cut through other swords. The purity came from what was kept out during the fire. I heard Malachi before I could stop myself: “He is like a refiner’s fire… He will purify the sons of Levi, and purge them as gold and silver” (Malachi 3:2–3). The Hebrew is צָרַף (tsaraph, tsah-RAHF) — to smelt, to refine, to test by melting away the dross. The impurities are burned out precisely because the air is kept out, the heat sealed and sustained. A soul that keeps fleeing the fire never becomes a blade that can cut through anything.

And so the makers of that field became preachers I did not expect. The woodcarver said plainly that a machine-made stamp is a “great alternative” for anyone who “doesn’t want to spend a crap ton of time and money learning how to do this,” but “physically making these is an entire different act.” There is no laser-engraved sanctification. The fur trader spoke words I have not been able to set down: “A lot of us, we use the whole animal. We make needles out of the bone. Nothing’s wasted.” God Himself wastes nothing — He takes the very death of His Son and makes it the life of the world; He takes our failures, our furnaces, our stretched and cracked places, and works the whole animal of our lives into something useful. “And we know that all things work together for good to those who love God” (Romans 8:28). The cold axe head dug from the field of Agincourt, a real weapon from a real slaughter held in gloved hands, testifies that God does not forget what happened on the fields of history; He stores even the tears of the saints in His bottle (Psalm 56:8). And then the trinket, pressed into a child’s hand with an explanation almost as an afterthought: when you visited a foreign land, the kings who hosted you gave you a token, and everyone who saw it knew which king it came from. The token was given, not earned — the whole logic of grace. Paul writes that “having believed, you were sealed with the Holy Spirit of promise, who is the guarantee of our inheritance” (Ephesians 1:13–14), the ἀρραβών (arrabōn, ar-rah-BONE), the down payment of the same substance as the glory to come.

Then the gnome-fairy, in full costume, crouched down to speak over one of the children, and unknowingly prophesied. He spoke of the childhood sticker given for a good speech, proof you could wear or trade or paste in a book. “Now as an adult,” he said, “they’ll tell you if they think you’re doing a good job or not. They don’t give stickers.” I laughed with everyone, and then the laughter went quiet inside me. He had named the exact wound the performers were bleeding from — one after another they came off their stages pleading for laughter, applause, and cash: “your laughter, your applause are absolutely two things that truly motivate me to do this.” Here are souls whose worth rises and falls with the crowd’s response, and is that not the very hunger the flesh feeds in all of us? This is what the Lord confronted when He said the hypocrites “have their reward” already (Matthew 6:1–4). The gospel answer to a world with no more stickers is a Father who is Himself the sticker — the eternal Witness who sees every hidden shot, every unclapped act of love. “Well done, good and faithful servant” (Matthew 25:21) is the reward the buskers ache for and cannot secure from any crowd.

So I find myself repenting at a Renaissance fair. I confess the half-drawn bow of my obedience and the arrows I have loosed toward the applause of men rather than the σκοπός (sko-POSS) of the upward call. I confess that I have wanted to be armored — competent, unreachable, plated in bone so that no blow could land — and worn my defenses so long that, like Nathan’s gambeson, the matting is coming out and I did not notice. I confess how easily my own heart shops for a voice to interpret my life, trusting the accurate whisper over the plain, unflattering word of God that simply says *trust Me and walk*. And I confess that I have fled the crucible, prying open the fire God sealed for my purity, wasting what He calls precious while claiming to follow a God who wastes nothing. For the beloved brethren this means our whole discipleship is being catechized in the ordinary. We were built to help one another bear the weight of the covering, to honor the strike zones we must never touch, to be the calming hand on the belly of the hardened brother, to keep our heading fixed on God’s revealed will against every counterfeit knowing, and to labor for an audience of One who wastes nothing and marks everything as His own.

## Implications and Challenges

**The half-drawn bow of Western discipleship.** The coach’s word, “when you do it, you got to commit,” exposes how much of our faith is lifted but never loosed. We nock the arrow of Sunday attendance, small-group membership, and vaguely good intentions, but rarely pull the string with the whole strength of our back. Paul refused to fight “as one who beats the air” (1 Corinthians 9:26), yet much of modern churchgoing is precisely that — motion without a mark. Recovering חָטָא (khah-TAH) as an archer’s word reframes repentance for a generation that hears “sin” only as legal violation: to sin is to loose the one life we were given and watch it sail past the glory of God. The challenge is not to add more religious activity but to draw the bow we are already holding all the way back. Grace does not lower the target; it strengthens the arm, and the overshot that ends the turn still sends us walking after the arrow rather than casting us off the field.

**The burden of being covered, and the soft belly beneath the bone.** We speak easily of the armor of God as though putting it on were the end of the struggle, but Nathan’s honest account of conditioning his body to bear a quarter of the weight on his head alone should chasten our sentimentality. Ephesians 6 never promises comfort; it promises we will still be standing when the evil day has done its worst (Ephesians 6:13), and much of maturity is simply the long conditioning to remain inside the kit without being crushed. Yet the reptile tent presses the opposite and equal truth: if we have grown so plated that no gentle hand is felt beneath our defenses, we have not achieved strength but isolation, and God’s own method — the heart of stone exchanged for a heart of flesh (Ezekiel 36:26) — finds no purchase in us. The mature saint is not the one who feels nothing but the one who has kept a soft place where the Father, and trusted brethren, may still lay a calming hand. A protection that never burdens you is probably not the panoply Paul described, and a strength that can no longer be gentled is not the heart of flesh He promised.

**Mistaking the brother for the opponent, and the illegal strike zones of the tongue.** Those armored men, engaged in real violence, honored with sobriety the three places they would not strike, because a blow there could paralyze or kill — the Church, engaged in a war of tongues, honors such zones far less. Proverbs says reckless words are like sword-thrusts while the tongue of the wise brings healing (Proverbs 12:18), and James warns the same mouth blesses God and curses men made in His image (James 3:9–10). We must relearn the forbidden zones — the confessed shame, the paralyzing fear, the back of the knee where a saint’s whole standing is rooted — and refuse to strike there even when we could. Worse still, the tournament exists to defeat the one across the lists, but the Kingdom does not; our wrestling is against principalities, not flesh and blood (Ephesians 6:12). Every time I have aimed my competence at a fellow believer as though winning against him were the goal, I have left the actual enemy untouched and wounded a soldier of my own field.

**The true-sounding whisper and the accusing machine that never sleeps.** Modern Western Christians have quietly outsourced discernment to entertainment, treating tarot, horoscopes, and “manifesting” as harmless flavor rather than the divination Deuteronomy 18 names as abomination. What rose through me at that booth was not that the cards lie, but that their accuracy is the bait; a church that only warns against “false” fortune-telling has missed that the true-sounding whisper is the more effective trap, and we must judge the source, not merely the accuracy, of every voice. Yet the same words I spoke — “they know everything, they never sleep, they’ve watched you” — describe the algorithmic surveillance we invite into our pockets and let interpret our desires daily. Psalm 139 gives the only cure: a Father whose knowledge of us is covenant love, not reconnaissance, and the saints who cannot tell surveillance from shepherding will be herded by their own data. Guard against the bent trajectory more than the dramatic fall, for the enemy wins not usually by grand apostasy but by plausible redirection, one degree at a time, until the ship arrives at the wrong continent.

**The applause economy and a world with no more stickers.** The performers’ honest plea to be paid in laughter and applause is a mirror the church rarely wants to look into, for Jesus warned that those who serve to be seen “have their reward” already (Matthew 6:2), and our platforms and even private devotions are riddled with the quiet angling for a visible clap. This is not a call to despise those who labor for wages, but to examine what actually motivates our service — the σκοπός (sko-POSS) of the upward call, or the basket at the end of the stage. The gnome accidentally named the ache of maturity: much modern burnout and desperate self-promotion flows from souls starving for a sticker that no longer comes. The gospel answer is not to manufacture endless affirmation but to know the Father who sees in secret and rewards openly (Matthew 6:4). We were never meant to outgrow being seen; we were meant to grow up into the gaze of the only Witness whose “well done” outlasts every crowd.

**No laser-engraved sanctification, and the God who wastes nothing.** The woodcarver’s honesty — that machine-made stamps are a fine alternative but hand-carving is “an entire different act” — is a mercy and a warning: sanctification cannot be outsourced or accelerated, but is carved by the Spirit through the long obedience of a real life kept in the sealed crucible (Malachi 3:3). Western Christianity has built an industry around avoiding the sustained heat that refines, prying the lid off the furnace the moment it grows uncomfortable, and so producing decorative swords, beautiful on the wall and useless in battle. The fur trader’s “nothing’s wasted” is a discipleship principle before it is a survival skill, convicting a culture that discards people, seasons, and sorrows the moment they seem unproductive; God makes needles from the bone and redeems the parts of our story we would throw away (Romans 8:28). And against every half-draw, every missed mark, every fled furnace stands the one perfectly aimed Shaft, hidden in the Father’s quiver and loosed at the cross (Isaiah 49:2) — the trinket of belonging pressed freely into our hands, the ἀρραβών (ar-rah-BONE) of an inheritance we could never purchase. Receive the token, beloved, and stop trying to buy what has already been given.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

Maturity in Christ is learning to draw the bow all the way back and aim at the right target even when no one is watching, to bear the weight of the covering without being crushed, and at the same time to keep a soft place beneath the armor where God can still reach. These disciplines seem opposed but they are one: the saint grows strong enough to stand in the evil day precisely because he has never plated over the belly where the Father’s hand rests. Maturity is also learning that being *known* is not the same as being *loved*, and that accuracy is not authority — the son in me returns the longing to be seen to the Father who searched me in covenant, not surveillance, so that I can hear a true-sounding whisper and still refuse it because I have weighed the source. And maturity means staying in the sealed crucible rather than prying it open at the first discomfort, receiving my belonging as a gift and then submitting to the long, hand-carved work of formation that no shortcut can produce. I want to stop measuring my growth by how unreachable I have become and start measuring it by whether I can still be gentled, still be corrected, still be aimed.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

The coach’s word is the Lord’s word: when you do it, you have to commit — so identify the half-draws in your life and pull them all the way back today. Obedience calls me to honor the forbidden strike zones in my brethren with the same sobriety those armored men gave to theirs, to stop treating fellow soldiers as opponents in a tournament, and to redirect every energy I have wasted defeating brothers toward the actual principalities and powers (Ephesians 6:12). It calls me to renounce every rival oracle, however entertaining, and return my ear to the one Prophet God commanded us to hear, teaching my household to bring every “knowing” voice under the plain Word before we let it near our heading. And it calls me to waste nothing — to bring the whole animal of my life to God, including the bones and the failures, to steward creation and sorrow with reverence, to be the one who stands beside a weary saint and helps him strap the load onto shoulders that can bear it, and to wear my seal openly, letting the world see whose King has hosted me.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

This trains us to keep our ears open in the least likely places, discerning the Spirit’s voice through buskers, coaches, armored strangers, and costumed gnomes who prophesy more than they know, refusing to divide our lives into sacred hours and empty ones. The watchman’s posture is to see the sermon underneath the show, to keep the soft underside tender toward God while the world hardens around us, and to know which blows are permitted and which are the back of the neck. It is to keep a fixed heading on God’s revealed will and read every other whisper against that fixed point, watching for the bent trajectory more than the dramatic fall, and testing the steel of every voice by more than its appearance. And it is to remain on our feet through the long melee, having done all, still standing, still soft where it counts, still helping the man beside us carry the weight of his covering — naming the ache we hear in every applause-hungry, sticker-starved soul and pointing them to the Father whose gaze outlasts every crowd (Ephesians 6:13).

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I come to You from the middle of an ordinary afternoon that You made holy without my permission, and I thank You that You will not leave me alone even at a fairground. I confess the half-drawn bow of my obedience, the arrows I have loosed toward the applause of men, and the many times my life has flown wide of Your glory in the missing of the mark; thank You that You have not cast me off the field but sent me walking after the arrow to draw again in Your mercy. Search me, Lord, and show me the forbidden places I have struck in Your other children, and forgive me for treating fellow soldiers as opponents while the true enemy walked the field untouched; lay Your hand upon the soft underside beneath all the bone I have grown to survive, and gentle me until the rambunctious hardness in me grows still under Your touch. I renounce the נָחָשׁ and its hiss of bent truth, I refuse the κατήγωρ who never sleeps, and I return my whole ear to the one Prophet You raised up, whose blood has already answered every accusation ever gathered against me. Keep me in the sealed crucible until the dross of my divided heart is burned away, and teach me to bring You the whole animal of my life, even the bones and the shameful parts, trusting that You waste nothing. Thank You for Jesus, the polished Shaft hidden in Your quiver, aimed perfectly at the cross so that His accuracy might cover my clumsy shots, pierced rather than plated that He might reach us all beneath our armor. As Your co-heir and Your servant, sealed with the ἀρραβών of Your Spirit and marked as one hosted at Your table, I make agreement with Your will to labor for an audience of One and to stop begging the crowd for what only Your face can give. Make me a coach’s hand to the exhausted performers among Your children, a calming hand for the hardened brother, a helping shoulder for the burdened one, and a clear voice of discernment for every soul that fears the singing blade. Bring the children You have given me back to fields like these one day, and let them find You already there. I bless You for the arrow, the armor, the whispered warning, the whole animal, the laughter, the snapped string against my lip, and the quiet voice beneath it all. In the name of Jesus Christ, come in the flesh, who never missed the mark, amen.
