# 07-18 Call Reflection: The Wilderness Season and Speaking to the Rock

**Title:** 07-18 at 11:39 — The Tent Maker in the Wilderness: Speak to the Rock

**Recorded:** 2026-07-18 11:39:55

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture)

**Classification:** Call Reflection

## Opening Observation

I sat with my brother Samuel across a screen and across oceans in what may be the rawest hour of his life — homeless, penniless, freshly divorced, uprooted from a life of ten thousand a month and set down in the farmland of Somerset with nothing but a backpack, a laptop he weighed selling, and a strange peace settling over a man with nowhere to lay his head. As I listened, I heard fear and faith wrestling in the same breath, and I felt the Spirit pressing something ancient through the whole exchange: that the wilderness is not abandonment but appointment. What arrested me most was the pattern beneath every word — a spiritual warfare season, then immediate testing, then a wilderness — and woven through it a man being “peaceably forced” out of Egypt, learning whether he would strike the rock or speak to it.

**Why This Matters:**

This matters because the modern disciple is trained to read collapse as curse and comfort as blessing, when Scripture repeatedly reverses that reading. The Body of Christ has largely lost the language for the wilderness — we know how to celebrate deliverance but have forgotten that deliverance is almost always followed by testing, by stripping, by a season where faith is either falsified or fortified. When a brother loses everything and cries out, “What is the Lord trying to teach me by making me homeless?” he is standing at the exact threshold where Israel stood after the Red Sea — freed, but not yet home, afraid the manna might not fall. How we interpret such a moment — whether we medicate it, flee it, or yield to it — shapes whether the wilderness becomes a grave or a womb. For the Body of Christ, learning to walk beside the newly stripped without either flattering their pain or fearing it is a discipleship we cannot afford to neglect.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

By natural reasoning, Samuel’s situation is simply a cascade of failures: a marriage dissolved, an income evaporated, a family fractured over petty arguments about a couch, and a man now sleeping rough because his mother told him to leave and then took the words back too late for pride to reverse. The world would call this rock bottom, a statistical fall from success into destitution, and would prescribe the homeless line, a shelter, a return to hustling, or perhaps — as the joke about the mental institution hinted — psychiatric intervention. The natural mind admires the hustle: he will sleep in a 24-hour gym, ration cafe purchases for water and warmth, sell his laptop, use ChatGPT to map out charging stations and cafés, even fabricate a receipt to score a cinema seat where he can nap and charge his phone. It admires the grit — he “finessed” a corporation, he will play pool for cash, become a sales legend, eat gladiator porridge and get shredded, spin his homelessness into content for a testimony channel. His father’s cold “you’ll be fine” reads as neglect; his talk of Thailand and a Muay Thai fight camp reads as escape fantasy. The mind of the flesh sees a man who “doesn’t know how the fuck we got here,” and either pities him as a victim or diagnoses him as the author of his own ruin. Even his instinct to send back the money mistakenly wired to him would look, to worldly wisdom, like foolishness — the one day he could have used it, he gave it away. The talk of God, in this frame, is coping — a comforting story he tells himself to keep despair at bay.

**The Limitation:**

But natural reasoning cannot see the hand that is doing the stripping. It measures a man by what he holds, so when the hands are emptied it declares the man empty. It cannot perceive that the same providence which removed the ten thousand a month may be the providence positioning him beside a farm, beside free soup, beside a gym with showers, beside a Tuesday viewing for a room — small mercies scattered like manna that the anxious eye reads as coincidence. It sees a man scrambling for bread and cannot see the One who withholds the bread to teach that man does not live by bread alone. It reads the father’s terse call as rejection and cannot perceive that the world running out of things to offer is often the very moment the ravens are dispatched. And it cannot account for the peace that fell precisely when there was no earthly reason for peace — “just then as you said that, I just got this feeling that everything was going to be okay.” The flesh interprets loss as the absence of God; the Spirit knows that God often draws nearest in the very hour our props are pulled away. What looks like a man falling apart is, in the Spirit, a man being taken apart on purpose — a man being weaned.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

As I listened to my brother describe his stripping, I heard him himself name the thing before I could: “I’m literally in the wilderness. This is literally my time in the wilderness to the fullest extent.” Something in me recognized that he had stumbled onto holy ground without knowing it, because the wilderness in Scripture is never merely a place of deprivation — it is the school of dependence. Israel was led into the מִדְבָּר (midbar, meed-BAR) — wilderness, uncultivated place — not to be destroyed but to be humbled and proven, “that He might make you know that man shall not live by bread alone; but man lives by every word that proceeds from the mouth of the LORD” (Deuteronomy 8:3). The very anxiety over bread that grips Samuel is the classroom Israel walked, and it is the classroom the Son Himself entered when the Spirit drove Him into the same wilderness to be tested on that identical word.

What the Spirit pressed on me first was the sequence. Samuel and I had just come through what I can only describe as a spiritual warfare season — a casting away, a clearing of his atmosphere — and then, almost in the same breath, he was called into the wilderness. I could not help but hear Mark’s Gospel, where the moment the Spirit descends upon Jesus at His baptism, εὐθύς (euthys, yoo-THOOS) — immediately — the same Spirit drives Him into the wilderness to be tempted. Deliverance did not lead to comfort; it led to confrontation. And I am seeing that this is not the exception in Scripture; it is the pattern. You cannot be in the wilderness unless you have first come out of Egypt, out of bondage. So the very fact that Samuel is in the wilderness is testimony that he has already been brought out. The stripping he endures is not God removing His favor; it is God removing Egypt from the man. This is why he could say, “Now there’s no option. Now there’s no going back” — for Israel could not truly follow God until the Red Sea closed behind them (Exodus 14). The removal of retreat is not cruelty; it is the geography of faith.

I told him what the Spirit kept pressing from Hebrews — that God “chastens” those He loves, and the word there, παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) — fatherly training, the discipline of a son — is not punishment but formation. The atheist argues that suffering disproves a loving God; Scripture argues the opposite: that this intimate, tailored testing is the proof of sonship, not its contradiction. “Whom the LORD loves He chastens, and scourges every son whom He receives” (Hebrews 12:6). And when I mentioned my own child, Samuel said, “I wanted to be a man, so He said, okay, do what a man does.” The Father’s discipline always looks like pressure before it looks like love — both of us, in different measures, being fathered through burden into responsibility.

Then something remarkable happened that I can only read as the Father’s tenderness — Samuel told me an old Christian man crossed his path while he charged his phone, and the old man told him to be grateful, that God was putting him through this for a reason. I heard in that the ministry of the ravens. Before the manna, before the tent, before any of it, God sent an aged saint to speak a single word of endurance. This is how the Father works in the wilderness: not always with a rescue, but often with a witness. And He scattered other confirmations through the day like breadcrumbs — the Nigerian smoker in the cinema who turned out to know a Baptist church, “you meet Christians in the world.” I told Samuel about the woman in room 307, my neighbor of over half a year I had never once seen, playing her charismatic worship loud by the elevator until she caught herself, and I said, “No, I love it, we need it.” These are not coincidences to the watching eye; they are the Father decorating the wilderness with reminders that His people are everywhere.

I keep returning to the first test. Money arrived that was not his — twelve pounds accidentally sent by a man named Caleb — and Samuel, at rock bottom, hungry, needing it desperately, sent it back. I told him plainly: that was the first temptation of the wilderness, and it was Christ’s own first temptation. Satan came to a starving Jesus and said, “If You are the Son of God, command that these stones become bread.” The enemy always attacks the identity first — “if You are the Son” — and always offers a legitimate need met through an illegitimate means. Samuel passed a test he did not even realize was a test. His faith was stronger than he knew, and God allowed the temptation precisely so Samuel could discover the strength already deposited in him. He said it himself: “If I do the right thing, the Lord will help. I just gotta keep doing the right thing.” That is the widow of Zarephath making the cake for the prophet first, out of her last handful of flour, before feeding herself and her son (1 Kings 17). It is the counterintuitive economy of the Kingdom, where obedience precedes provision.

Yet the same man who returned the twelve pounds later celebrated a fabricated receipt as a “hundred IQ move,” and here the Spirit put a Scripture in my mouth almost sideways. In the wilderness, Israel still got water even though Moses struck the rock instead of speaking to it (Numbers 20:8–11). The water still came — נָכָה (nakah, naw-KAW), to strike, smite — but Moses was kept from the Promised Land for it, because he “did not hallow” God before the people. Samuel caught it instantly: “Amen, that’s all I did, bro, I just struck a rock.” That recognition was the Spirit working, not me. For there is a difference between provision seized by cunning and provision received by trust. The rock Moses was first told to strike (Exodus 17:6) he was later told only to speak to, because the striking had already happened once and Christ, our Rock, “was struck” once for all — “that Rock was Christ” (1 Corinthians 10:4). To strike the rock again is to treat as unfinished the very thing God has finished. So when I told him, “This time don’t strike it — speak to the rock,” I was not scolding; I was inviting him into the higher way, the way of asking rather than finessing. And his response moved me — “It’s okay, you’ll work that out with the Lord… there is always time for redemption.” He did not spiral into condemnation, and neither did I press him there. Even the misused shortcut got him “somewhere safe for a couple of hours.” That too is mercy — but the invitation is always upward, toward the cleaner miracle.

I notice how naturally Christ Himself surfaced as the one Unshakable thing when we spoke of his whole environment coming apart. The Spirit pressed on me the word from Hebrews, quoting Haggai: “Yet once more I shake not only the earth, but also heaven” (Hebrews 12:26), σαλεύω (saleuō, sah-LEW-oh) — to shake, agitate — “that the things which cannot be shaken may remain.” When I reached for the image of the Rock, I was reaching for Peter’s confession — “You are the Christ, the Son of the living God” — and the Lord’s response, “on this rock I will build My church” (Matthew 16:16, 18). The Greek makes the mystery sing: Peter is Πέτρος (Petros, PEH-tros) — a stone — but the church is built on the πέτρα (petra, PEH-trah) — the massive bedrock, the revelation itself. Everything else in Samuel’s life was Petros, small and movable; only the petra endures. Samuel diagnosed it himself from another angle: “I was too comfortable… that’s what my weapon was. That’s what was killing me.” Comfort is rarely named as an enemy in our churches, yet here is a homeless man who tasted its slow strangling — for “the cares of this world, the deceitfulness of riches, and the desires for other things entering in choke the word” (Mark 4:19), where μέριμνα (merimna, MER-im-nah) — anxious care — is the very strangler.

Something in me stirs at the tent. Almost playfully we spoke of it, but the Spirit was in the play — for Paul was σκηνοποιός (skēnopoios, skay-no-poy-OSS) — tentmaker — and this theologian-turned-laborer wrote from that very trade that “I have learned in whatever state I am, to be content” (Philippians 4:11). The tent is the dwelling of the pilgrim who has “no continuing city, but we seek the one to come” (Hebrews 13:14) — Abraham dwelling in tents, “heirs with him of the same promise,” waiting for “the city which has foundations, whose builder and maker is God” (Hebrews 11:9–10). And I am pressed further, for our own bodies are tents: “if our earthly house, this tent, is destroyed, we have a building from God” (2 Corinthians 5:1). The Greek σκῆνος (skēnos, SKAY-noss) — tent, tabernacle — names the frailty of the flesh we all inhabit, homeless or housed. In one sense, every one of us is homeless; we are all camped in a fragile tent, awaiting a permanent house not made with hands. And the God who tabernacled among us — “the Word became flesh and dwelt (ἐσκήνωσεν, eskēnōsen, es-KAY-no-sen — pitched His tent) among us” (John 1:14) — knows the nomad’s life intimately.

I notice the tension between fear and provision running through the whole exchange like a taut cord. “I am absolutely fucking terrified,” he confessed, and I did not rebuke the fear, because fear is honest and the Psalms are full of it. His honest cry was “help my unbelief” made visible — the same frightening prayer I confess to praying, because God answers it. There is still, in most of us, an interior Judas, an atmosphere of unbelief coexisting with real faith; Samuel would say “I trust the Lord” and worry about finances in the same breath, and I recognized it instantly because I do the same. But there is no secular versus spiritual, no wall between the bills and the belief. The bills are spiritual. The gym couch is spiritual. And even as he named his terror, the provisions kept surfacing: showers at the gym, free soup at the food company, three meals from the vending machine, free factory yogurt, a room viewing booked for Tuesday. The raven-fed prophet, the manna-fed nation, the disciples gathering baskets of fragments — the God of Scripture provides not usually in abundance but in daily sufficiency, “Give us this day our daily bread” (Matthew 6:11), exactly the ration of the wilderness where מָן (man, mahn) — manna — could not be hoarded overnight without breeding worms (Exodus 16:19–20).

This is why I told Samuel to stop praying vague prayers. Strength, faith, patience are real, but they are hard to see answered, and there is a spiritual comfort in praying for what cannot be measured and therefore cannot be seen to fail. The Father is a specific Person, so I urged him to pray for the tent, for the exact amount he is missing. When you ask for five pounds and forty-one pence and it arrives to the penny, there is no room left for doubt — that is the glory of God made undeniable, for the vague answer can be attributed to chance, but the exact answer can only be attributed to the Father who numbers the hairs on our heads. And when I said, “Just ask the Father and He’ll give it to you,” echoing “everyone who asks receives” (Matthew 7:8), αἰτέω (aiteō, eye-TEH-oh) — to ask, request — his answer stopped me: “Well apparently you already have, because in about a minute and a half, it starts.” Buried in the jest was a real testimony — that the asking and receiving are already woven together, that the Father is already ahead of the need, the childlike faith Jesus said we must have to enter the Kingdom (Matthew 18:3).

I keep returning, too, to when I told him to take inventory — “how many loaves do you have?” — echoing Jesus before the multitude, who did not ask what the crowd lacked but “How many loaves do you have? Go and see” (Mark 6:38). The Kingdom economy never begins with the absence; it begins with the little that is present, blessed, broken, and multiplied, ἄρτος (artos, AR-tos) — loaf, bread. And my brother took it up instantly: “I got a roof over my head. I got a couch to sleep on.” Then the doxology broke out in the middle of desperation — “I called people and I had food. Glory to God. All glory to God.” To count loaves and give thanks is a eucharistic act, for εὐχαριστία (eucharistia, yoo-khar-is-TEE-ah) — thanksgiving — is the very word of the Lord’s Table. His oats and free yogurt become a strange communion, daily bread received with thanksgiving from the hand of God. Yet I do not want to baptize everything uncritically, for his plan to “play games for cash” and become “an absolute pool legend” carries the flavor of the same hustle-and-glory the world sells; the Spirit’s invitation is dependence, not a new and more spiritual self-reliance.

That encounter with Thomas arrested me deeply. Here is a man who once fed a homeless person only to have his phone stolen, and now, homeless himself, he meets Thomas — another man further down the same road, “off his head,” as Samuel put it, “been out there too long.” When Samuel had nothing to give but prayer and Thomas refused even that, telling him not to “breach” him, I felt the ache. This is the very scene the Lord described: “whoever will not receive you… shake off the dust from your feet” (Luke 10:11). But it is also a mirror — Thomas is what the road makes of the bitter; the question hanging over Samuel is what the same road will make of the surrendered. And I saw the calling hidden inside the crisis. We spoke of him making tents for other homeless people, teaching the skill, lobbying businesses to remove the spikes meant to drive the vulnerable away — and he said, “That’s not a bad idea at all… if we could house people, that’s exactly what I had thought of.” Here the Spirit began to turn the wound into a well, for the one comforted in tribulation becomes able to comfort those in any trouble “with the comfort with which we ourselves are comforted by God” (2 Corinthians 1:4). A man who has slept rough carries an authority to serve the roofless that no comfortable philanthropist can purchase. His descent may be his commissioning.

The staff pressed on me as well. When Samuel asked what the Lord could be teaching him, the words that rose in me were “absolute dependence on Him,” and immediately the sending of the seventy-two came to mind. “Carry neither money bag, knapsack, nor sandals” (Luke 10:4), yet in the parallel commissioning He permitted a staff (Mark 6:8). It was no small thing that Samuel, without prompting, answered “except for a staff?” and said he needed to find a good walking stick. The staff is the one permitted possession — the emblem of a pilgrim who leans, the shepherd’s rod that both steadies and defends. Even in being stripped, God does not leave His sent ones without something to lean upon, and that something is always a sign of relationship rather than self-sufficiency.

What broke open in me most, though, was Job. Samuel’s father called and said only “you’ll be fine” and hung up, and it wounded him deeply. I saw Job’s friends — theologically correct, and yet no comfort at all — insisting he must have sinned. Job did not need a sermon; he needed seven days of silence and the freedom to wrestle. And when God finally answered from the whirlwind, סְעָרָה (se’arah, seh-ah-RAH) — whirlwind, storm — I have come to read it differently than I once did. I used to hear only “you are small, I am God.” Now I hear a Father inviting Job into the sheer, staggering complexity of creation — “Do you know how the rain is made? How the rainbow is formed? I am holding all of this together, and still, here I am, in front of you, answering.” That is not the voice of a distant deity but of a God with time and sovereignty enough to attend to one suffering man and the whole cosmos at once. This is what I want Samuel to hear beneath his father’s cold three words — that his true Father is bent low and present. And I do not rush past his wound; the Lord is “near to those who have a broken heart, and saves such as have a contrite spirit” (Psalm 34:18). This is why I reached for the Psalms — nearly every lament travels the same road, honest complaint poured out before God, resolving into trust and praise: “Why are you cast down, O my soul? … Hope in God, for I shall yet praise Him” (Psalm 42:11), where תּוֹחֵלֶת (tocheleth, toh-KHEH-leth) — hope, expectation — is the resting place of the wrestling soul.

There is the vinegar, too. I keep thinking of Christ on the cross, thirsting after six hours, and the last thing the world offered Him was a sponge of sour wine. When the world gave Him that, He knew it had nothing left to offer, and He gave up His spirit — τετέλεσται (tetelestai, teh-TEL-es-tie), “it is finished,” the debt paid in full. Samuel has now tried every option the world holds, and one by one they have offered him vinegar. But for the believer, the moment the world runs dry is not the moment of death — it is the moment the Father’s provision begins. The ravens are already in flight. And I marvel that this whole covenant discipleship is happening across oceans between two men who have never met face to face — “we have a shared testimony,” Samuel said, “which is actually even more powerful, even more weighty.” In the mouth of two or three witnesses every word is established (2 Corinthians 13:1), and the brethren overcame “by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony” (Revelation 12:11), μαρτυρία (marturia, mar-too-REE-ah) — testimony, witness. When Samuel wondered if this might be his time to “come home,” I could only tell him what I believe: it is this life that continues — we sleep, and we are raised, in a body we cannot yet imagine, where there will be no more need for tears. We will have our grand reunion, if not in this life, then in the resurrection.

I keep returning to his phrase, “it’s almost like I was pushed to do this… peaceably forced.” This is precisely how I understand being led by the Spirit at its deepest. There are the light impressions — do not talk to that coworker, go this way, wait here — and those are real. But there is also a heavier sovereignty in which God moves the very pieces of a man’s environment to press him out of Sodom, the way the angels physically seized Lot’s hand: “the men took hold of his hand… the LORD being merciful to him” (Genesis 19:16), חָזַק (chazaq, khaw-ZAK) — to seize, take hold firmly. Lot hesitated, and mercy dragged him. Samuel hesitated in his old life, and mercy dislodged the whole house. We have free will, and yet God can move even our will up to the measure He allows. And I found deep comfort in the manna and the mended clothes — an entire generation raised their children in the wilderness with clothes that did not wear out, sandals that did not break (Deuteronomy 8:4). If He clothed a faithless generation, He will not abandon a son who sent back the twelve pounds. He is teaching Samuel, as He taught Paul, that we carry this treasure in earthen vessels, ὀστράκινος (ostrakinos, os-TRAH-kee-nos) — made of clay, earthenware — clay pots God can crush and reform, “that we should not trust in ourselves but in God who raises the dead” (2 Corinthians 1:9), ἐξαπορέομαι (exaporeomai, ex-ah-por-EH-oh-my) — to despair utterly — the whole curriculum of the streets tonight.

The final picture the Spirit gave me was Peter in prison, chains falling off in the middle of a midnight prayer, the angel striking the iron doors open (Acts 12:6–7), προσευχή (proseuchē, pros-yoo-KHAY) — prayer. It was while they were praying that the doors blew open. This is the shape of the whole conversation: a man with nowhere to sleep is being invited to become a man who prays until the doors open. Not who finesses the doors, not who strikes the rock — but who speaks, and watches the Rock become a river. He reached for the image of the gladiator — the Roman fighter fed on cheap porridge, hardened for the arena — not knowing how close that sits to Scripture, for Paul told Timothy to “endure hardship as a good soldier of Jesus Christ” (2 Timothy 2:3), στρατιώτης (stratiōtēs, stra-tee-OH-tase) — soldier. What he means as survival, God may mean as sanctification. And in the end he said, “I know nothing at all, but to trust.” That confession is the whole of it — the poverty of spirit Jesus called blessed, πτωχός (ptōchos, ptoh-KHOS) — utterly poor, destitute — “theirs is the kingdom of heaven” (Matthew 5:3). Moses spent forty years in the desert before the burning bush; David was hunted through caves before the throne; even the Lord came out of the wilderness “in the power of the Spirit” (Luke 4:14). The stripping is not the story’s end; it is the story’s forge — “you meant evil against me; but God meant it for good” (Genesis 50:20).

## Implications and Challenges

I want to hold all of this gently, because a man in genuine crisis does not need a sermon weaponized against his pain — what I witnessed was faith fighting for its life, and that is a holy thing. Yet the Spirit does press some things worth naming.

**We have equated the favor of God with financial ascent.** “One minute I’m doing ten k a month… next minute I’m divorced, homeless” — and the instinct of us all is to read the first as blessing and the second as abandonment. Scripture rarely permits that math. Samuel named his own comfort as the weapon that was killing him; we have built church cultures around removing discomfort when Scripture treats certain discomforts as the Father’s tool for maturity.

**We have sanitized the wilderness and medicalize every hardship.** Modern Western Christianity loves the testimony of deliverance but flinches at the season that follows it. The joke about the mental institution carried a real edge — that a culture uncomfortable with suffering will try to sedate the sufferer rather than sit with him. Not all anguish is pathology; some is the ache of God carving a deeper vessel. We must recover that the Spirit Himself leads the delivered into testing, and that this is love.

**We pray too vaguely.** There is comfort in praying for “strength” and “faith” because such prayers cannot be measured and therefore cannot be seen to fail. But the Father invites specificity — the tent, the pounds and pence, the exact need — so His answer becomes undeniable glory. Vague prayer can be a hiding place for unbelief.

**We confuse trust with self-reliance dressed in spiritual language, and cleverness with prayer.** Our age worships the “hundred IQ move,” the finesse, and it is possible to say “all glory to God” while building a kingdom by our own hustle. There is a real difference between speaking to the Rock and striking it. Grace covers the striking, but grace also calls us higher, toward provision we never have to hide.

**We confuse theological correctness with love, and prayer with a substitute for aid.** Job’s friends were often right and always unhelpful. When Samuel could offer Thomas “only prayer,” it was because he had nothing else — but how often do those of us with plenty offer the same empty blessing James warned against: “Depart in peace, be warmed and filled,” without giving “the things which are needed for the body” (James 2:16)? A loaf shared in silence may preach louder than a flawless doctrine of providence.

**We live isolated, having divorced the spiritual from the practical.** When Samuel and I marveled at how in Filipino villages the whole community cooks and shares, we were mourning something the Western church has largely lost — the communal table of Acts, where “no one said that any of the things he possessed was his own” (Acts 4:32). A truly homeless brother in a truly communal Body would never sleep in a field alone. And I say all this as a fellow wilderness-dweller — the comfortable among us, myself included, must remember that our own housing is a tent, and the day may come when only the Rock remains. Better to know now, while we are dry, that He alone is our dwelling place.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

Maturity is learning to read our seasons the way Heaven reads them rather than the way our fears read them — to stop measuring God’s nearness by the fullness of our bank accounts and start discerning His hand in the daily rations of the wilderness: the shower, the soup, the shelter that shows up just in time. It is learning to count loaves instead of lacks, to be content in every state as Paul was, and to stop resenting the crushing of the clay so we can trust the Potter’s hands. It is also the humility to catch ourselves mid-strike and turn back to speaking.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

The call is to keep “doing the right thing” even in the hour when compromise would be most excusable — to send back the money that is not ours, to refuse the world’s illegitimate provision to wait on the Father’s legitimate supply, to speak to the Rock rather than strike it, to bless the Thomas who curses us, and to take whatever bread God provides and immediately share it with the next homeless brother. Obedience looks like trusting the One who provides rather than scheming to become our own provider.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

This trains us to wait upon the Lord without knowing where we will sleep, to lean on the staff of relationship when every other prop is gone, and to pray until the doors open rather than force them. It is disciplined receptivity — more alone time, more Word, more presence — and learning to read the breadcrumbs: the old man at the charging station, the stranger who knows a church, the neighbor with the worship music, the cinema seat that becomes a sanctuary. We watch, we yield, we trust the Peg-Driver in the dark, and we record faithfully what the Rock does, so those who come after will know He is faithful.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I come as Your servant and co-heir, standing in the authority of the New Covenant between the heavens and the earth, and I lift my brother Samuel before Your throne in the hour of his emptying. You who pitched Your tent among us in flesh, who had nowhere to lay Your head, You know this wilderness intimately and You do not despise it. I thank You that You have brought him out of Egypt, that the stripping is preparation, that the fear is honest, and that Your manna is already falling for tomorrow’s need. I make agreement now with Your revealed will: send the ravens, provide the tent to the penny, mend the holes in his garments each morning as You did in the wilderness of old, blow open the prison doors in the midnight of prayer, and seize the hesitating hand as You seized Lot’s, pulling him peaceably out of every Sodom into the wide place of dependence on You alone. Forgive us where we have struck the Rock in our cunning when You bid us speak to it, and teach us the cleaner miracle of asking. Turn his wound into a well; make the homeless man a tentmaker who houses the roofless, that the enemy’s intended ruin would become Your instrument of mercy. Deal graciously with the interior unbelief in him and in me, for we have both prayed the frightening prayer, help our unbelief. And I thank You that across oceans, without ever a face-to-face meeting, You have knit two of Your servants together — trusting that whether in this life or in the resurrection, You will bring us to our grand reunion, in a body free of every tear. To You be all glory in the whirlwind and in the wilderness alike. All glory to God, all glory to God, all glory to God. In the name of Jesus Christ, come in the flesh, I pray. Amen.
