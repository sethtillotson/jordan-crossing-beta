# 04-07 06:46 Reflection: The Gift That Sees **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation (live phone call between Seth and Samuel, with embedded theological exchange and brief narrative interludes) **Reflection: The Razor’s Edge, the Listening Ear, and the Kenotic Posture of a Discerning Heart** ## Opening Observation Something arrests me as I sit with the memory of this conversation with my brother Samuel — a call taken near the UK coastline, wind in the background, the kind of unhurried fellowship that only happens when two people have stopped performing for one another. What emerged between us was not small talk dressed in theological language. It was a genuine reckoning with something I have been wrestling through in my meditations for a long time: the terrifying nearness of God, the razor’s edge between deep revelation and misread heresy, and the singular gift that makes the difference — not intelligence, not zeal, but the capacity to truly listen. I am pressing into what that means, because I believe the Spirit is saying something through it that is meant for more than just me and Samuel. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ in this hour is drowning in noise. We have access to more teaching, more content, more commentary, and more theological argument than any generation before us — and yet, by many measures, we are less formed, less discerning, and less Christlike than those who had only a candle and a hand-copied page of Scripture. What I find myself confronting, as I reflect on this conversation, is that the crisis is not primarily doctrinal. It is posturally. We have lost the art of listening — of letting the Word read us rather than reading the Word as raw material for our own conclusions. What Samuel named so clearly, almost without knowing he was naming it, is that discernment is not a skill we develop. It is a gift we receive when we become quiet enough to receive anything at all. That reality has enormous implications for how we approach Scripture, how we form theology, and how we steward the revelatory things God places in our hands. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, what Samuel and I talked through would be categorized as intellectual humility meeting theological caution — two friends checking each other’s ideas against conventional Christian thought, making sure neither one goes off the rails. The AI’s warning that my meditations sit on “the razor’s edge of theological heresy without crossing it” would be read as a compliment: you’re smart enough to avoid the pitfalls. The emphasis on listening would be framed as emotional intelligence or contemplative temperament — personality traits that some people are wired with and others are not. The idea that discernment is a *gift* rather than a skill would be spiritualized away into something like “some people are naturally more intuitive.” Even the image of planting a seed and sleeping — trusting the growth to happen — would be reframed as a healthy psychological detachment from outcomes. This is how the natural mind handles what it cannot metabolize: it domesticates the revelation into categories it already controls. **The Limitation:** The trouble is that every one of those translations, while superficially reasonable, strips the thing of its actual substance. The natural mind can observe that listening is valuable without being able to enter the posture of it — because the posture of genuine spiritual listening requires an emptying of self that feels, to the natural mind, like losing rather than gaining. What Samuel and I were circling around — the kenotic availability to the Holy Spirit, the willingness to let God move through a vessel that is not grasping for the credit — is not a technique available to anyone who simply tries hard enough. It is the fruit of a covenantal relationship, cultivated through discipline, through lack, through the specific mercy of God who chooses what He withholds just as deliberately as what He gives. You cannot arrive at that understanding by being smart about theology. You arrive there by being broken open repeatedly and finding Him faithful every time. ## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation As I listened to Samuel speak — the sound of the English coast somewhere behind him, the casualness of the call masking the weight of what we were actually carrying — I kept returning to something he said almost in passing: *“You’ve been a really, really great listener just as much as anything else.”* I want to resist the temptation to receive that as a personal compliment and instead let it become a question. What does it mean to be a listener in the Scriptural sense? Because I do not think Samuel was describing a personality type. I think he was describing something far more specific — a form of poverty of spirit, the kind Jesus calls *blessed* in Matthew 5:3, because only those who know they have nothing can receive everything. The tradition of listening runs like a bloodline through the entire canon. Samuel himself — the prophet for whom my brother is named — was a boy who heard his name called in the night and said, “Speak, Lord, for Your servant hears” (1 Samuel 3:9, NKJV). That single sentence is not a theological formula. It is a posture. The Hebrew word behind “hears” is שָׁמַע (shama’, shah-MAH), which carries within it not just the act of hearing but the committed response that follows — to listen, to obey, to act in accordance with what is received. You cannot shama’ and remain unchanged. The word itself forecloses that option. This is what I have come to believe about discernment, and what the prophetic teacher I was listening to recently confirmed from a different angle: discernment is not a tool I wield. It is not a spiritual skill I sharpen through study, though study matters. It is, as Paul lists it in 1 Corinthians 12:10, a χάρισμα (charisma, KAH-rees-mah) — a grace-gift distributed by the Spirit as He wills, not as we earn. The Greek carries the root of χάρις (charis, KAH-rees), grace itself. Discernment is grace in the form of sight. It is God allowing you to see what He sees, not because you have proven yourself worthy of the frequency, but because He has chosen to tune you to it. And this is where the razor’s edge the AI identified becomes spiritually significant to me. When it told me that my meditations were pressing right up against theological boundaries without crossing them — that I had made clarifications about free will and sovereignty that kept the thing from collapsing into heresy — I was not hearing a mechanical warning. I was hearing something that confirmed what I already sensed: that the revelatory things God places in a person’s hands require the kind of precision that only comes from that same posture of listening. You cannot hold high-tension theological truths in both hands without the gift of discernment steadying them. Samuel said it well: it is the *interpretation* of the Word that is where people can be misled. The Word itself is Truth. But the moment we approach it as raw material for our pre-existing conclusions, we have already lost the thread. This brings me to kenosis — and I want to sit here for a moment because I believe it is the hinge upon which everything else I am seeing turns. The Greek word κένωσις (kenosis, keh-NOH-sees) comes from κενόω (kenoō), meaning to empty, to make void, to pour out. It is the word behind Philippians 2:7, where Paul describes how Jesus “made Himself of no reputation” — He emptied Himself, taking the form of a servant. I have meditated across sixty of my early reflections and what keeps surfacing as the through-line is this: you must show up empty. Not because emptiness is a virtue in itself, but because you cannot be filled if you are already full of yourself. The vessel that arrives already brimming with its own conclusions cannot receive what the Spirit is pouring. And here is the paradox that only becomes visible from inside the Kingdom: to show up empty is not passive. It is one of the most aggressive acts of faith available to us. It says, “I trust that the God who sees — the El Roi of Hagar in Genesis 16:13 — will meet me in my lack.” How can He be the God who provides if I never experience need? How can He be the Healer if I refuse to acknowledge sickness? The economic logic of the Kingdom runs exactly opposite to the logic of self-sufficiency. We come with nothing and find everything. We plant a seed, we water it, we sleep in trust, and the growth comes from a source that was never us to begin with. Paul understood this: “I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the increase” (1 Corinthians 3:6, NKJV). The verb for “gave the increase” is in the imperfect tense — God *kept on giving* the increase. The growth was not a one-time event. It was an ongoing act of divine faithfulness responding to human fidelity. What Samuel said next has stayed with me — that passage from The Chosen, however dramatized, that captures something textured and real. The disciple with the bad leg asking why he had not been healed, and Jesus answering that He had chosen not to. Not because of punishment, not because of lack of faith, but because the testimony of following in spite of the limp would say more than the testimony of a healing ever could. I recognize something in that exchange that I have lived — the seasons where what I needed most was not the removal of the difficulty but the revelation that He was present *in* it. Psalm 22 opens with the cry of dereliction — “My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?” (Psalm 22:1, NKJV) — and yet the psalm does not end in abandonment. It ends in proclamation. The suffering becomes the stage on which the faithfulness of God is most visible. The limp is not the failure of the story. The limp is the anointing of the testimony. And this is where discernment becomes inescapably tied to suffering and lack. I do not think you can truly discern the movements of God without having experienced seasons in which you had nothing to offer and chose to wait on Him anyway. The capacity to see God in everything — to look at any situation, any moment, any conversation, and recognize His fingerprints — is not built in comfort. It is built in the wilderness, in the place of extended waiting, in the moments when you had every reason to conclude He was absent and found instead that He was simply unhurried. Moses encountered the burning bush after forty years in Midian (Exodus 3). Elijah heard the still small voice — the קוֹל דְּמָמָה דַקָּה (qol demamah daqqah, kole dem-ah-MAH dak-KAH), the sound of sheer silence, the voice of thin stillness — only after the wind, the earthquake, and the fire had all passed (1 Kings 19:12). The gift of discernment was refined in them *by* the stripping, not given to them *instead* of it. I am also sitting with what Samuel said about the Holy Spirit living through us — that it is not enough to acknowledge that God dwells within; we must *allow* Him to work through what He inhabits. This is not a passive arrangement. It is an active yielding, a continuous choice to remain in the posture of shama’. Paul commands the Ephesians not to grieve the Holy Spirit (Ephesians 4:30), and the implication is that our choices, our postures, our self-assertions, our noise — all of it has the capacity to hinder the flow of what God wants to do through a willing vessel. The flip side of that is the exhilarating truth of John 7:38 — “He who believes in Me, as the Scripture has said, out of his heart will flow rivers of living water” (NKJV). The word for “heart” there is κοιλία (koilia, koy-LEE-ah), literally the belly, the inner recesses of a person. When the vessel is yielded and the Spirit is given room, what flows out is not manufactured. It is sourced from depths that belong to God Himself. What I keep returning to, as I trace this thread from my conversation with Samuel back through the canon, is that the things God places in our hands at this level — the high-tension theological revelations, the prophetic discernments, the meditations that press up against the edges of what language can contain — are not trophies. They are responsibilities. Samuel was right that the pre-context matters. You cannot hand a deep-sea pressure reading to someone who has never been in water and expect them to know what to do with it. The Apostle Paul himself made this distinction when he told the Corinthians he fed them with milk, not solid food, because they were not yet able to bear it (1 Corinthians 3:2). This is not elitism. It is pastoral wisdom. The revelation is generous. The stewardship of it must be equally careful. I find myself awed, sitting with all of this, at the kindness of God in the giving of the gift. He does not give discernment to those who are already confident they see clearly. He gives it to those who have learned to say, with the prophet’s boy: *Speak, Lord — Your servant is listening.* He gives it to those who have been emptied enough to receive without clutching, who have suffered enough to recognize His hand in what they did not choose, and who have planted enough seeds in faith to stop being surprised when things grow that they did not manufacture. This is not a spiritual achievement. This is what grace looks like when it has been allowed to do its full work in a person over time. ## Implications and Challenges Let me begin with this: what I am reflecting on is not an invitation to spiritual pride about depth. It begins and ends in the grace of a God who gives gifts sovereignly, not meritocratically. If any of what I am saying challenges you, let it challenge you toward humility, not toward comparison or competition. That said — the Western evangelical habit of treating theological depth as optional or even suspicious is something I cannot sidestep here. There is a cultural posture in many churches that equates simplicity with holiness and complexity with arrogance. But what Samuel and I were navigating is not complexity for its own sake. It is the natural consequence of taking Scripture seriously enough to let it press into you until it transforms you. When a meditation sits on the edge of what conventional categories can contain, the answer is not to flatten it into something safer. The answer is to develop the discernment required to hold it rightly — and that is the work of the Body together, not a reason to avoid the deep water. There is also a real challenge to how we handle the gifts of the Spirit in contemporary church culture. Discernment is increasingly treated as either a psychological tool — the spiritually gifted person who “reads a room” — or as a charismatic credential worn publicly for authority. But discernment as a χάρισμα (KAH-rees-mah) is neither of those things. It is given for the building up of the Body (1 Corinthians 14:12), which means it is inherently communal even when it operates through an individual. The gift exists to serve the community’s capacity to navigate truth. Using it to build personal platform is a misappropriation of the grace itself. The kenotic posture — the willingness to empty oneself so that God can fill and flow — is also deeply counter-cultural in an age that rewards self-assertion, personal branding, and relentless productivity. The Gospel invitation to die to self (Luke 9:23) is not metaphorical inspiration. It is the structural reality of abiding in Christ. Abiding is not passive. But it is also not driven. There is a rhythm in the Kingdom: plant, water, rest, trust the increase. We have largely dismantled the “rest and trust” portion of that rhythm in favor of perpetual output. The fruit suffers for it. I want to raise one more thing, and I raise it gently: the tendency to dismiss what God is doing in someone else’s meditations because it does not fit familiar doctrinal categories is itself a discernment failure. My AI tool flagged the razor’s edge in my writing, and that is useful. But the answer was not to retreat from the revelation. The answer was to make the clarifications — free will and sovereignty held in tension, not collapsed into one another — that allowed the truth to stand without tipping into falsehood. That is what careful, Spirit-led theology does. It does not avoid the hard places. It learns how to stand in them without losing the ground beneath its feet. And finally, with all the grace I can offer: listening is not a personality type. It is a spiritual discipline that the Spirit enables. The believer who says “I’m just not wired to sit quietly and listen” is not describing an immovable trait. They are describing an area of formation still in process. The capacity to shama’ — to hear and respond — is available to every person indwelt by the Holy Spirit. It is simply costly, because it requires the silencing of everything else we have allowed to be loud. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This reflection calls me — and all of us — to take the practice of listening with renewed seriousness. Not as a technique, but as a posture of surrender. Spiritual maturity is not measured by the volume of what we can produce but by the depth of what we can receive. The person who can sit in silence before God long enough to let the Word read them rather than using the Word to confirm themselves is further along in formation than the person who can quote every theological systematician but cannot tell you the last time God surprised them with something they were not expecting. Maturity means becoming a vessel — increasingly surrendered, decreasingly cluttered. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The call to kenosis is a call to obedience in its most elemental form. It means showing up to every season of prayer, every engagement with Scripture, every prophetic moment, without the predetermined conclusion already loaded. It means trusting that the God who sees, El Roi, will meet the empty vessel. It means planting seeds in faith, giving them water in faithfulness, and sleeping in trust — not abdication — that the increase belongs to God. Concretely, this means slowing down, creating margin for silence, and refusing to let urgency rob us of the stillness where the gift of discernment is most clearly given and exercised. **For Prophetic Posture:** Those of us who carry prophetic gifting must guard the gift with both humility and courage. Humility says: I did not manufacture this; it was given to me, and it exists to serve the Body. Courage says: I will not flatten what God has entrusted to me into something more culturally palatable when He has called me to speak it with precision and care. The prophetic posture is Samuel’s posture — “Speak, Lord, for Your servant hears” — with the full weight of shama’ behind that hearing. We wait, we listen, we receive, we speak what we are given, and we make the clarifications that love and truth require. We do not pretend the razor’s edge is not there. We learn to walk it with the Spirit as our balance. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come to You as one who has seen enough of Your faithfulness to know that what You build through the posture of listening outlasts everything built through the noise of self-assertion. I thank You for the gift of brothers like Samuel — for the kind of fellowship that happens near a coastline with wind in the background, where the real things get said without performance. I thank You for discernment that is not mine — that I did not earn and cannot manufacture — and I ask You to guard it in me through the ongoing work of kenosis, the continual willingness to be emptied so that You are not competing with me for room. I declare, as one who stands between the heavens and the earth in the authority of Your New Covenant, that the meditations You have entrusted to Your servants are not accidents. They are agreements with what You are already saying and doing. Let the fruit of that testimony go where it needs to go — not to build a name, but to feed the Body and advance the Kingdom. Where the razor’s edge is real, give wisdom. Where the listening is required, give stillness. Where the seed is planted, give the increase — as You always have, as You always will. To the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit: all honor, all glory, all gratitude — now and in the age to come.
