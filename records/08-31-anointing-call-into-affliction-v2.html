# 08-31 Teaching Reflection: The Anointing as a Call Into Affliction **Title:** 08-31 at 10:43 — The Anointing Is a Call Into Affliction **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** E — Recorded Sermon, Video, or Online Reel + Reflection **Classification:** Teaching Reflection --- ## ⚡ Opening Observation I keep returning to one sentence that landed like a stone dropped into still water: *"The anointing is a call into affliction, and if you don't see it that way, you will miss it."* I heard a former Muslim, now a preacher, describe spending his entire student allowance on a worship meeting and then begging people to come enjoy the money he had already spent — and instead of hearing a complaint, I heard a doctrine. The weight of this teaching is that it dismantles the prosperity-shaped Christianity so many of us have inhaled without ever naming, and it does so not with argument but with testimony after testimony. **Why This Matters:** This matters because an entire generation has been catechized to believe that God's favor arrives as comfort, elevation, and material increase — that the anointing is a ladder rather than a cross. If that assumption is false, then countless believers are interpreting their afflictions as signs of God's absence when they may be the very signature of His hand. The teaching I heard reframes suffering not as punishment or failure but as commissioning, and this reframing has enormous consequences for how the saints endure, how they discern seasons, and how they refuse to abandon obedience when it costs them. If we cannot tell the difference between the anointing and ambition, we will chase the wrong things and despise the right ones. The Body of Christ needs sober believers who can carry burdens without concluding that God has forsaken them. It matters for discipleship because Jesus Himself said the servant is not above his Master. And it matters for love, because those who understand affliction as anointing become like Bibles others can read. --- ## 🌍 The Worldly Mind's Interpretation To the natural mind, this preacher's stories are simply a chronicle of poor planning and religious naivety dressed up as spiritual insight. A young convert wastes his money, gets fleeced by musicians, begs an empty room to fill — and rather than admit inexperience, he sanctifies the disappointment by calling it "anointing." The barren woman prayed thirty-five years with no child; the reasonable conclusion is that prayer does not reliably work, and the eighty-year-old's story of a five-year-old son is a comforting anecdote that survivorship bias makes memorable. The three ministries that closed in the shared space were led by men over sixty who lacked energy and market reach; the honest explanation is demographic, not divine. Even the lifting headache during preaching is easily attributed to adrenaline. The worldly mind hears all of this and files it under religious rationalization — the human tendency to find meaning in randomness so the pain feels purposeful. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind is working with a truncated ledger. It can only measure what is gained, retained, and displayed; it has no column for what is lost on purpose, no category for a fruitfulness that ripens through delay, no instrument that detects glory hidden inside apparent failure. It sees the barren years and cannot see the woman being slowly written into a living testimony. It sees three closed doors and cannot perceive a Father saying *the water is no longer stirred for you*. The flesh reasons from outcomes upward, but the Spirit reveals purposes descending downward, and these two accountings will never reconcile because they are not measuring the same thing. What looks like waste to the eye that worships increase may be worship itself to the God who received a broken alabaster jar as the fragrance of devotion. --- ## ✝️ What the Spirit Reveals Through It I cannot get past the image of this man, freshly converted out of Islam, standing with empty pockets and a printed poster, going person to person and begging them to come and *help him enjoy his money*. There is something almost absurd in it, and he knows it — you can hear the disbelief still in his voice years later. He gave everything he had, and then he had to plead for the privilege of giving more. He expected that serving God would gather people to him; instead it scattered his allowance and left him pleading at doors. That is where I want to stay for a moment, because most of us have quietly assumed the opposite equation: that if we are truly walking with God, the doors will open and the crowds will come and the cost will somehow be covered. He was learning, in real time, that the arithmetic of the Kingdom runs the other direction. And what arrests me is that he did not treat this as a lesson in better logistics. He treated it as an initiation. *"That's the anointing."* He kept saying it, like a refrain, over story after story, until I realized he was not describing bad luck — he was describing a pattern woven so consistently through Scripture that it must be intentional. The Spirit seems to be pressing something through this repetition: that the very thing we run from when it visits us may be the confirmation that God has laid His hand upon us. The anointing is not the reward at the end of the road. It is the oil poured on the head of the one who is about to be led somewhere hard. The first echo that rises in me is Mary. He asked, *"Have you imagined that God told a virgin to conceive a child?"* — and suddenly the annunciation, which we sentimentalize on Christmas cards, becomes what it actually was: a teenage girl being handed a pregnancy no one in Nazareth would believe, a reputation surrendered, a sword promised to pierce her own soul. Simeon told her plainly, *"Yes, a sword will pierce through your own soul also"* (Luke 2:35). The favor of God — *"Blessed are you among women"* (Luke 1:42) — arrived clothed as scandal and would culminate in her watching her own Son, who was God, die on a Roman cross. The most favored woman who ever lived was favored into the deepest maternal grief imaginable. That is the anointing. The word for anointing itself carries this. When the Scripture speaks of the Anointed One, it gives us מָשִׁיחַ (mashiach, mah-SHEE-akh) — anointed, Messiah — and the Greek renders it χριστός (christos, khris-TOSS) — anointed one, Christ. The oil of anointing set kings, priests, and prophets apart, but it set them apart *for* something, and in the case of the true Anointed One, it set Him apart for Gethsemane and Golgotha. To be christened, to be an anointed one, a "little christ," is to be marked with the same oil that led the Head to the cross. We have taken a word soaked in suffering and turned it into a word for platform and prestige. Trace it across the canon and the pattern refuses to break. Moses spends forty years in a wilderness leading a people who resented him, and when he strikes the rock in his exhaustion, the teacher noted something piercing — *nobody prayed for him*. The intercessor who stood in the breach for the golden-calf generation, pleading *"blot me out of Your book"* (Exodus 32:32), received no intercessor of his own in his moment of failure. Joseph, chosen and dreaming, carries the burden of preserving the very brothers who sold him, saying at last, *"you meant evil against me; but God meant it for good"* (Genesis 50:20). And Job — the man God Himself *boasted* about in the heavenly court while men mocked him in the dust — became, as this preacher put it, "the doctrine of suffering." God scanned the earth and asked *"Have you considered My servant Job?"* (Job 1:8), and that consideration was the beginning of everything being stripped away. Then he named the thing that reorders the whole map for me: *"If God is sending you a comforter, it might mean that you will need comforting."* I have read John 14 many times and never let that logic land. Jesus promised the παράκλητος (paraklētos, par-AK-lay-tos) — the Comforter, the one called alongside to help — precisely because His disciples were being sent into a world that would hate them, scatter them, and kill all but one of them. The gift of the Comforter presupposes affliction. We celebrate the sending of the Spirit as empowerment for triumph, and it is that; but it is first the provision of a God who knows His children are about to walk through fire and will not send them in alone. This is where the theological tension cracks wide open. He said it with the force of a man who found it strange precisely because he did *not* grow up in church: *"Faith is not just what we can get through God; it is what we can also lose."* We have built a devotional culture almost entirely around receiving, and we quote *Jehovah Jireh* — יְהוָה יִרְאֶה (Yahweh yireh, yah-WEH yir-EH), "the LORD will provide" — as a slogan for supply. But the man who received that revelation, Abraham, did not climb Moriah to *collect*. He climbed it to *slaughter his son* (Genesis 22:14). The name "the LORD will provide" was born on an altar of surrender, not at a table of increase. We have severed the provision from the sacrifice and kept only the half we like. What natural reasoning would miss entirely is the mystery he uncovered about the three shuttered ministries. Any strategist would say: older men, less energy, smaller reach — of course they failed. But interceding, he saw what the eye could not: all three were over sixty, and the Spirit pressed a harder word than mere encouragement. The old man in Houston who gripped his hand — *"if I can't become it, at least let me touch it"* — was living the ache of one who arrived at the stirred water too late. There is a season when the pool is troubled and the healing is available, and the paralytic's lament, *"while I am coming, another steps down before me"* (John 5:7), becomes the sorrow of a delayed obedience. This is a fearful mystery, and I hold it with trembling, because it warns that *now* is not a small word. Yet I dare not flatten it into fatalism, for the God who found Moses at eighty and Abraham beyond hope is not bound by any timetable but His own — and the teaching's sharpest edge is not against the aged but against the *sleeping*, against those who keep spectating while the hour to run passes over them. And this is where it turns on me personally. I hear the confession beneath his own testimony — the overworked traveling preacher whose headache lifted the instant he began to teach and returned the instant he set down the microphone, as if God said *"Thanks. Now use your faith."* I recognize the temptation to want the anointing to also be my comfort, to expect that the oil which enables me to serve should also insulate me from the affliction of serving. The Spirit is exposing in me a quiet bargain I never consciously made: that I would give God my labor if He would spare me the loss. But Paul, who baffled this preacher and baffles me, wrote of *"having nothing, and yet possessing all things"* (2 Corinthians 6:10), and *"as poor, yet making many rich."* I want to be made rich; I am slower to become poor so that others might be. For the beloved brethren, this reframes the whole landscape of endurance. The barren sister who prayed thirty-five years and declared God unfaithful was not wicked — she was exhausted, and I feel the honesty of her rage. But the eighty-year-old handed her a mirror instead of a rebuke: *"When you have prayed 75 years and God has not answered, judge Him unfaithful."* What God was doing across those decades was writing a woman into a living Bible that people would read. This is what the anointing does to the saints — it makes our lives legible testimonies of a faithfulness that outlasts our timelines. We are being written, and the writing takes longer than we would choose. So to the Body I would say what he said to the three fainting pastors and to himself in his own five barren months with only his wife in the room: *don't stop.* The church that grew after five months of emptiness, the space that was not cursed but contested, the intercession that opened his eyes — all of it testifies that the burden we are carrying may be the very sign that God has chosen us, not forgotten us. We are not spectators of a triumph happening elsewhere. We are runners, looking to *"Jesus, the author and finisher of our faith, who for the joy that was set before Him endured the cross"* (Hebrews 12:2) — and the joy was on the far side of the cross, never instead of it. --- ## 🤔 Implications and Challenges **The gospel of comfort must be repented of, gently but thoroughly.** Much of modern Western and prosperity-influenced Christianity has taught believers to read every hardship as an attack to be rebuked and every blessing as material increase to be claimed. This teaching exposes that grid as unbiblical, because it cannot account for Mary, Moses, Joseph, Job, or the eleven apostles who died. The challenge is not to swing into a morbid worship of suffering, but to recover the sober truth that the oil of anointing has always led God's chosen ones through fire. If we only have a theology for gain, we will be spiritually destroyed by the losses that faith itself requires. Grace makes room for our disappointment; it simply refuses to let disappointment have the final word. **Delay is not the same as denial, and this cuts both ways.** The barren woman's thirty-five years and the eighty-year-old's seventy-five confront our culture of instant answers, where a prayer unanswered for a season is treated as proof that God is silent or absent. Yet the sober word about the three ministries over sixty also warns that there are stirred waters we can miss by sleeping. The saint must hold both: never judge God unfaithful in the waiting, and never presume the hour will always remain open. This is the narrow discernment of seasons that the flesh cannot manage on its own. Only the Spirit can teach us when to wait in faith and when to run without delay. **"Gimmick Christianity" cannot profit the soul, however well it fills a room.** The posters, the paid musicians, the publicity machinery — the young convert learned that the mechanics of religious enterprise are not the same as the anointing of God. Our institutions have become adept at producing crowds and unpracticed at producing crosses. The correction here is pointed: a Christianity engineered for attendance and increase, severed from sacrifice, "cannot profit them." This is not a call to despise excellence or planning, but a warning that no amount of strategy can substitute for the altar. Where we have trusted the machine, we are invited back to the sacrifice. **The healing evangelist whose children are dying rebukes our transactional faith.** This is one of the sharpest lines, and it must land softly on the wounded and firmly on the presumptuous. Anointing to bless others does not guarantee immunity for oneself, and the servant who ministers power may still walk his own valley of the shadow. This dismantles the idea that God's gifts function as a personal insurance policy. It calls the saints to serve without demanding exemption from the very sufferings we help others through. And it invites deep compassion for anointed servants who are quietly grieving even as they give. **The urgency to run should convict the comfortable spectator.** *"Are you not tired of spectating? There's no profiting in spectating."* Many believers have settled into an audience posture — consuming teaching, admiring the runners, delaying their own obedience until conditions feel ideal. This teaching insists that faith is enacted in the running, not the watching, and that "you don't have mileage" because you are "still sleeping." The grace in this is that it is a summons, not a sentence: the invitation to rise is still open while it is called today. But the invitation carries weight, and to keep spectating is to let the water go unstirred. **To be made a "living Bible" is a costly honor we should stop resisting.** The Spirit's aim in the long barrenness was that "people will read your life." We tend to want our testimony to be one of quick deliverance, but God often writes His clearest words through our longest waits. This reframes our afflictions as pages being authored by a faithful hand. The challenge is to stop demanding that our lives read like an easy chapter and to trust the Author who endured the cross for the joy beyond it. Grace assures us that no page is wasted and the story is not finished. --- ## 🚀 What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity here means acquiring a new set of eyes — eyes that can look at loss, delay, burden, and apparent failure without immediately concluding that God has withdrawn. It means learning to say "that's the anointing" over the very circumstances the flesh would call abandonment, not as denial of pain but as trust in purpose. The mature believer stops measuring the favor of God by comfort and increase and starts recognizing the cruciform shape of every genuine calling. This changes how we repent, too: instead of repenting for having attracted trouble, we repent for having expected exemption. We grow up into the mind of the One who endured the cross, and we stop being scandalized when His path leads through Gethsemane before it reaches resurrection. **For Kingdom Obedience:** This calls forth an obedience that keeps giving even when the giving is not reimbursed, that keeps building even through five silent months, that intercedes for others' perseverance rather than nursing our own discouragement. It calls us to run rather than spectate, to answer the summons while the water is still stirred, and to refuse the seduction of a "gimmick Christianity" that trades altars for algorithms. Concretely, it means finishing the assignment even when the room is empty, laying our resources on the altar without demanding a return, and using our faith when the headache comes back. The obedience of the anointed is not glamorous; it is faithful. **For Prophetic Posture:** The saints are trained here to watch, wait, and discern seasons with sobriety — to know that some waters are stirred now and must not be missed, while some answers ripen over decades and must not be abandoned. We learn to abide as those being written into living testimonies, willing to become legible to others even at great personal cost. We hold intercession as a sacred labor, standing in the breach for one another's endurance as this preacher did for three fainting ministries. And we testify from the place of surrender rather than acquisition, remembering that the one who first cried *the LORD will provide* was standing over a bound son on an altar. --- ## 🔥 A Closing Prayer Father, I come to You as Your servant and co-heir, standing between the heavens and the earth to make agreement with Your revealed will, and I thank You that You have never lied to Your children about the cost of following You. Forgive me for the quiet bargain I made without noticing — the hope that Your oil would spare me the fire it was poured out to lead me through. Thank You that the anointing is Your hand upon me and not Your abandonment of me, that when You sent the Comforter it was because You knew I would need comforting, and that You have wept ahead of me on every road You ask me to walk. Teach me to read my afflictions as pages You are authoring, to endure the long barrenness without judging You unfaithful, and to run while the water is stirred rather than sleep through my season. I lay my resources, my reputation, and my timeline on Your altar as Abraham laid Isaac, trusting that on this mountain You will provide. Make my life a living Bible that the weary may read and take courage, and let me carry the burden of others' preservation as Joseph did, without bitterness and without demanding rescue. I intercede now for every fainting servant over sixty and under twenty alike, for the barren who have prayed for decades, for the healing evangelists grieving in secret — strengthen their hands and let them not stop. Fill me afresh with the παράκλητος, that I may finish the assignment even in an empty room, looking unto Jesus, the Author and Finisher of my faith. To the Anointed One who endured the cross for the joy set before Him be all glory, honor, and dominion, world without end. Amen. --- claude-opus-4.8 --- ## 📝 Scripture References & Original Language Notes | Passage (NKJV) | Original Language | Theological Insight | |----------------|------------------|---------------------| | Luke 2:35 | מָשִׁיחַ (mashiach, mah-SHEE-akh) — anointed one | The anointing that made Mary "favored" also promised a sword through her soul; favor and grief were one gift. | | John 1:41; Acts 10:38 | χριστός (christos, khris-TOSS) — anointed one, Christ | The Anointed One was anointed unto the cross; to bear His name is to bear His oil toward Golgotha. | | John 14:16, 26 | παράκλητος (paraklētos, par-AK-lay-tos) — one called alongside, Comforter | The sending of the Comforter presupposes affliction; the gift assumes the wound. | | Genesis 22:14 | יְהוָה יִרְאֶה (Yahweh yireh, yah-WEH yir-EH) — the LORD will provide | The name of provision was revealed on an altar of sacrifice, not a place of collection. | | Job 1:8 | — | God boasted of His servant in heaven while men mocked him on earth; the anointing made Job "the doctrine of suffering." | | Exodus 32:32 | — | Moses interceded for the rebellious yet had no intercessor in his own failure; the burden of the anointed is often carried alone. | | Genesis 50:20 | — | Joseph, chosen and afflicted, carried the burden of preserving the very brothers who betrayed him. | | 2 Corinthians 6:10 | — | "As poor, yet making many rich; as having nothing, and yet possessing all things" — the paradox of Kingdom wealth through loss. | | Hebrews 12:2 | — | Jesus endured the cross for the joy set beyond it; the runner looks to Him and does not stop. | | John 5:7 | — | The lament of the one who arrives at the stirred water too late — a warning about missed seasons. | ---
