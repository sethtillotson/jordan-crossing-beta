# 07-13 Teaching Reflection: Sound Mind, Love as a Verb, and Cultivating God’s Presence **Title:** 07-13 at 17:29 — My Broke Is Rich: Sound Mind, Love, and the Atmosphere of His Presence **Recorded:** 2026-07-13 17:29:02 **Source Discernment:** E — Recorded Sermon, Video, or Online Reel + Reflection **Classification:** Teaching Reflection ## Opening Observation As I sat with this teaching from Prophet Lovy Elias, three anchoring truths began to press themselves into me with increasing weight: the condition of the mind, the nature of love as a verb, and the difference between being *in* God’s presence and merely acknowledging that God’s presence exists everywhere. These are not three unrelated points — they are a single cascading revelation about what it means to walk in Kingdom energy rather than being perpetually drained, depleted, and unknowingly feeding the very darkness you believe you are resisting. What struck me most deeply was how plainly this teaching exposes the mechanics of spiritual depletion — and then, with equal plainness, charts the path toward accumulation. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ in this hour is filled with people who declare the right things but remain impoverished in their inner world — not because the Word is weak, but because the mind in which the Word is deposited has become a plantation rather than a sanctuary. If the saints do not understand that negative thought patterns, critical postures, and fear-laden spiritual atmospheres are not simply bad habits but are active mechanisms through which demonic forces are fed and sustained, then no amount of declaration, no worship service, and no prophetic word will produce lasting transformation. This teaching touches the nerve of why so many believers cycle through spiritual highs and return, again and again, to depletion — and it does so not with condemnation, but with a clarifying light that is itself an act of love. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, hearing a message about positive thinking, the danger of negativity, and the energy dynamics of the inner life, will nod along and file this under the category of motivational psychology. Cognitive behavioral frameworks would affirm that thought patterns shape emotional states, that rumination feeds anxiety, and that criticism as a habitual lens produces isolation and diminished wellbeing. A secular therapist might say that this prophet is essentially teaching neuroplasticity — that the brain rewires itself around the patterns it rehearses most frequently. The world would praise this as emotionally intelligent leadership, perhaps note the charismatic delivery, and commend it as good advice for mental health. It would translate “sound mind” into “mental wellness,” translate “love as a verb” into “emotional labor,” and translate “remaining in God’s presence” into “mindfulness practice.” The framework would be affirming but entirely horizontal — a self-help repackaging of ancient scriptural wisdom stripped of its Author. **The Limitation:** The problem with that reading is not that it is entirely wrong — it is that it stops precisely where the revelation begins. The natural mind can observe the pattern but cannot perceive the Person. It can identify that negativity depletes and love empowers, but it cannot explain *why* — that behind the mechanics of spiritual energy lies a living being who is actively seeking what he may devour, and a living God whose presence is an atmosphere that renews the very strength the enemy has attempted to exhaust. The natural mind cannot receive this because the knowledge of demonic feeding patterns, of spiritual plantation and spiritual accumulation, of the difference between prayer as a religious act and thanksgiving as an atmospheric engagement with the omnipresent God — these things are spiritually discerned. They are foolishness to the one who has not been made alive to the Spirit’s dimension of reality, and no amount of psychological sophistication closes that gap. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with the opening declaration of this teaching — that you can confess one thing while thinking another, and the confession means nothing — I am immediately arrested by the Proverb that has always lived in the background of every prosperity declaration I have ever witnessed: “For as he thinks in his heart, so is he” (Proverbs 23:7). The word for “thinks” there is שָׁעַר (sha’ar, shah-AR) — a word that carries the nuance of calculating, gatekeeping, reckoning. It is not idle thinking. It is the inner steward of the self, the one who decides what is true about you. And if that inner steward is still operating from a ledger of lack, of weakness, of failure — then no declaration shouted in a service overrides it. This is not a word against declaration. This is a word that calls declaration back to its proper foundation: the renovated mind. What the prophet is identifying here connects directly to what Paul called the λογισμοί (logismoi, loh-gis-MOY) — reasonings, imaginations, strongholds — in 2 Corinthians 10:4–5. Paul says the weapons of our warfare are not carnal but mighty in God for pulling down strongholds, casting down every λογισμοί (loh-gis-MOY) and every high thing that exalts itself against the knowledge of God. A stronghold is not a demon outside you throwing darts. A stronghold is an internal architecture of thought that has been constructed over time and now functions as a demonic dwelling — a place of residence for darkness, built from the bricks of negative agreement, fear, shame, self-contempt, and chronic critique. The enemy does not need to attack what he already inhabits. This is where the teaching’s insight about the man possessed by Legion becomes theologically luminous. Mark 5 describes a man who lived among tombs, who cut himself, who could not be restrained by chains — a man who was perpetually in motion toward self-destruction. What I see now, sitting with this, is that Legion did not simply possess him. Legion constructed an atmosphere around him — tombs, blood, isolation, night, regret — because those conditions *fed* the demonic presence. The darkness needed the negativity to remain powerful. And this is not an isolated case; it is a pattern. Demons are not self-sustaining in their torment of humans. They require atmosphere. They require what this teaching rightly calls *plantation* — a cultivated environment where what they need grows. This is why the prophet’s statement that some believers have become plantations of demons is not hyperbole. It is diagnosis. A plantation is not a place where something is merely present — it is a place where something is *cultivated*, tended, multiplied, and harvested. When a believer’s inner world is consistently sown with criticism, suspicion, spiritual paranoia, fear of attack, and the rehearsal of every demonic possibility — they are not just struggling spiritually. They are actively farming the very thing they claim to oppose. And here the canon opens wide: “Neither give place to the devil” (Ephesians 4:27). The word “place” is τόπος (topos, TOH-pos) — a location, a territory, a region. Paul is saying do not give the enemy a *topos*, a zone of occupation within you. But what the teaching exposes is that some have not just given him a room — they have given him the whole estate. The antidote, however, is not merely *stopping* negativity. It is *filling*. A sound mind, σωφρονισμός (sōphronismos, so-fron-is-MOS) — the exact word in 2 Timothy 1:7, which is best understood not simply as “self-control” but as a *whole, disciplined mind* — is not an empty mind swept clean, which would simply invite reoccupation (Matthew 12:43–45). It is a mind filled with hope, filled with faith, filled with love, filled with the acknowledgement of who God is and what He has already done. This is the architecture of resistance. When the prophet says that Satan had to leave Jesus alone because Jesus gave him nothing to feed on — I am watching the Temptation narrative in Matthew 4 with new eyes. Every time the adversary spoke, Jesus answered with *It is written*. Not because He was performing a ritual of Scripture quotation, but because His inner world — His σωφρονισμός (so-fron-is-MOS) — was so saturated with the Father’s word and the Father’s reality that the enemy found no tόπος (TOH-pos), no territory, no atmospheric landing strip in Him. Then the teaching turns to love, and here it deepens in a way I was not expecting. Love is not described as a feeling to be cultivated but as a verb — as capacity built through resistance, through continuing to do good when doing good is costly. The gym metaphor is striking and scripturally honest: the reps that count are the ones you do when you have nothing left. This is agape. ἀγάπη (agapē, ah-GAH-pay) — not the love of affection, not the love of preference, but the covenantal, willful, self-expending love that operates regardless of return. And what I see in this teaching is that ἀγάπη (ah-GAH-pay) is not just a virtue to be admired. It is a *weapon*. When you choose to pray for someone who wronged you, when you choose to seek their restoration rather than celebrate their downfall, you are doing something that starves the demonic and simultaneously expands your own capacity. The depletion is not destruction — it is dilation. You are being stretched to hold more. The cross of Jesus is the ultimate illustration. “Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do” (Luke 23:34) — spoken from the place of maximum depletion, from a body nailed to wood, from a position that every natural instinct would have screamed to escape. And yet in that moment of ultimate expenditure, something was *accumulating*. The Resurrection was not separate from the crucifixion — it was the harvest of it. The depletion was for expansion. This is the pattern: not just of Jesus, but of every saint who refuses to let the ἀγάπη (ah-GAH-pay) go cold. The third movement of this teaching — remaining in God’s presence — is the one I find myself returning to most. The prophet makes a distinction that I believe many in the Western church have entirely collapsed: the distinction between the *omnipresence* of God and an *atmosphere of His presence*. God is everywhere. Even in hell, as Psalm 139:8 confesses. But not everyone is in His presence in the relational, atmospheric, renewing sense. And worship, thanksgiving, and adoration are not petitions — they are not prayers in the conventional sense. They are acknowledgements. They are the act of a human soul engaging with a reality that is already there, already true, already established — and in that engagement, creating the very atmosphere in which supernatural renewal becomes possible. “Those who wait on the LORD shall renew their strength” (Isaiah 40:31) — and the prophet’s reading of this is precise: *their own strength* is renewed. This is not God injecting something foreign into you from outside. This is God activating, through the atmosphere of acknowledged presence, what He has already deposited within you. The Hebrew word for renew here is חָלַף (chalaph, khah-LAHF) — to pass on, to change, to sprout fresh, as a plant sends up new growth. The implication is organic. You are not a battery being recharged by an external source. You are a living thing being watered at the root. And the water is the atmosphere of acknowledgement — of “Lord, You are still good” in the middle of pain, in the middle of lack, in the middle of betrayal. This is where the instruction to “count it all joy” in James 1:2 finds its full-bodied meaning. James does not say *feel* joy. He says *count* it — ἡγέομαι (hēgeomai, hay-GEH-oh-my) — reckon it, consider it, classify it as joy. This is a deliberate act of the σωφρονισμός (so-fron-is-MOS) — the disciplined, whole mind choosing to categorize trial as opportunity rather than as assault. And in doing so, the atmosphere of His presence is sustained rather than broken. The circumstances do not move you from where He has planted you. And because you remain planted, you renew. You accumulate. You become, over time, an unbreakable person — not because you have never been broken, but because every breaking has been counted as currency, and that currency has compounded. I want to sit with one final thread: the prophet’s word about prophetic accuracy and celebration. He describes foretelling a death in government and the lowering of a flag at the White House — and then refusing to celebrate “I told you so.” Why? Because celebration of accurate prophecy that involves death and tragedy is itself a negative energy. It feeds the wrong thing. Prophecy, in its truest form, is not a demonstration of a prophet’s insight. It is an invitation to intercession. It is God saying: *here is what is coming — will you pray?* This aligns precisely with the pattern of the canonical prophets. Jonah’s reluctant prophecy over Nineveh was not intended as condemnation — it was the merciful advance warning of a God who desired repentance. Amos declared coming judgment not to humiliate Israel but to create a window of return. Prophecy that has no invitation to pray, no urgency toward intercession, no compassion for the people it describes — that prophecy, however accurate, has been emptied of its Kingdom purpose. ## Implications and Challenges This teaching confronts a deeply embedded habit in much of contemporary charismatic Christianity: the confusion of spiritual vigilance with spiritual negativity. Many believers have been taught that discernment means identifying darkness, warning about deception, and marking every concerning development in the Body or the culture. And there is a legitimate place for scriptural discernment. But when the dominant spiritual posture becomes one of perpetual alarm — when the primary output is warning, rebuking, and cataloguing what is wrong — something has shifted from watchman to feeder. The question the teaching forces is not “Are there things worth warning about?” but “What atmosphere am I creating, and what is living in it?” There is also a real challenge here for those whose theological identity is built around *correctness* rather than *fruitfulness*. The prophet’s observation that critics never flourish is not merely motivational — it is a Kingdom diagnostic. Jesus said, “You will know them by their fruits” (Matthew 7:16). Fruitfulness requires sunlight, water, and root depth. It requires the kind of atmospheric engagement with God that produces growth. A tree spending all its energy defending its bark grows no fruit. And the saints who have oriented their ministry primarily around what they oppose rather than what they are building often find, over time, that their audience shrinks, their inner world grows brittle, and their declarations carry less and less authority — not because God has withdrawn from them, but because the atmosphere they inhabit cannot sustain the weight of Kingdom fruit. The distinction between God’s omnipresence and a cultivated atmosphere of His presence is one of the most practically important theological clarifications in this teaching, and it deserves far more attention in how we structure corporate worship, personal devotion, and pastoral care. Many believers have been taught that God is always present and therefore concluded that the *experience* of His presence is irrelevant or superfluous — a form of emotionalism to be resisted. But the Scriptures consistently draw a distinction between the God who fills all things (Jeremiah 23:24) and the manifest dwelling of God in a specific atmosphere (Exodus 40:34–35, Acts 2:1–4, Psalm 22:3). To collapse these into one another is to evacuate worship of its function and believers of their responsibility to *cultivate* rather than merely *claim*. The teaching on love as a verb also challenges a Western Christian sentimentality that has reduced ἀγάπη (ah-GAH-pay) to a warm feeling toward those who are easy to love. The gym metaphor is spiritually honest: the capacity for love is built in resistance, not in comfort. A church culture that avoids the difficult reps — the forgiveness of the one who deeply wronged you, the prayer for the one who publicly mocked you, the seeking of good for the one who wanted your downfall — is a church culture that remains spiritually small regardless of how loudly it proclaims its love for God. Finally, this teaching gently but clearly challenges the habit of using prophetic gifting as a platform for spiritual celebrity rather than priestly intercession. The prophet’s refusal to celebrate a fulfilled word about a tragedy is a model worth examining. Prophetic culture in our generation has at times drifted toward the validation of the prophet’s credibility rather than the mobilization of the saints toward intercession and compassion. When accuracy becomes the point, rather than invitation to prayer, the prophetic has been bent from its Kingdom purpose and is in danger of becoming exactly what the prophet warns against: a feeding trough for the wrong kind of energy. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** We are called to examine the inner architecture of our thought life with the same seriousness that a builder examines the foundation of a structure before adding weight. Spiritual maturity in this season means not merely avoiding sin in the behavioral sense, but actively refusing to build strongholds of negativity, criticism, and fear-rehearsal through the daily choices of what we meditate on, what voices we amplify, and what inner verdicts we allow to stand unchallenged by the Word. The σωφρονισμός (so-fron-is-MOS) Paul describes is not passive — it is a disciplined choosing, day after day, to fill the mind with what is true, noble, just, pure, lovely, and of good report (Philippians 4:8). **For Kingdom Obedience:** The response this teaching calls forth is threefold: cultivate the atmosphere, continue in love, and remain planted in acknowledgement. Practically, this means making worship and thanksgiving not a preliminary warm-up for the real spiritual work but the very substance of the spiritual environment in which real work is done. It means choosing, when someone who has wounded you crosses your path or your awareness, to pray rather than celebrate their difficulty. And it means identifying honestly where our inner world has become a plantation — and beginning, through repentance and the discipline of the renewed mind, to break up that ground and sow different seed. **For Prophetic Posture:** The saints are called to carry prophetic insight the way the canonical prophets carried it — as a burden of love, not a badge of credibility. When the Spirit reveals what is coming, the first response must be intercession, not announcement. The atmosphere of His presence is the only environment in which genuine prophetic authority is sustained and replenished. Departure from that atmosphere — whether through bitterness, celebrity, fear-mongering, or the celebration of confirmed doom — is departure from the very source of the sight. We watch, we warn, we weep, and we pray — and we do all of it from the posture of the one who has counted the trial as joy and found, in that counting, the deep current of a Kingdom that cannot be shaken. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who stands in the inheritance of Your Son, a co-heir of the Kingdom, a servant of the Word — and I make agreement with what You are speaking in this hour. I thank You that You have not given us a spirit of fear, but of power, and of love, and of a sound mind. I pray now for every heart that encounters this word: that the Spirit of the Living God would locate every stronghold that has been built from negative agreement, from fear rehearsed, from criticism made habit — and pull it down, not with condemnation but with the clean authority of the Word. Let the ἀγάπη (ah-GAH-pay) that was demonstrated in the cross be the measuring rod against which we weigh our inner responses, our declarations, and our prophetic offerings. Let us become people who cultivate Your atmosphere — who say “You are still good” when the report is bad, when the table is empty, when the betrayal is fresh — and who find, in that acknowledgement, the renewal of strength that no external injection can provide. We are not merely going through it, Lord. We are being opportuned. Receive our acknowledgement as incense and let the atmosphere of Your presence be the territory in which we live, move, and have our being. To You be all glory, honor, and dominion — now and to the age of ages. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |----------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Proverbs 23:7 — “For as he thinks in his heart, so is he” | שָׁעַר (sha’ar, shah-AR) — to reckon / calculate / gatekeep | The inner reckoning of the heart governs actual identity more than outward declaration | | 2 Corinthians 10:4–5 — “casting down arguments and every high thing” | λογισμοί (logismoi, loh-gis-MOY) — reasonings / imaginations / strongholds | Strongholds are cognitive-spiritual structures, not merely external demonic assignments | | Ephesians 4:27 — “nor give place to the devil” | τόπος (topos, TOH-pos) — location / territory / region | The warning is against granting the enemy a zone of occupation within the believer | | 2 Timothy 1:7 — “a sound mind” | σωφρονισμός (sōphronismos, so-fron-is-MOS) — whole, disciplined mind / sound judgment | Not merely self-control but a fully integrated and ordered inner life | | Isaiah 40:31 — “renew their strength” | חָלַף (chalaph, khah-LAHF) — to pass on / sprout fresh / change as a plant | Renewal is organic and rooted, not mechanical;
