# 09-01 Call Reflection: Provision, Open Hands, and the Crucified Old Man **Title:** 09-01 at 04:30 — Take Your Robes Off Me: The Vape, the Watchman, and the Old Man Who Doesn't Exist **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection --- ## ⚡ Opening Observation I was driving home from a twelve-hour welding shift when I called my brother Samuel, and what began as two tired men trading complaints about food deliveries and metrics became, without either of us steering it, a conversation about what it looks like to let go. The thread that keeps returning to me is the vape I handed my nineteen-year-old coworker without ever deciding to — words that came out of me before I could weigh them. Somewhere between his frantic posting schedule and my aching, twenty-pound boots, the Spirit kept surfacing the same question: whose hands are actually holding the outcome? I am still turning over the strange grace of not-wanting, and the disappointment-glare of a God who is standing closer than my own frustration. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the whole Christian life bends on the hinge of provision — not merely material provision, but the deeper provision of a will that is no longer clutching. Samuel and I are both living inside financial precarity, he chasing a sale before rent is due, I recovering from months of borrowing gas money to work a job that paid too little. The temptation in that place is to grip harder, to premeditate, to secure ourselves through anxious effort while our lips still confess trust. But discipleship is precisely the slow uncrucifying of the old man who insists he can save us if he just works fast enough. When a brother and I can name each other's projecting even as we affirm each other's faith, that is the Body doing what it was made to do — sharpening, rebuking in love, and reminding one another that the Lord provides in a timing we cannot manufacture. This is the daily war, and it is fought in vape cartridges and food-delivery refunds as truly as in any pulpit. --- ## 🌍 The Worldly Mind's Interpretation Read naturally, this is a phone call between two struggling young men managing scarcity. One is behind on rent and flooding social media with content, chasing an algorithm that might reward consistency with a sale. The other is exhausted from physical labor, underperforming against company KPIs, and coping with nicotine dependence and marital tension over money. The worldly frame would say: Samuel needs a better marketing strategy and a financial safety net; Seth needs to make more logical career decisions and manage his household anxiety; both would benefit from quitting nicotine for their health and finding therapy for the stress and PTSD Samuel names. The giving-away of the vape looks like impulsive generosity, perhaps even irresponsible given Seth's own tight budget. The talk of "the Holy Spirit coming on me" and "goosebumps" reads as emotional intensity mistaken for the supernatural. And the refusal to "just look for another job" appears to be spiritualized passivity dressed up as faith. **The Limitation:** The natural mind can see the scarcity but not the school it has become. It can measure the metrics, the boot weight, the piss-test policy — but it cannot detect the sovereign hand that arranges an hour-long drive into farmland, alone, with no one who speaks your language and nothing but Scripture for company. What looks like passivity to the flesh is often the hardest labor of all: the discipline of not moving until the One who commands the angels moves first. The worldly frame cannot categorize a gift that its giver did not decide to give, or a craving that simply lifts without a plan, because it has no room for a self that is being displaced by Another. It reads the goosebumps as feeling because it has never known the specific weight of holiness pressing on a body that cannot contain it. Where the flesh sees mismanagement, the Spirit is teaching two men to keep their hands open. --- ## ✝️ What the Spirit Reveals Through It I keep returning to the moment in the car. Kyler is nineteen, trying to quit vaping, and he can't even legally buy one here until he's twenty-one. We were passing mine back and forth at the gas station, the way men do, and I stepped down out of his car and heard myself say, "Oh, you can just hang on to that, by the way." He didn't understand. "What?" I said it was a full vape and he could have it. He kept asking if I was sure, said he'd pay me back, and I told him no, it's fine, I'll get another one at lunch. I am a slow talker. Samuel knows this about me — I think before nearly every word I say, I speak with intent. But those words did not come from that deliberating place in me. They arrived already spoken. What arrests me is that I had, just the day before, told my wife that nicotine is the one thing I cannot give up — that going without it changes me, sours my attitude, makes me a man I don't like being. I had drawn a line around that one appetite and called it non-negotiable. And then, without any inner debate, the very thing I had fenced off was simply handed away. It was only afterward that I realized the boy couldn't even buy his own, that this small object mattered to him. I did not feel the sacrifice because there was no self standing there to feel deprived. Something had already moved through me before the flesh could file its objection. The first echo that rose in me was of the men Jesus sent out, told to give as they had received, to carry nothing extra and to trust that provision would meet them. "Freely you have received, freely give" (Matthew 10:8). There is a kind of giving that is calculated — I have enough, therefore I can spare this — and there is a kind that flows from a source outside your own accounting. Mine was the second kind, and it frightened me a little, in the good way, because it revealed that I was not the one doing the math. I had a hundred dollars I thought I couldn't spare on AI credits, and afterward discovered I still had over a thousand in the account. The scarcity was in my perception, never in the supply. There is a word the Scriptures use for this open-handedness that I want to sit inside: ἁπλότης (haplotēs, hap-LOT-ace) — singleness, simplicity, generous sincerity of heart. Paul praises the Macedonians whose "deep poverty abounded in the riches of their liberality" (2 Corinthians 8:2), and the word underneath their giving is this singleness — an undivided heart that does not calculate because it is no longer double-minded about who owns the goods. My giving of the vape was a small tremor of ἁπλότης (hap-LOT-ace) breaking through a divided heart. I had been double-minded about nicotine, gripping it as the one thing that was mine. The Spirit simply pried that finger open without a lecture. This is where the older shadow becomes bright. When Samuel and I got to talking about waiting on the Lord, I found myself back at Zechariah's vision of Joshua the high priest, standing in filthy garments while Satan accuses him, and the LORD says, "Take away the filthy garments from him... See, I have removed your iniquity from you, and I will clothe you with rich robes" (Zechariah 3:4). Samuel actually said it — "Take your robes off me" — half-joking about the flesh, and I felt that goosebump-fire come. Because that is exactly the war. The old man wants to keep wearing the filthy garments, wants credit for the giving, wants to secure the rent, wants to grip the vape. And the answer is not our striving but our standing still while Another clothes us. The high priest in that vision says nothing and does nothing. He simply stands and is re-robed. And this connected in me to what I have long called the posture of the watchman — the one who stands on the rampart with his eyes fixed on the horizon of the firmament, so that when it moves, he knows. Habakkuk says, "I will stand my watch and set myself on the rampart, and watch to see what He will say to me" (Habakkuk 2:1). This is not passivity. It is the most active vigilance there is: disciplined receptivity every second of every day, waiting for the prompt that drops into the chest, the specific nausea or the outlet-shock of goosebumps that tells you the Holy Spirit has entered the moment and made it holy. Here the theological tension opened between us, and I had to speak it to Samuel in love. He was telling me that if he stays consistent he'll have posted over a hundred and sixty times by the end of twenty days — and I heard the projection creeping in. I said, gently, "I notice you're projecting again." He resisted at first — "I still gotta do the work or it's not gonna come" — and that is true. But the tension is razor-thin: the same mouth that says "the Lord will provide" can, two breaths later, be doing the algebra of its own salvation. I told him the way I would do it: focus only on the deed itself, the four videos, making them, posting them, and do not look at the metrics until something happens. The work is obedience. The outcome is the Lord's. To mix those two is to steal the robe back off the priest. The Kingdom mystery I could not have reasoned my way into is this: it is not harder to be a Christian man, it is easier. Samuel said it and I felt it land. We had both believed the lie that following Christ is the heavier yoke, that the flesh's road is the road of freedom. But Jesus said, "My yoke is easy and My burden is light" (Matthew 11:30), and the word there, χρηστός (chrēstos, khray-STOSS) — kind, well-fitted, good — means the yoke is tailored to the ox that wears it. The burden is heavy only for the old man, because the old man is being crucified by it. To the new man, walking in the Spirit, the same road is rest. Stressing, as Samuel said out of his own PTSD and chronic stress, has never gotten either of us anywhere. Staying calm and trusting has gotten us somewhere. And then there is the strangest mercy — the disappointment-glare. We talked about how sometimes we do the thing we know we shouldn't, and I described how I can feel Him, not raging but grieved, standing right there. "Really? Which one?" He asks when I plead that the old man won today. "The one that's buried? He doesn't exist. You mean the one you crucified with Me?" This is the sharpest rebuke of all, because it will not let me hide behind a fiction. Paul says plainly, "our old man was crucified with Him, that the body of sin might be done away with" (Romans 6:6). When I blame the old man, I am claiming a corpse can still command me. The rebuke is not condemnation; it is the refusal to let me lie about my own freedom. I saw myself clearly in that. There is a self-pitying comfort in saying "the flesh got me," because it excuses me from the authority I actually carry. But the Spirit will not coddle a crucified man into thinking he is still enslaved. This is a severe mercy — to be told, in effect, that I am more free than I want to admit, and therefore more responsible. The goosebumps I get when the holy enters a conversation are the flesh's inability to contain what the reborn spirit can now hold. My wife has noticed it, asked why my hairs stand up when we talk of Balaam's donkey or of Christ late into the night. "That's just the Holy Spirit coming on me," I told her. "The flesh can't even handle it." For the Body, what unfolds here is the whole shape of brotherhood under pressure. Samuel and I are not counseling each other toward worldly wisdom — get a safer job, diversify your income, manage your anxiety. We are reminding each other of the hope, of the fulfillment of the promises, rehearsing the goodness of the Lord precisely in the moment when the rent is unpaid and the KPIs are unmet. When I named his projecting, and he received it, that was the Body building itself up in love. When he called himself "Gentile Sam," and I said "for we once were foreigners, exiled from the chosen," and he said "but then the chosen rejected their God" — two brothers were preaching the whole gospel to each other on a phone call, the grafting-in of the Gentiles, without a pulpit between us. This is what the saints owe one another: not the anxious agreement of the world, but the stubborn reminder that He is sovereign, that He will stand until it is time to move, and then He will command the heavens. My wife's frustration when it looks like I am doing nothing — I said I'll drink that cup. Samuel's terror before the twentieth — he says he knows it will be sorted, though he has no idea how. That "I have no idea how, but I know it will be sorted" is faith in its cleanest form, and it is the very thing Jaden wrestled with when he asked how you can have faith in provision you have not yet seen. You can, because faith is "the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen" (Hebrews 11:1). The Body exists to hold that line for one another when the flesh of any single member starts to buckle. ## 🤔 Implications and Challenges **The open hand exposes the double heart.** When I handed away the one appetite I had sworn I could not surrender, it revealed that my scarcity was a perception, not a reality — I thought I had five or six hundred dollars left and actually had over a thousand. Much of modern Western Christianity, my own heart included, operates from a felt scarcity that God's actual provision keeps contradicting. We fence off the one non-negotiable — the vape, the salary, the security — and call it wisdom, when it is really the last stronghold of the double mind. The challenge is not to force ourselves into giving, which only produces a new legalism, but to let ἁπλότης (hap-LOT-ace) grow until the hand opens on its own. Where in your accounting have you declared something non-negotiable that the Spirit is quietly asking to hold loosely? **Confessing "the flesh won" can become a hiding place.** The rebuke I heard — "the old man doesn't exist, you crucified him with Me" — cuts against a whole devotional vocabulary that treats the flesh as an equal opponent still holding real power over the believer. Romans 6:6 says the old man was crucified; to blame him is to grant authority to a corpse. This is not to deny the war of the flesh Paul describes, but to refuse the self-pity that excuses sin by pretending we are still slaves. The gentle severity of this is that it makes us more responsible, not less, because it insists we are already free. Begin here with grace: He is not glaring in rage but in the grief of One standing right beside you, saying you know better than this. **Projection wears the mask of diligence.** I had to tell Samuel, in love, that projecting a hundred and sixty posts over twenty days had crept back in even as he confessed trust in the Lord. The correction is subtle because the work itself is obedience — he does have to make and post the videos. But the moment we fix our eyes on the metrics, on the projected outcome, we have quietly climbed back onto the throne of provision. The discipline is to labor at the deed and refuse to audit the result until the Lord acts. This challenges the whole metrics-driven, growth-projecting instinct that the culture has smuggled into ministry and Christian entrepreneurship alike. **Spiritualized waiting and fleshly passivity are not the same, and only the watchman can tell them apart.** The world reads my refusal to hunt for a new job as irresponsibility, and honestly, the logical move would have been to secure my family months earlier. But I did not come to Scripture through logical decisions; I came because something illogical was happening to me. The danger cuts both ways: some use "waiting on the Lord" to excuse genuine passivity and sloth. The safeguard is Habakkuk's rampart — disciplined receptivity, eyes fixed on the horizon, ready to move the instant He moves. If you are "waiting" but not watching, not straining to see, not ready to obey the moment the prompt drops, examine whether it is faith or fear wearing faith's clothes. **The easy yoke offends the flesh precisely because it strips it of its labor.** Samuel's discovery that it is easier, not harder, to be a Christian man overturns the martyr-complex many of us carry, where Christian faithfulness is measured by how heavy and joyless it feels. The yoke is χρηστός (khray-STOSS) — well-fitted, kind — heavy only to the old man being crucified by it. Yet this ease must never become presumption; the very next breath in our call named that "anything that is the Lord's will can be followed through with, even emptying yourself to the cross as Christ did." The ease is the rest of trust, not the ease of a lowered standard. The challenge is to stop wearing our stress as a badge of devotion. **Brotherhood must speak truth, not just comfort.** The most counter-cultural thing in this whole call may be that Samuel and I rebuked and reminded each other rather than merely commiserating. When he named that stressing gets him nowhere, and I named his projecting, and we both refused to let the conversation stay in complaint — "let's not talk about that, let's talk about how good the Lord is" — we were doing what the Body is for. Much modern fellowship has decayed into mutual venting that never lifts a brother's eyes. The grace-filled challenge is to become the kind of friend who can say "I notice you're projecting again" out of love, and the kind of man who can receive it. ## 🚀 What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity is measured not by how tightly we can hold our lives together but by how freely our hands open when the Spirit moves through them. The mark of the mature is that the giving, the surrender, the release of the guarded appetite happens before the flesh can lodge its complaint — because the self that would complain is being steadily displaced by Christ. We grow up into Him when we stop treating the crucified old man as a living master and start living from the freedom that is already ours. This is slow, unsanctified in places, honest about the withdrawals and the cravings, but always moving toward the singleness of heart that no longer calculates what it can afford to give. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience here looks like laboring at the deed and leaving the outcome with God — making the videos without worshiping the metrics, welding the box without securing the KPI as an idol, driving the hour into farmland because that is where He said to be. It means drinking the cup of looking like we are doing nothing when the Lord has told us to stand still, even when those we love are upset that we will not move on the world's timeline. Obedience is refusing to premeditate our own salvation, whether through anxious job-hunting or algorithm-chasing, and instead doing the next faithful thing in front of us. The command is clear: work as unto Him, and refuse to steal back the robe He is placing on us. **For Prophetic Posture:** This trains us to become watchmen on the rampart, eyes fixed on the firmament, cultivating a disciplined receptivity so fine-tuned that we know the prompt when it drops into the chest. The prophetic posture is not the manufacturing of words but the readiness to speak the ones that arrive already spoken, as the vape-word arrived in me. It is learning to feel the difference between our own deliberation and the fire that comes on us when a moment turns holy, and to yield to it without gripping. And it is the courage to speak the loving rebuke to a brother, and the humility to receive it, so that the whole company of the saints keeps its eyes on the horizon together, waiting for the One who will stand until it is time, and then command the heavens. ## 🔥 A Closing Prayer Father, I come to You from the cab of a car and the weight of these boots, tired in the body but strangely light in the spirit, and I thank You that You provide in a timing I cannot manufacture and did not deserve. I bless You that You pried open my hand around the one thing I called non-negotiable, and let me give it away before I could count the cost — and I ask that You keep doing it, until singleness of heart is my whole condition. Forgive me for the times I have blamed a crucified man for my own choices, and thank You for the mercy of Your disappointment-glare, which loves me too much to let me hide in that lie. I stand with my brother Samuel before You now, and I intercede for the sale before the twentieth, for the rent, for the food he lacks, and I make agreement with Your will that You are the God who provides and that it will be sorted, though neither of us knows how. Clothe us both in the rich robes, take the filthy garments off us, and set us on the rampart with eyes that never leave the horizon. Teach us the easy yoke that offends only our flesh, and let our brotherhood be one that speaks the truth in love and never sinks into mere complaint. I yield the metrics, the outcomes, the job, the timing, and even the withdrawals to You, and I ask that You would finish the sanctifying You have begun, gently, in Your own good time. Until then, keep me watching, keep me open-handed, and keep reminding me how good You are. In the name of Jesus Christ, who emptied Himself to the cross and is now my life, amen. --- claude-opus-4.8 --- ## 📝 Scripture References & Original Language Notes | Passage (NKJV) | Original Language | Theological Insight | | --- | --- | --- | | Matthew 10:8 | — | "Freely you have received, freely give" — the Spirit-borne generosity that flows from a source outside our own accounting, mirrored in the unpremeditated giving of the vape. | | 2 Corinthians 8:2 | ἁπλότης (haplotēs, hap-LOT-ace) — singleness, generous sincerity | The Macedonians' poverty overflowed in liberality; the undivided heart gives without calculating because it no longer disputes who owns the goods. | | Zechariah 3:4 | — | Joshua the high priest stands silent in filthy garments and is re-robed by the LORD — the picture of surrender: we stand still while Another clothes us. | | Habakkuk 2:1 | — | "I will stand my watch and set myself on the rampart" — the active vigilance of the watchman, disciplined receptivity awaiting the Lord's word. | | Matthew 11:30 | χρηστός (chrēstos, khray-STOSS) — kind, well-fitted, good | The yoke is tailored to the one who bears it; heavy only to the old man being crucified, restful to the new man walking in the Spirit. | | Romans 6:6 | — | "Our old man was crucified with Him" — to blame the flesh is to grant authority to a corpse; the rebuke that insists we are already free. | | Hebrews 11:1 | — | Faith as "the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen" — Samuel's "I don't know how, but I know it will be sorted" in its cleanest form. | ---
