# 05-03 Call Reflection: Spiritual Discernment — False Shepherds, Ministry Fraud, and Protecting the Flock **Title:** 05-03 at 16:50 — When the Wolf Wears the Collar of a Shepherd **Recorded:** 2026-05-03 16:50:26 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture — Phone/Video Call) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother walk me through what he had uncovered — the overdue filings, the layered organizations, the carefully constructed language of ministry wrapped around what appeared to be a systematic financial operation — something in me recognized the gravity of the moment long before either of us named it. This was not merely a story about one man’s questionable ministry; this was a collision with one of the oldest snares in the enemy’s playbook. What arrested me most was not the financial irregularity, troubling as it was, but the image my brother described: a father weeping in public confession before a ministry leader he had been conditioned to trust — and that image became the moral center of gravity for the entire conversation. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ in every generation has been required to contend not only with open persecution from without, but with something far more dangerous — corruption that wraps itself in the language, the aesthetics, and the emotional currency of genuine faith. When spiritual authority is wielded not to shepherd but to extract — when tears are harvested, vulnerabilities are monetized, and the vocabulary of Kingdom is harnessed for personal enrichment — the damage inflicted is not merely financial. It is theological. It teaches people that God is a commodity, that intimacy with Him is a product, and that vulnerability before the Body is a risk to be weaponized rather than a gift to be protected. The saints must be able to discern this. The failure to do so is not simply a matter of being deceived; it is a matter of being formed in a false image of God Himself. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, what my brother uncovered could be reduced to a straightforward story of institutional fraud: a charismatic leader building a network of organizations, leveraging public trust through religious language, and collecting funds with insufficient financial accountability. The natural mind would frame this entirely as a legal and regulatory problem — something for the Charity Commission, the Ombudsman, and perhaps investigative journalism. The emotional hook, the weeping father, the altar call for wallets — these would be read as manipulation tactics borrowed from the playbook of high-pressure sales, not fundamentally different from any other confidence operation dressed in respectable clothing. Natural reasoning would also be quick to offer a clean binary: report it and expose it, or walk away. It would not easily perceive that the decision itself might be a spiritual one, or that the timing — a fraud investigation company formed on the very same day as the event — might carry the weight of divine appointment rather than mere coincidence. **The Limitation:** What the natural mind cannot perceive from inside this situation is that the real wound being inflicted on those people is not primarily financial — it is covenantal. When a shepherd uses the language of “sowing” and “blessing” and “honor” to redirect the generosity of the saints toward personal enrichment, he is not simply mismanaging funds; he is distorting the very image of God as Father and Provider. The natural mind can audit a charity’s filing status, but it cannot account for the way false spiritual authority reshapes a person’s understanding of grace, of giving, of confession, and of belonging. Only the Spirit can illuminate how deeply a counterfeit shepherd wounds the flock — not merely their wallets, but their capacity to trust, to worship, and to come near. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sat with what my brother shared after we ended the call, the first thing the Spirit pressed upon me was a word from the mouth of Jesus Himself: *“Beware of false prophets, who come to you in sheep’s clothing, but inwardly they are ravenous wolves”* (Matthew 7:15). The Greek word translated “ravenous” there is ἅρπαξ (harpax, HAR-pax) — it means rapacious, predatory, one who seizes by force. It is the same word used in Luke 18:11 of the self-righteous Pharisee who distinguished himself from “extortioners.” Jesus was not speaking metaphorically about mild theological error when He used that word. He was describing a predatory orientation — a fundamental posture of taking rather than giving, consuming rather than laying down one’s life. What my brother described — the layered organizations, the ticket revenues that somehow still fell short, the donation link in the closing moments, the invitation to “sow with your wallets” — carries the distinctive mark of ἅρπαξ (HAR-pax) disguised in pastoral language. What struck me further was the particular phrase my brother recounted at the end of that audio: *“Let’s sow with our prayer and let’s sow with our wallets.”* The coupling of prayer and money in that construction is not incidental — it is deeply revealing. It conditions the listener to treat financial giving as spiritually equivalent to intercession, so that the refusal to give feels like a failure of faith rather than an exercise of wisdom. Paul warned Timothy of exactly this posture: *“…who suppose that godliness is a means of gain. From such withdraw yourself. Now godliness with contentment is great gain”* (1 Timothy 6:5–6). The Greek word there for “means of gain” is πορισμός (porismos, por-is-MOS) — a commercial term for revenue generation, a profit-making enterprise. Paul’s diagnosis was not that money is evil, but that the commodification of godliness — the treatment of spiritual access, spiritual vocabulary, and spiritual authority as instruments of financial extraction — is a corruption of the highest order. There is something I must name carefully here, because it carries real weight: the image that moved my brother to act was not the spreadsheet, not the overdue confirmation statements, not the web of companies. It was the sound of a father weeping. A man sitting in his own shame, confessing a disgusting thought before a ministry leader, tears streaming — and that vulnerability being presided over by someone who would, within the same gathering, direct the congregation toward a donation link. The Spirit does not let me pass over that image lightly. In Scripture, the place of confession and weeping is holy ground. When Jacob wrestled and wept, it was before God (Hosea 12:4). When David wept in his affliction, his tears were a covenant appeal (Psalm 56:8). The tears of the repentant are precious to God — and any spiritual authority that harvests those tears as social currency to reinforce their own position of trust has committed a deep violation against the image of the Father. This calls to mind the indictment in Ezekiel 34, one of the most searching passages in all of Scripture regarding false shepherding: *“You eat the fat and clothe yourselves with the wool; you slaughter the fatlings, but you do not feed the flock”* (Ezekiel 34:3). What God condemns through Ezekiel is not merely negligence — it is active consumption. The shepherds of Israel were not simply absent; they were feeding themselves from the very sheep they were commissioned to protect. And the language of clothing themselves with the wool is striking: they wore the appearance of the flock while extracting from the flock. This is exactly what religious branding does when it is weaponized — it takes the wool of genuine spiritual longing and weaves it into a garment of credibility for those who are, at their core, extracting rather than giving. I am also sitting with the timing my brother mentioned — that on the very same day he attended that event, before the event, he had formed Northgate Intelligence, a company centered on fraud investigation. I want to be careful here not to over-speak what the transcript does not fully confirm, but I will say this: the convergence of a man’s vocational formation and his exposure to a specific injustice on the same day is the kind of detail that deserves prayer rather than dismissal. Paul writes in Ephesians 2:10 that we are *“His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand that we should walk in them.”* The Greek word for “workmanship” is ποίημα (poiēma, poy-AY-mah) — the same root from which we get “poem.” We are God’s composed work, shaped with intention toward specific moments and specific labors. Whether my brother is meant to pursue this investigation is between him and the Spirit. But the convergence itself is the kind of thing worth holding before God with open hands. What the Spirit also pressed upon me is the nature of the appeal being made in that recorded message — the language of blessing, honor, and the testimony of twenty years of ministry, three books, national initiatives, discipleship schools. There is nothing inherently wrong with any of those things. But when they are assembled as a credential chain to establish authority prior to a financial request, they function rhetorically in a way that is worth naming: they create a social debt. The implicit message becomes, *look at what has been poured out — now it is your turn to pour back.* This is a subtle inversion of grace. Grace flows downward from God to humanity. The gospel does not ask the congregation to replenish the reservoir; it declares that the reservoir is inexhaustible. When a ministry consistently returns to the congregation as its financial source and frames that return as participation in Kingdom sowing, it has quietly relocated the source of supply from God to the people — and that is a theological error with spiritual consequences for everyone in the room. Paul’s own posture stands in instructive contrast. In 1 Corinthians 9, he carefully establishes his *right* to financial support — citing the law of Moses regarding the threshing ox, the logic of the soldier and the farmer, and the direct instruction of the Lord regarding those who preach the gospel. And then he does something remarkable: *“But I have used none of these things”* (1 Corinthians 9:15). His argument for the legitimacy of support becomes the very platform from which he declares his choice not to exercise it — so that the gospel would not be hindered, so that no one could say they became disciples because they felt financially obligated to their teacher. There is a kind of ministry that is so conscious of the Spirit’s supply that it holds its own financial needs loosely, and there is a kind of ministry that is so conscious of its own financial needs that it has quietly ceased to be Spirit-supplied at all. The discernment between the two is not always obvious — but the fruit eventually speaks. I found myself thinking of 2 Peter 2, where the apostle writes with an urgency that still carries heat across two millennia: *“By covetousness they will exploit you with deceptive words; for a long time their judgment has not been idle, and their destruction does not slumber”* (2 Peter 2:3). The word translated “exploit” is ἐμπορεύομαι (emporeuomai, em-por-YOO-om-eye) — a marketplace word, a commercial term, the word for a merchant conducting trade. Peter is saying that false teachers treat the congregation as a market and their words as merchandise. The transaction is spiritual in form and commercial in substance. And his warning is that this is not a peripheral concern — it is one of the clearest signs that a spirit other than the Spirit of Christ is operating. The question my brother wrestled with aloud — whether to pursue investigation, report to the authorities, or walk away — is genuinely difficult, and I do not want to answer it for him. But I will say this: the impulse to protect the vulnerable, when it is submitted to the Spirit and not driven by the adrenaline of exposure alone, is a righteous impulse. Proverbs 31:8–9 says, *“Open your mouth for the speechless, in the cause of all who are appointed to desolate. Open your mouth, judge righteously, and plead the cause of the poor and needy.”* The fathers and mothers sitting in that room, weeping, opening their wallets, trusting that God was moving through the man behind the microphone — they are the ones appointed to desolation in this picture. And the question of whether to speak on their behalf is not merely strategic. It is covenantal. What I keep returning to is the closing exchange between the two of us — almost offhand, almost ordinary — where I said what I genuinely believe: *“He comes disguised as an angel of light.”* Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 11:14, *“And no wonder! For Satan himself transforms himself into an angel of light.”* The Greek word for “transforms” is μετασχηματίζω (metaschēmatizō, meh-tah-skay-mah-TID-zo) — to change the outward form, to disguise, to appear as something other than what one truly is. The danger of that disguise is not that it fools the discerning; it is that it fools the tender-hearted, the newly believing, the spiritually hungry — those who are looking for a father and find instead a merchant wearing a father’s robe. And that is precisely why discernment is not a luxury in the Body of Christ. It is an act of love. ## Implications and Challenges There is a grace I want to extend at the outset: most people who enter rooms like the one my brother described are not fools. They are hungry. They are looking for genuine community, genuine fathering, genuine encounter with God. The existence of false shepherds is, in a painful way, a testimony to how deep the need for real ones is. Let us never speak about the deceived with contempt; let us speak about them with the grief that befits those who know what they deserved and were denied. That said, the Western church has developed some habits that make the kind of operation my brother uncovered not only possible but structurally invited. The culture of celebrity ministry — in which platform size, book count, years of service, and global reach function as automatic credibility markers — makes it difficult to ask the most basic questions: Is this person’s life accountable? Are their finances transparent? Does their private life match their public proclamation? When authority is granted on the basis of performance and personality rather than character and accountability, the door for exploitation swings wide open. The correction here is not cynicism about ministry; it is a return to the biblical pattern of elders whose lives are known, whose households are ordered, and whose financial dealings are above reproach (1 Timothy 3:1–7). The coupling of prayer and financial giving in the same motivational appeal — as in “sow with your prayer and sow with your wallet” — represents a theological conflation that deserves direct challenge. Prayer is communion. Giving is stewardship. Both are acts of worship, but they are not interchangeable, and the rhetorical joining of them in an appeal for funds is manipulative in its effect even if not always conscious in its intent. The saints deserve teachers who distinguish between these things clearly, who teach generous giving from the abundance of a transformed heart rather than from the pressure of a ministry budget. The use of weeping and public confession as a social context for financial appeals is one of the most troubling dimensions of what my brother described. Vulnerability is sacred in the Body of Christ. When someone breaks open before the community, that breaking is meant to produce healing, not harvest. A spiritual leader who presides over genuine brokenness and then pivots to a donation link has failed their most fundamental calling — to be a safe place for the wounded. The saints must be taught that their tears are not leverage, and that any community where their vulnerability consistently precedes a financial appeal is a community worth leaving. My brother’s instinct — to pray before acting, to determine whether this is Spirit-led before moving — is exactly right, and it deserves to be named as maturity rather than hesitation. The adrenaline of investigation is real, and I recognize it. But the adrenaline of righteous exposure, if it is not submitted to the Spirit, can become its own form of spiritual pride — the satisfaction of being the one who uncovered the darkness. The goal, if pursuit is warranted, must be the protection of the sheep and the removal of a harmful structure, not the performance of discernment before an audience. Let us both hold that before the Lord. Finally, the existence of this kind of operation should drive the genuine Body of Christ not to suspicion of all ministry, but to a deeper commitment to becoming the real thing. The antidote to counterfeit currency is not the refusal of all currency — it is the proliferation of the genuine. The antidote to false shepherding is not the abandonment of spiritual community — it is the raising up of leaders whose lives are offered rather than extracted, whose authority is servant-shaped, and whose financial dealings would survive any scrutiny because they were never about accumulation in the first place. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This conversation is a call to cultivate discernment as a discipline of love, not suspicion. The mature saint does not receive every ministry claim at face value, nor does they approach every gathering with accusation already formed. Rather, they learn to watch — to hold what is presented against what Scripture actually teaches about the character of genuine shepherds, to notice the pattern of appeals over time, to ask who benefits and who bears the cost. Spiritual maturity means holding a posture that is simultaneously open to genuine ministry and alert to the counterfeit, without collapsing into either naivety or cynicism. **For Kingdom Obedience:** If my brother is led by the Spirit to pursue this further, it must be done with the seriousness of a priestly act — not the enthusiasm of an investigation for its own sake. If there are sheep being fleeced, and if God has positioned a man with the specific skills to document and expose that, then doing so is not merely a civic responsibility. It is a Kingdom one. At the same time, obedience here also means walking away cleanly if the Spirit says to walk away — trusting that God is not dependent on any one instrument to protect His people. Obedience is not always escalation; sometimes it is the discipline of release. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic community must learn to hold two things simultaneously: a fierce commitment to protecting the vulnerable and a fierce commitment to not becoming accusers of brethren based on incomplete information or personal offense. The prophetic saints are watchmen on the wall — but watchmen who pray before they sound the alarm, who verify before they declare, and who remain submitted to the Spirit even when the adrenaline of righteous anger is coursing through them. We watch, we pray, we verify, we submit, and then — when the Spirit confirms — we speak. ## A Closing Prayer Father, we come before You as those who are Your own — co-heirs with Christ, servants of the Living God, made to stand between the heavens and the earth and agree with Your will and Your word. We thank You that You are not unaware of what is happening among Your people — that no overdue filing, no misdirected donation, no harvested tear escapes Your sight. We ask You to cover every person who sat in that room, who wept before a man they trusted, who gave from their lack into what they believed was Your purposes — cover them with the knowledge of Your true Fatherhood, restore what the thresher has taken, and bring each of them into the company of shepherds who lay down rather than extract. We pray for our brother who is carrying this weight — give him clarity, Lord. Confirm Your word to him. If he is meant to be Your instrument of protection for those sheep, send him with courage and restraint in equal measure; and if he is meant to release it to You, give him the peace that surpasses understanding to do so without guilt. And we ask You, Father, to raise up in this generation men and women whose ministry is so genuinely Spirit-supplied, so transparently uncovetous, so unmistakably servant-shaped, that the counterfeit stands exposed by contrast alone — not because we hunted it down, but because the real thing has come. To the Lamb who was slain and who is worthy to receive honor, glory, and dominion — all of it belongs to You. We receive it as a gift and return it as an offering. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Matthew 7:15 — “Beware of false prophets, who come to you in sheep’s clothing, but inwardly they are ravenous wolves” | ἅρπαξ (harpax, HAR-pax) — rapacious, predatory, one who seizes by force | The term describes not mere error but a fundamental predatory orientation;
