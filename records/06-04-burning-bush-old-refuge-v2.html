# 06-04 Call Reflection: The Burning Bush, Old Refuges, and Spiritual Formation **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Title:** 06-04 at 15:12 — The Burning Bush, the Old Refuge, and the God Who Dwells Within **Recorded:** 2026-06-04 15:12:02 **Classification:** Call Reflection — ## Opening Observation As I listened back over this call with my brother, I could feel one thread tying together what at first seemed like many separate movements: the Father was training attention, endurance, repentance, and surrender in the middle of ordinary pressure. The visible story included exhaustion, unfair treatment, family burdens, financial strain, longing, temptation, Scripture, forgiveness, Moses, prayer, and the ache of wanting to belong wholly to God. But beneath all of it, I am seeing a deeper invitation: turn aside when the bush burns, refuse the old refuge when pressure rises, and remember that the God outside creation has chosen to dwell within His people. **Why This Matters:** This matters because discipleship is rarely tested in clean, controlled, religious environments. It is tested when a boss speaks unjustly, when the body is tired, when family needs keep pressing, when old cravings whisper again, when faith feels low, when relationships expose idols, when forgiveness feels impossible, and when God interrupts our assumptions with holy fire in the wilderness. The Father is not merely trying to make us appear more spiritual; He is forming sons who can discern the battle beneath the day, receive correction without shame, pray for enemies without pretending wounds are small, and obey what is right when feelings lag behind. This call reminded me that the Spirit often reveals Kingdom reality not through polished moments, but through tired brothers telling the truth before God and choosing, even weakly, to keep walking toward Christ. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The worldly mind could hear this conversation and say it was simply two men processing stress, faith, exhaustion, emotional overload, personal transformation, and religious meaning. It could observe that my brother had endured workplace pressure, unfair criticism, lack of training, family responsibilities, debt, relational longing, and exposure to someone else’s despair, all while resisting substances that once functioned as coping mechanisms. It could interpret the return of cravings as a predictable stress response, the talk about Moses and Scripture as religious identity formation, the desire for marriage as unmet attachment longing, the tears over childhood songs as nostalgia, and the movement from feelings-based decisions to value-based decisions as emotional maturity. There is compassion in that reading, because the human frame truly does wear down under pressure. **The Limitation:** But natural reasoning can identify pressure without discerning purpose. It can diagnose exhaustion without perceiving formation. It can name the craving without recognizing the enemy’s strategy behind it or the Father’s training within it. It may say, “You wanted to return to old comforts because the day was brutal,” and that is true at one level, but the Spirit presses deeper: the brutal day was not the center of the battle; it was the setting in which the heart was being tested, exposed, and strengthened. The natural mind can observe changed behavior, but it cannot fully explain why a man can look poor on paper and yet feel alive in Christ, or why a late-night conversation about work, anger, Moses, forgiveness, Scripture, and temptation can become a burning bush where God calls His servants to turn aside. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I listened to my brother describe the weight of his day, I noticed how easily the visible conflict could have become the whole story. There was unfairness at work, exhaustion in his body, pressure at home, emotional burden from hearing someone speak about suicide, and the kind of accumulated strain that makes the flesh beg for relief. None of that should be minimized. Yet while he was speaking, something in me recognized that the boss, the work environment, and the stressful circumstances were not the deepest attack. The real Trojan horse was the old craving trying to come back through the open gate of fatigue. That distinction matters because temptation often hides inside circumstances that feel justifiable. James says, “But each one is tempted when he is drawn away by his own desires and enticed” (James 1:14, NKJV). The word πειρασμός (peirasmos, pay-rahz-MOS) — trial, testing, or temptation — carries a sobering double edge. The same pressure can become either a proving ground for faith or a doorway into sin. The circumstance itself is not always the point; the question is where the soul runs when the heat rises. My brother had every natural excuse to smoke, drink, or return to an old refuge. But he did not. In the Kingdom, sometimes victory looks like coming home exhausted and still refusing to feed what once mastered you. I am seeing that the craving was not merely about weed or alcohol. It was about refuge. The deeper question was, “Where will I go when I am crushed?” If I run to the old refuge, I strengthen the old identity. If I run to Christ, even weakly, even angrily, even through a phone call to a brother, I strengthen the new man. Paul speaks of putting off “the old man” and putting on the new, “created according to God, in true righteousness and holiness” (Ephesians 4:22–24, NKJV). The old man has habits, comforts, reflexes, and escape routes. But the Father is not merely forgiving an old identity; He is training us out of agreement with it. That is why the call itself became part of the warfare. When I told my brother that calling and speaking it out was a form of fleeing from the devil, I was thinking of James 4:7: “Therefore submit to God. Resist the devil and he will flee from you” (NKJV). The order matters. Resistance is not mere willpower; it begins with submission. The verb ὑποτάσσω (hypotassō, hoo-po-TAS-so) — to submit, arrange under, come under authority — helps me see that resisting the devil starts by coming back under God’s truth, God’s covering, and God’s way of escape. Hidden temptation loses strength when brought into accountable light. The devil thrives in secrecy, but confession drags the battle into the open. This is παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) — fatherly training / discipline — in motion. Hebrews says, “Now no chastening seems to be joyful for the present, but painful; nevertheless, afterward it yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it” (Hebrews 12:11, NKJV). The Father’s discipline is not mere punishment. It is the education of sons. It is the forming of holy reflexes until righteousness becomes more than agreement with doctrine; it becomes the practiced fruit of endured correction. My brother said correction from the Lord is one of the greatest gifts He gives, and I felt the weight of that. The flesh hates correction, but the spirit recognizes it as love. This also reframed the conversation about Marcus Aurelius and the idea that the obstacle is the journey. There is a measure of natural wisdom there, an observation from creation about hardship, endurance, and duty. But Christ reveals something deeper. The obstacle is not merely the journey; the obstacle becomes the place where the Father reveals what still needs to be formed in us. Stoicism may teach a man to endure pain with composure, but Christ teaches a son to endure pain in communion. The world can teach discipline without dependence, resilience without resurrection, and self-command without surrender. Jesus says, “Abide in Me, and I in you” (John 15:4, NKJV). The word μένω (menō, MEN-oh) — to remain, abide, continue — reminds me that fruit comes from union, not self-manufactured toughness. Then the conversation moved into a holy tenderness. My brother spoke of hearing an old Christian song from childhood and breaking down in tears. I do not believe that was mere nostalgia. It was a moment where the Father touched memory, innocence, grief, and calling all at once. Sometimes tears come because the Lord allows us to feel both how far we have wandered and how near He has remained. I thought of Elijah under the broom tree, depleted after fire had fallen on Mount Carmel. The Lord did not first rebuke his exhaustion. An angel touched him and said, “Arise and eat” (1 Kings 19:5, NKJV). God is not only the God of public fire; He is the God of quiet bread beside a collapsed servant. That image matters deeply to me. My brother’s day had fire in it, but it also needed bread. Sometimes the most spiritual thing a person needs is not another exhortation, but sleep, food, brotherhood, and the reassurance that God sees the body as well as the soul. The journey was too great for Elijah, and heaven knew it before Elijah had strength to name it. The Lord is commander, but He is also Father, shepherd, and provider. When my brother said he was upset but not worried because he knew the Lord would provide, I heard remembrance at work. Faith grows by building a history with God. David faced Goliath because he remembered the lion and the bear. Israel was commanded to remember Egypt, manna, water from the rock, and covenant mercy. Forgetfulness breeds panic, but remembrance strengthens trust. I also sensed the Spirit pressing on the small responsibilities: dishes, tables, meals, shopping, helping family, showing up, restraining anger, doing the ordinary thing in love. These can seem small, but Jesus says, “He who is faithful in what is least is faithful also in much” (Luke 16:10, NKJV). The flesh wants spiritual greatness without domestic obedience, prophetic language without practical love, revelation without responsibility. But the Father trains sons in hidden places before entrusting them with visible weight. The Kingdom is not only revealed in worship gatherings and theological insight; it is revealed when a tired man does the next faithful thing without applause. There was also grace in the way my brother did not let certain people see the fullness of his anger. He vented in brotherhood, where it could be witnessed and brought into the light, but he restrained himself where release might have caused harm. That does not automatically make anger righteous, but it does show the beginning of governance. Proverbs says, “A fool vents all his feelings, But a wise man holds them back” (Proverbs 29:11, NKJV). Self-control is not pretending there is no fire; it is refusing to burn the wrong altar. Anger must come under Christ, not become a private throne. From there, the conversation widened into the mystery that keeps arresting me: “The One who is outside of creation is in me.” That is not sentimental language. That is Colossians 1:27, “Christ in you, the hope of glory” (NKJV). The transcendent God, the One whom heaven and earth cannot contain, has chosen by His Spirit to dwell within redeemed people. The cross does not merely forgive from a distance; it inverts the order of reality. The alienated creature becomes the temple of the Living God. The weak, the foolish, the weary, and the poor in spirit become dwelling places of divine presence. That is why the question about low faith became so important. When my brother was asked what he uses to bring his faith back up when it is low, the deeper question emerged: “Why is my faith low?” God often begins not by handing us a technique, but by locating us. “Where are you?” He asks Adam in Genesis 3:9. In Hebrew, אַיֶּכָּה (ayyekah, eye-YEH-kah) — where are you? — is not an informational question. It is relational and diagnostic. God is calling the hidden man out of the bushes. Faith is not restored by hype; it is restored by return. The Father’s question is already pursuit. Jesus does something similar when He asks the disciples, “How many loaves do you have?” (Mark 6:38, NKJV). They see lack, but He trains them to notice what the Father has already placed in their hands. It may not be enough in their hands, but it becomes abundance in His. This is another form of παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah). The Father does not deny insufficiency; He invites insufficiency into communion with omnipotence. When faith feels low, I am learning not merely to ask how to feel spiritual again, but where my gaze has shifted and what has taken the central place that belongs to God. That led naturally into the ache of the “God-shaped vacuum” in man. Ecclesiastes says God “has put eternity in their hearts” (Ecclesiastes 3:11, NKJV). We think we want the car, the status, the relationship, the affirmation, the achievement, or the appearance of success, but beneath many cravings is a hunger for glory apart from God. Created things cannot bear the weight of divine presence. Marriage, family, companionship, and intimacy are holy gifts, but they collapse when asked to become gods. The Lord does not despise human love; He purifies it by bringing it under His lordship. This is why the conversation about marriage and singleness needed tenderness. Genesis says, “It is not good that man should be alone” (Genesis 2:18, NKJV). Marriage is not a concession to carnality; it is a gift from God, created before the fall. Yet Paul also says, “He who is unmarried cares for the things of the Lord—how he may please the Lord” (1 Corinthians 7:32, NKJV). These are not contradictions. They are different callings under one Lord. Some are graced for marriage. Some are graced for singleness. Some burn with desire and should marry. Some are drawn into undivided devotion. The point is not hierarchy, but obedience. The phrase “boundaries with God” troubled me because it touches something dangerous and real. God is not an accessory to be managed around our preferred life. He is jealous. “For the LORD, whose name is Jealous, is a jealous God” (Exodus 34:14, NKJV). His jealousy is not insecurity; it is covenant fire. He refuses to let idols devour the heart He created for Himself. Yet I must be careful not to turn intensity into a doctrine for everyone. Consecration must not become an excuse for neglect, pride, or relational irresponsibility. But neither should “balance” become the name comfort gives to compromise. The question is always: what has God given me, and what does faithfulness require? Moses helped hold that tension for me. He encountered God in a way that changed him. His face shone when he came down from the mountain, and the people were afraid to come near him (Exodus 34:29–30, NKJV). Deep encounter with God alters a person. It may unsettle familiar relationships because the one who returns from the mountain is not exactly the same as the one who went up. Yet Moses was not called to contempt for people; he was called to carry God’s presence among them. The issue is not whether human relationships matter. The issue is whether they can endure the glory of God without demanding that the servant dim his face. The burning bush became the central image that gathered all of this. Exodus says, “So when the LORD saw that he turned aside to look, God called to him from the midst of the bush” (Exodus 3:4, NKJV). The Hebrew סוּר (sur, SOOR) — to turn aside — matters here. Moses saw the fire, but the call came when God saw his attention. The Lord did not call Moses because Moses had a clean record. Moses had blood in his history. He had fled Egypt. He was hidden in the wilderness. Yet he still turned toward holy fire. Cain had blood on his hands and went out from the presence of the LORD. Moses had blood in his past and turned aside toward the presence of the LORD. That humbles me because we often measure readiness by external qualification: eloquence, reputation, institutional approval, theological polish, clean résumés. But the Lord searches for the one who will turn aside. This does not excuse sin. It magnifies mercy. The poor in spirit are blessed because they no longer pretend to possess sufficiency in themselves. Matthew 5:3 says, “Blessed are the poor in spirit, For theirs is the kingdom of heaven” (NKJV). The word πτωχός (ptōchos, ptoh-KHOS) — poor, beggarly, dependent — reveals not spiritual self-hatred, but empty-handed availability. God entrusts Kingdom to those who know they need mercy. I was also struck by the mercy of correction in handling Scripture. Sometimes I remember the truth of a passage but forget the exact reference. Sometimes I may mislabel a book or need a brother to correct me. That should not make me careless, but neither should it make me hide. Jeremiah 31:33 says, “I will put My law in their minds, and write it on their hearts” (NKJV). The Word remains authoritative outside me, and the Spirit writes it within me. If correction comes, I do not need to preserve an image by pretending I knew all along. Correction itself can become witness. παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) is not shame; it is sonship. The conversation also moved into the wonder of walking in the Spirit. I described it almost like being connected to an intelligence beyond comprehension, but even that analogy falls short. The Holy Spirit is not a mechanism, enhancement, or spiritual upgrade to human thinking. He is God. He searches all things, testifies of Christ, convicts of sin, comforts the saints, distributes gifts, and conforms us to the Son. To walk in the Spirit is not to become superhuman; it is to become truly human under the lordship of Jesus Christ. My brother spoke of wanting to be with God forever, and that opened the tension between longing and mercy. Moses asked to see God’s glory, and the LORD said, “You cannot see My face; for no man shall see Me, and live” (Exodus 33:20, NKJV). God’s כָּבוֹד (kavod, kah-VODE) — glory, weight, manifest honor — is not one more experience among experiences. His holiness would undo unglorified flesh. This helps me understand delay as mercy. The Bridegroom has not yet come in final consummation, not because He is neglectful, but because He is patient. Peter writes that the Lord is “longsuffering toward us, not willing that any should perish but that all should come to repentance” (2 Peter 3:9, NKJV). The glory that has not yet broken in fully may itself be mercy. Forgiveness then came into view with weight. My brother shared that someone had said, in essence, “I am not Jesus; I do not have to forgive.” There is painful honesty in that. No human being has the native capacity to hold perfect justice and perfect mercy without corruption. Yet the command remains: “forgiving one another, even as God in Christ forgave you” (Ephesians 4:32, NKJV). The verb χαρίζομαι (charizomai, khah-RID-zoh-my) — to forgive freely, show grace, release as an act of grace — reminds me that forgiveness is not denial of justice. It is entrusting judgment to the only One worthy to carry it. The cross proves that God does not overlook evil. Sin is so serious that the Son of God bore it in His own body. Forgiveness does not mean calling evil good, trusting prematurely, or erasing wisdom. It means I refuse to enthrone myself as final judge. I can release the offender to God without pretending the wound was small. I can bless without enabling. I can forgive without abandoning discernment. The Lord alone can hold judgment and love without becoming twisted by either. Hatred, however, cannot be cherished as a private right. John writes, “He who says he is in the light, and hates his brother, is in darkness until now” (1 John 2:9, NKJV). The word μισέω (miseō, mee-SEH-oh) — to hate, detest, reject with hostility — reveals that hatred contradicts the life of Christ. Yet I also have to speak carefully. Not every boundary is bitterness. Not every cry for justice is hatred. Not every wound has healed simply because a person can say religious words. The Spirit does not flatten trauma into slogans. He leads wounded people into truth, and truth includes both holiness and tenderness. This also sharpened discernment around false religious zeal. A person can use Christian language, speak of destiny, purity, providence, moral urgency, or even Jesus, and still bear fruit contrary to the Lamb. Jesus said, “You will know them by their fruits” (Matthew 7:16, NKJV). καρπός (karpos, kar-POS) — fruit, produce, visible outcome — matters because the source of a tree is revealed by what it bears. Christian vocabulary without cruciform love becomes a counterfeit banner. The leaven of hypocrisy, power, and human agenda can hide inside religious speech if it is severed from the nature of Christ. Judas, Jonah, and Peter became warnings and encouragements. Judas felt remorse, but he did not return to the Lord in living repentance. There is a difference between μετάμελομαι (metamelomai, meh-tah-MEH-lo-my) — remorse or regret after the fact — and μετάνοια (metanoia, meh-TAH-noy-ah) — repentance, a change of mind and direction toward God. Judas grieved consequence and collapsed inward. Jonah fled, resisted, and still prayed from the belly of the deep, “Salvation is of the LORD” (Jonah 2:9, NKJV). Peter denied Jesus three times, wept bitterly, and was restored by the risen Christ on the shore: “Do you love Me?… Feed My sheep” (John 21:17, NKJV). The warning is not only against sin, but against the pride and despair that refuse to return after sin. That distinction between remorse and repentance matters for all of us. Many people mistake pain over consequences for repentance. But μετάνοια (meh-TAH-noy-ah) is not self-loathing dressed as holiness. It is the mercy of God granting a new mind and a new direction. Peter’s failure did not become final because he returned to Jesus. Jonah’s rebellion did not end the story because he cried out from the deep. The enemy wants failure to become identity. The Lord calls failure into the light so mercy can finish its work. One of the clearest signs of grace in my brother was that he had stopped making decisions merely based on how he felt and had begun choosing based on what was right. That is not mere self-improvement. That is fruit. Feelings are real, but they are not lord. Philippians holds the mystery together: “work out your own salvation with fear and trembling,” and then, “for it is God who works in you both to will and to do for His good pleasure” (Philippians 2:12–13, NKJV). It is truly us obeying, and yet we would not obey without the strength of Christ. Grace initiates, strengthens, corrects, and sustains, but obedience is not bypassed. Even the small conviction about asking the mechanic’s name mattered. That man was not merely “the mechanic.” He was a neighbor, a soul, someone known by God. Jesus calls His sheep by name (John 10:3, NKJV). He called Mary by name after the resurrection, and grief turned into recognition (John 20:16, NKJV). Love becomes practical when people stop being background characters in my story. The Kingdom trains me out of abstraction and into priestly attention. It is one thing to pray vaguely for “that guy who helped me.” It is another to know his name and carry him before the Father as a person. The same priestly attention appeared when my brother recognized the need to pray for his boss. The flesh wants to rehearse offense, diagnose people with contempt, and secretly hope judgment falls. But Jesus says, “Love your enemies, bless those who curse you… and pray for those who spitefully use you and persecute you” (Matthew 5:44, NKJV). Prayer for the difficult person does not mean enabling wickedness. It means refusing to let bitterness become master. It means discerning the enemy beneath temptation, accusation, despair, addiction, and compromise without misdirecting hatred toward flesh and blood. The conversation also touched the wonder of Scripture itself. Someone assumed Scripture must have been written by extraordinarily impressive intellectuals, but the marvel of the Bible is not that God gathered the naturally elite to display genius. The marvel is that the Holy Spirit breathed through shepherds, fishermen, kings, exiles, prophets, tax collectors, physicians, prisoners, and men with blood on their hands. Moses was a murderer before he became the prophet through whom God gave Torah. Peter denied Christ before preaching resurrection power. Paul persecuted the church before becoming an apostle of grace. “All Scripture is given by inspiration of God” (2 Timothy 3:16, NKJV). θεόπνευστος (theopneustos, thay-OP-nyoo-stos) — God-breathed — means Scripture is holy because God breathed through human vessels without ceasing to be God. As I sit with the whole call, I see that the Spirit was not moving randomly across topics. The Father was revealing one living thread: God enters ordinary lack and reveals Himself as enough. Low faith, hidden idols, old cravings, longing for marriage, exhaustion, debt, anger, forgiveness, Scripture, Moses, prayer, repentance, and calling all converged around one question: Will God be central, or will He be used to support something else? The One outside creation dwells within us. The Shepherd who calls by name teaches us to ask others their names. The God who fed multitudes with loaves teaches us to notice what is already in our hands. The Christ who prayed for His enemies teaches us to pray for our offenders. The Spirit who breathed Scripture through weak men is still forming weak men into witnesses. And I am left with the burning bush. Turn aside from image management. Turn aside from hatred. Turn aside from false zeal. Turn aside from despairing remorse. Turn aside from half obedience. Turn aside from distraction. Turn aside from the assumption that God only calls the already-qualified. The bush is burning in the wilderness, and the question is not whether God can speak. The question is whether I will look long enough to hear Him call my name. ## Implications and Challenges Grace meets us first. The Lord is not indifferent to exhaustion, unfair treatment, family strain, mental health burdens, financial pressure, relational longing, or withdrawal from old coping patterns. We must be careful not to spiritualize pain in a way that becomes cruel. Sometimes holy care looks like sleep, food, brotherhood, practical help, and the reassurance that God sees the whole person. At the same time, this challenges the modern habit of treating every craving as morally neutral simply because it is emotionally understandable. A craving may have history, chemistry, trauma, and stress behind it, and compassion must take that seriously. But a craving can still become a spiritual doorway if obeyed. The question is not only, “Why do I want this?” but, “Where will this lead me if I submit to it?” This meditation confronts shallow ideas of faith as emotional maintenance. The question is not merely, “How do I feel spiritual again?” The deeper question is, “Where have I moved away from God? What has taken the center? What is the Father exposing so that I can return?” Faith is strengthened not by hype, but by truthful return to the Shepherd who is already calling. It also challenges our view of holiness and ordinary life. Cooking, cleaning, working, shopping, praying for a boss, asking a mechanic’s name, restraining anger, and helping family can become altars of obedience. The Kingdom is not only displayed in public ministry or theological insight. It is often revealed in hidden faithfulness when no one applauds. We must recover a biblical understanding of forgiveness. Forgiveness is not emotional denial, weakness, or the erasure of accountability. It is entrusting judgment to God because Christ has borne what no human heart can carry rightly. Hatred cannot be baptized as discernment, but neither should wounded people be rushed with slogans. The Spirit leads us into truth with holiness and tenderness. Finally, grace sends us forward with hope. The Lord is not asking us to become impressive before we turn aside; He is asking us to turn aside so He can make us holy. He restores Peters, hears Jonahs from the deep, warns us through Judas, feeds exhausted Elijahs, and calls Moses from wilderness fire. The Father’s correction is not rejection. It is the training of sons. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** We learn to interpret pressure with discernment. We do not deny natural causes, but we do not stop there. Maturity asks what the Father is forming, what the enemy is exploiting, what the flesh is demanding, and what obedience looks like in the next faithful step. We stop treating low faith as merely a mood problem and receive it as an invitation to locate our hearts before God. **For Kingdom Obedience:** We refuse old refuges even when the excuse feels valid. We call a brother, confess the craving, eat, sleep, pray, worship, forgive, ask names, bless enemies, open Scripture, and do what is right even when emotion resists. Obedience may look small, but in the Spirit, small obedience can close a massive door. **For Prophetic Posture:** We watch for the fire of God in ordinary places. The prophetic posture is not hype, suspicion, or dramatic exaggeration. It is sober attentiveness under holiness. We listen, test fruit, search Scripture, receive παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah), discern counterfeit zeal, wait for the Bridegroom, and testify faithfully to what God is actually revealing without adding to it. ## A Closing Prayer Father, in the name of Jesus Christ, I thank You that You are the God who commands fire from heaven and the Father who prepares bread for exhausted servants. I thank You that the One outside creation has chosen to dwell in us by Your Spirit, and that You do not despise broken vessels who turn aside toward Your fire. As a co-heir of the Kingdom under the authority of the New Covenant, as one called to stand in agreement with Your will between heaven and earth, as an answer to prayers You have stirred and as one of Your servants who desires to honor the secrets You give to Your servants the prophets, I surrender again to Your searching questions, Your holy jealousy, Your fatherly correction, and Your mercy. Teach us to recognize the Trojan horse before we open the gate, to refuse old refuges when pressure rises, to forgive as forgiven ones, to pray for those who burden us, to love people by name, and to return quickly when we fall. Strengthen my brother, strengthen me, and strengthen all the saints who are fighting hidden battles under visible pressure. Let every burning bush You appoint find us attentive, barefoot, and ready to obey. Let old cravings lose their voice, false refuges collapse, bitterness surrender to the cross, and the peaceable fruit of righteousness grow in us through Your loving discipline. Yours is the Kingdom and the power and the glory forever. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |-------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | James 1:14–15 | πειρασμός (peirasmos, pay-rahz-MOS) | Trials and temptations expose desire;
