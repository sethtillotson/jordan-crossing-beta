# 03-28 Sermon Reflection: Divine Standards and Refusing the Valley - Prophet Lovy L. Elias **Source Discernment:** E — Recorded Sermon/Teaching + Reflection **Reflection: Divine Standards and the Refusal to Return to Egypt** ## Opening Observation I am sitting with a powerful word from Prophet Lovy L. Elias, and something in his declaration has arrested my spirit: “You cannot condemn something if you don’t even know the law and the standard of your life that God has ordained for you.” This is not motivational rhetoric—this is covenant language. This is the voice of a man who has learned to discern between what was permissible in a season of Egypt and what is forbidden in a season of Canaan. I hear in his words the echo of a fundamental spiritual truth: transformation is not complete until we learn to recognize, name, and refuse what once defined us. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Church is filled with people who have been delivered but not transformed, rescued but not renewed, saved but not separated unto holiness. We celebrate what God brought us *out of*, but we have not yet embraced what God is calling us *into*. Without divine standards—without the Spirit-illuminated knowledge of what God has ordained as the pattern and posture of our lives—we cannot properly condemn the old, refuse the familiar, or set boundaries against those who would pull us back into valleys we were never meant to inhabit again. Discernment without standards is sentimentality. Deliverance without standards is a revolving door. But when we know the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus, we gain the authority to say: “That was good for that time. Not for right now.” ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind hears this teaching and interprets it through the lens of self-improvement and personal boundaries. It says: “This is about setting healthy limits, protecting your energy, cutting off toxic people, and refusing to let anyone drag you back into past trauma or unhealthy patterns.” It frames the prophet’s words as therapeutic empowerment—learning to say no, recognizing your worth, refusing to be codependent or to absorb others’ pain. It celebrates the friend who moved on after divorce, who found new love, who “leveled up” and left the past behind. The worldly mind sees this as emotional maturity, psychological resilience, and the natural progression of healing after loss. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind cannot see that this is not primarily about emotional health—it is about **covenant fidelity**. It cannot perceive that the standards the prophet is speaking of are not self-generated boundaries but **divinely ordained laws of life in the Spirit**. The unspiritual person hears “you cannot be next to me” and thinks it is about protecting one’s peace; but the Spirit reveals that it is about **protecting one’s calling**. The flesh hears “I will not feel your pain” and calls it coldness; the Spirit recognizes it as **apostolic sobriety**—the refusal to be moved by sympathy into disobedience, the refusal to let another’s unbelief become the ceiling of one’s own faith. What the natural mind cannot grasp is that there is a **law**—a divine standard, a kingdom order—that governs what is permissible in each season of spiritual maturity, and that ignorance of that law leaves us vulnerable to regression, compromise, and the seduction of the familiar. ## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation I am seeing something here that I have often felt but rarely heard articulated with this clarity: the spiritual life is not a static state of “saved” or “unsaved,” but a **progressive revelation of divine standards**. What was acceptable in the wilderness is forbidden in the Promised Land. What was tolerated under the law is condemned under grace. What was permissible in spiritual infancy becomes unbearable in spiritual maturity. The prophet says, “There is a certain way I will never travel again. There are certain ways I used to eat. I will never eat like that again.” This is not legalism—this is the testimony of a man who has been **taught by the Spirit** what the will of God is for his life, and who has learned to condemn—that is, to judge, to reject, to refuse—anything that contradicts it. The Greek word for “condemn” in the New Testament is κατακρίνω (*katakrino*, kah-tah-KREE-no)—to judge against, to pronounce sentence upon, to declare unfit. It is a legal term. It is not emotional rejection; it is **judicial discernment**. The prophet is saying: I have come to know the law of my life in God, and by that law, I now pronounce certain former patterns, relationships, and appetites as **condemned**—not because they were evil in themselves, but because they are incompatible with where God is taking me. This is the same spirit that caused Paul to write, “All things are lawful for me, but all things are not helpful. All things are lawful for me, but I will not be brought under the power of any” (1 Corinthians 6:12, NKJV). Paul knew the **standard**. He knew the law of liberty. And by that law, he condemned even lawful things that would hinder his race. I notice the prophet’s insistence: “If you are going to walk with me, you better come out of that valley.” This is not arrogance. This is the **law of spiritual companionship**. Amos asked, “Can two walk together, unless they are agreed?” (Amos 3:3, NKJV). The Hebrew word for “agreed” is יָעַד (*ya’ad*, yah-AHD)—to appoint, to meet at an appointed place, to come into covenant alignment. The question is not whether two people like each other or sympathize with each other, but whether they are **walking toward the same destination** under the same covenant. If one is still in Egypt and the other is in Canaan, they cannot walk together. If one is mourning the loss of slavery’s leeks and garlic, and the other is eating the fruit of the land, they cannot share a table. The prophet is not rejecting the wounded; he is refusing to **return to the place of wounding** in order to keep them company. Something arrests me here: “When people are in pain, they want everybody to be in pain, to feel their pain. I am not going to feel it. I will pray for you. I will think of you where you are, but I will encourage you to come out because there are better days.” This is a razor-sharp distinction between **compassion** and **codependence**, between **intercession** and **infection**. The modern Church has conflated empathy with agreement, sympathy with solidarity, and we have lost the prophetic edge that says: “I will stand with you in prayer, but I will not stand with you in unbelief.” The prophet is modeling what it looks like to **love without lowering**—to love someone enough to refuse to validate their valley, to refuse to make their brokenness normative, to refuse to let their pain become the atmosphere of the relationship. I am reminded of the story the prophet tells—a friend devastated by divorce, convinced his wife was his life, met with the sober word: “Why are you still breathing? Before she came into your life, were you not alive? She enhanced your life. She was not your life.” This is not cruelty. This is **surgical truth**. It is the kind of love that cuts through the lie of codependency and identity confusion and reminds a man that his **life is hidden with Christ in God** (Colossians 3:3, NKJV). The world calls this coldness. The Spirit calls it **deliverance**. Because the prophet knew something the broken man could not yet see: God had more. God had better. And the only way to receive what God had was to **condemn the old definition of life**. The prophet says, “I also know friends that died. That was nothing better than that yet God has more.” This shakes me. There are people who die—physically, emotionally, spiritually—because they cannot let go of what God has already judged. They cling to the valley because the valley is familiar. They refuse the mountain because the mountain requires them to **condemn the old standard**. But the man who knows the divine standard, who has learned to discern the law of his life in God, becomes “very dangerous” in the best sense: “Because nothing moves me. I’m fine regardless. I know I will be okay and I will thrive.” This is not self-sufficiency. This is **God-sufficiency**. This is the testimony of a man who has learned that **only the Lord is the center**. I notice the language of centrality: “I love my children. I would give my life at any time, but I even know they are not the center of my life. Nobody is only the Lord. Only the Lord.” This echoes Jesus’ own hard word: “He who loves son or daughter more than Me is not worthy of Me” (Matthew 10:37, NKJV). The divine standard is not that we love our children less, but that we love Christ **supremely**—so supremely that every other love finds its proper place, its proper proportion, its proper **subjection to the law of the Kingdom**. When God is the center, we can love freely without idolatry. When God is not the center, every love becomes a potential snare. The prophet concludes with a charge: “I want you to look through your life and begin to condemn every single thing that contradicts the word of God concerning your life.” This is not a call to self-hatred. This is a call to **Spirit-led judicial review**. It is the believer’s responsibility to **know the standard**, to study the Word, to sit under the illumination of the Holy Spirit, and to say: “By this law, I condemn this appetite. By this law, I condemn this relationship. By this law, I condemn this pattern of speech, this manner of eating, this path of travel.” The Greek word for “word” here is λόγος (*logos*, LAW-gos)—the declared will, the divine decree, the governing principle. When we know the λόγος (LAW-gos) of God concerning our lives, we gain the authority to **legislate against** everything that opposes it. As I sit with this word, I am seeing that the science of prayer the prophet promises to teach is inseparable from this foundation: **prayer is only as powerful as the standard by which we live**. A man who does not know what God has ordained for his life cannot pray with authority, because he does not know what to ask for. He does not know what to condemn. He does not know what to refuse. But the man who knows the divine standard prays **from the law**, not toward it. He prays from the place of covenant alignment, and his prayers carry the force of **judicial decree**. This is why the prophet can say, “When power is available”—because power flows through the man who lives by divine standards, not through the man who is still negotiating with Egypt. I am also seeing the danger of **addiction to pain**. The prophet says, “All these things can be extremely addictive.” This is a profound psychological and spiritual insight: people can become so accustomed to suffering that they cannot function without it. They organize their identity around their wounds. They recruit others into their narrative of brokenness. And when someone refuses to participate, they feel abandoned. But the Kingdom does not call us to **participate in one another’s valleys**—it calls us to **intercede for one another’s deliverance**. There is a vast difference. Participation validates the valley. Intercession calls forth the mountain. The story of the friend who was delivered through divorce is a perfect case study. The world would call it tragedy. The prophet called it **deliverance**. And two years later, the evidence proved the prophet right: children, flourishing, gratitude. But here is the key: the friend said, “I’m grateful that you did not allow me.” This is the fruit of **apostolic friendship**—the refusal to affirm what God has condemned, the courage to speak the hard word, the willingness to stand alone in truth until the other catches up. The prophet knew where his friend was, and where he was going. And he refused to let the present pain obscure the future promise. I am pressing into this question: What does it mean to have **divine standards**? It means knowing, by revelation and by study, what God has declared concerning your life. It means being so familiar with the Word that you can **discern contradictions** instantly. It means being so yielded to the Spirit that you can **feel the dissonance** when something is out of alignment—even if it is culturally acceptable, emotionally comforting, or relationally convenient. Divine standards are not arbitrary rules. They are the **laws of the Kingdom**, the governing principles of the age to come, made manifest in this present age through the lives of those who walk by the Spirit. The prophet’s repeated refrain—“It was good for that time, not for right now”—is a masterclass in **spiritual discernment across seasons**. This is not relativism. This is the recognition that God leads us through progressive stages of maturity, and what He permits in one season, He may prohibit in the next. What He tolerates in the wilderness, He judges in the land. What He allows under the old covenant, He condemns under the new. The believer who does not understand this will either legalize the past or excuse the present. But the believer who knows the law of his life will **honor the season** without being enslaved to it. ---
