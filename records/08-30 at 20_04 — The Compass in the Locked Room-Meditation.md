# 08-30 Call Reflection: Freedom from the “Man of the Flesh” and New Identity in Christ

**Title:** 08-30 at 08:01 — The Man of the Flesh Held Me Hostage

**Recorded:** 2026-08-30 08:01:54

**Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture)

**Classification:** Call Reflection

## Opening Observation

My brother called before I had fully shaken off the night, and within a few sentences the whole weight of the conversation surfaced: he feels the past holding him hostage, and he wants to know who he is in the Lord. I heard him name the “man of the flesh” as the very thing dragging on him, and I heard him confess that he is hard on himself in all the wrong ways. There was tenderness in it, and there was ache. And when we hung up, I found myself thanking Jesus aloud for the miraculous work being done — in Samuel, and in me.

**Why This Matters:**

This matters because the cry my brother voiced is not a passing mood but the central struggle of every disciple learning to walk in the newness of Christ. To feel held hostage by the past is to feel the pull of the old man that Scripture insists has already been crucified, and the confusion of that gap — between what is finished in Christ and what still feels alive in the flesh — is where countless believers lose their footing. If we cannot see ourselves as God sees us, we will keep flogging the wrong person, disciplining a corpse while the true self starves. This is a question of identity, and identity governs endurance, obedience, holiness, and love. When a brother asks, “Who am I with the Lord?” he is asking the very question the gospel was written to answer. And because he named that we are walking through this in different ways yet the same season, it becomes a matter for the whole Body: we sanctify one another by carrying these questions together rather than alone.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

The natural mind hears this call and reaches immediately for the language of self-improvement. It would say my brother simply needs to forgive himself, move on, set goals, and reframe his self-talk — that he is carrying unnecessary guilt and ought to release it through better boundaries and a more positive self-image. The bad night’s sleep, the missed church service, the mind that would not quiet down: all of it reads to the flesh as ordinary stress requiring rest and a fresh start. The worldly framework treats “coming into a new identity” as an act of the will, a decision to become a better version of oneself, motivated by affirmation and sustained by discipline. It is compassionate advice, and it is not entirely wrong about the ache. But it locates both the problem and the cure inside the man himself.

**The Limitation:**

The trouble is that the man of the flesh cannot crucify the man of the flesh; the drowning man cannot pull himself out by his own hair. Self-forgiveness, offered as the flesh’s remedy, quietly enthrones the self as the one with authority to acquit — and the conscience knows better, which is why the guilt keeps returning no matter how firmly one resolves to move on. The natural mind cannot see that identity in the Kingdom is not achieved but received, not manufactured but revealed, not the reward of striving but the gift of a finished cross. It reads my brother’s question as a psychological need for confidence when it is in fact a spiritual hunger for revelation. What he is reaching for cannot be constructed from below; it must be unveiled from above, and that unveiling belongs to the Spirit alone.

## What the Spirit Reveals Through It

I keep returning to the way he said it — that the past feels like it is holding him hostage, that there is “this person which is holding me back,” the man of the flesh. He was tired, he had been awake through a night too warm to sleep, he had missed the morning gathering and felt the grief of it, and out of that raw place he handed me the truest thing in him. He wanted a proper Bible study, not a chat. He wanted to know who the Lord sees when the Lord looks at him, and he wanted to embody that person. There is something holy in a man too weary to posture, saying plainly what he longs for.

What arrests me is the precision of his self-diagnosis: he said he is hard on himself, but in all the wrong ways. That single line carries more truth than most sermons on the subject. He has sensed that his severity is misaimed — that he is punishing the wrong man, or punishing rightly but without the cross, which turns discipline into torment. The Spirit is pressing on that exact seam, because the difference between godly conviction and fleshly self-condemnation is the whole difference between life and death, and my brother can feel the two tangled together inside him.

The first Scripture that opens over this is Paul’s cry in Romans, “For the good that I will to do, I do not do; but the evil I will not to do, that I practice” (Romans 7:19), climaxing in “O wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death?” (Romans 7:24). Notice Paul does not answer his own question with self-forgiveness or self-effort. He answers it with a Person: “I thank God—through Jesus Christ our Lord!” (Romans 7:25). The deliverance my brother is reaching for has a name, and it is not a better version of himself.

The old man he feels chained to has a specific fate in Scripture. Paul writes, “knowing this, that our old man was crucified with Him, that the body of sin might be done away with, that we should no longer be slaves of sin” (Romans 6:6). The word underneath “old man” is παλαιός (palaios, pah-lai-OSS) — old, worn out, belonging to a former age that has passed. It is not merely old in years but obsolete, superseded, belonging to a world order that the cross has already dismantled. When my brother feels held hostage by that man, he is being held by a captor whom God has already sentenced and executed. The chains are real to his experience but they are legally severed at the cross.

And over against the παλαιός (pah-lai-OSS) stands the new. “Therefore, if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation; old things have passed away; behold, all things have become new” (2 Corinthians 5:17). The word Paul reaches for is καινός (kainos, kai-NOSS) — new not merely in time but new in kind, unprecedented, of a different quality altogether. This is why my brother cannot construct his new identity by self-improvement: you cannot improve your way into a different kind of being. The new creation is not the old man renovated; it is a καινός (kai-NOSS) thing spoken into existence by the same God who said, “Let there be light.”

I see the canonical shadow of this stretching all the way back. When Jacob wrestled through the night and would not let go until he was blessed, he limped away with a new name — Israel — and a new walk (Genesis 32:24–31). The old name Jacob, “supplanter,” the grasping deceiver, was not simply improved; he was given a new identity by encounter, and it cost him a night of striving and a wound in his flesh. My brother told me he lay awake all night, his mind running, sensing the Spirit pushing him toward something. I wonder whether he is closer to the Jabbok than he knows, in the very sleeplessness he apologized for.

The theological tension here is the one every honest believer feels: the “already” and the “not yet.” The old man is already crucified, yet he still seems to speak. Scripture resolves this not by denying the struggle but by relocating our identity. Paul does not say “try harder to feel new.” He says, “Reckon yourselves to be dead indeed to sin, but alive to God in Christ Jesus” (Romans 6:11). The word “reckon” is an accounting term — λογίζομαι (logizomai, log-EED-zoh-my), to count as true, to credit to the account. We are commanded to agree with the verdict heaven has already rendered, not to generate a new verdict by our feelings. My brother’s severity toward himself is, in this light, a quiet refusal to reckon.

Here is the Kingdom mystery natural reasoning would miss entirely: the reason he cannot forgive himself is not that he lacks the will, but that self-forgiveness is a category the gospel never offers, because it is a throne no creature can occupy. Guilt is a debt owed to God, and only the offended party can absolve it. When my brother tries to forgive himself and cannot, he is actually being protected — kept from a counterfeit peace — and driven toward the only One who can say, “Neither do I condemn you; go and sin no more” (John 8:11). His frustrated attempts at self-acquittal are secretly a summons to the mercy seat.

And there is a deeper mystery still in how he sees himself. He wants to know how God sees him. Scripture answers with staggering intimacy: we are “accepted in the Beloved” (Ephesians 1:6). The Father does not look at the believer and see a man on probation; He sees him “in Christ,” clothed in a righteousness not his own. The high priest Joshua stood before the angel in filthy garments while Satan accused him, and the word came, “Take away the filthy garments from him… See, I have removed your iniquity from you, and I will clothe you with rich robes” (Zechariah 3:4). My brother is standing in that courtroom, hearing the accuser, and the Lord is reaching for clean garments while my brother is still trying to scrub the old ones.

So I confess what I saw in myself as I listened. I told him, “I know what you’re talking about,” and I meant it — because I too have flogged the wrong man, mistaking self-punishment for repentance. I too have lain awake with a mind that would not stop, sensing the Spirit pushing me toward something I could not yet name. Hearing my brother name it aloud pulled it into the light for me as well. That is why, when the call ended, the prayer that came was not “help him” but “thank You for the miraculous work in Samuel, and in me.” The Spirit used his confession to expose and heal my own.

This is the communal shape of it. My brother said this study would be “useful for both of us,” that we are going through it in very different ways but must both embody the new self in this new chapter. He is right, and it is a picture of how the Body actually grows — not by each man privately achieving his identity, but by brothers carrying one another’s questions until the light breaks. He offered to write down notes so he could word it better; I offered him any time on my day off. That mutual yielding of schedule and pride and privacy is itself the new creation at work, the καινός (kai-NOSS) man visible in ordinary Sunday logistics.

And so what began as a phone call about when to meet became a small revelation of the whole gospel: a man exhausted, honest, held hostage by a captor already crucified, reaching not for self-help but for the face of God, and finding a brother reaching alongside him. The Bible study has not even happened yet, and already the Spirit has been teaching before either of us opened a book.

## Implications and Challenges

**On the counterfeit of self-forgiveness.** My brother named forgiving himself as part of what he needs, and I honor the ache beneath it — but the Spirit presses me to say gently that Scripture never commands us to forgive ourselves. Modern Western Christianity has absorbed this phrase from therapeutic culture and treated it as gospel, when in truth it seats the self on a throne only God may occupy. The reason he cannot manufacture that peace is a mercy, not a failure. The gospel offers something far better than self-acquittal: the blood-bought verdict of the Judge Himself, “no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus” (Romans 8:1). To reckon that true is harder and holier than forgiving oneself, and it actually holds.

**On disciplining the wrong man.** He said he is hard on himself in all the wrong ways, and that line deserves to be honored as genuine spiritual discernment. There is a severity that looks like holiness but is actually the flesh punishing the flesh — a corpse being beaten while the new man goes unfed. The challenge here confronts a strain of Christian earnestness that mistakes self-loathing for humility and constant self-flagellation for repentance. True paideia comes from the Father’s hand and produces “the peaceable fruit of righteousness” (Hebrews 12:11), not despair. Where our severity produces only exhaustion and shame, we may be sure it is not from God.

**On identity as reception, not achievement.** My brother wants to “embody” the new self, and the desire is righteous, but the ordering matters immensely. The καινός (kai-NOSS) man is received before he is embodied; we walk out what has been declared, we do not walk our way into a declaration. This challenges the self-improvement gospel that quietly permeates our churches, where sanctification becomes a self-help program with Scripture verses. We are not building a better self; we are learning to reckon as dead a self already crucified and to inhabit a self already made alive. The whole weight rests on Christ’s finished work, not our becoming.

**On the sleepless night as secret place, not failure.** He apologized for waking late, grieved missing the gathering, and half-lamented the night too warm to sleep — yet he also said, “it just means time in the secret place.” I want to affirm that instinct against the guilt clinging to it. The Lord met Jacob in a night of wrestling and Paul in the darkness of a Damascus road; the sleeplessness my brother endured while the Spirit “pushed him toward something” may be the very anteroom of his blessing. Western Christianity often measures faithfulness by attendance and productivity, but the secret place is not less holy than the sanctuary. God is not disappointed with the man who missed church while lying awake seeking Him.

**On the Body as the place of formation.** He said this study would serve us both, and offered to prepare notes so he could word it better — a small picture of how identity is forged in fellowship, not isolation. This challenges the privatized faith so normalized among us, where each believer wrestles alone and calls it maturity. The new man is revealed as brothers carry one another’s confessions into the light. Where we hide our struggle, the accuser thrives; where we speak it plainly to a trusted brother, the filthy garments come off. We were never meant to become new creatures in solitary confinement.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

Maturity here means learning to distinguish the voice of godly conviction from the voice of fleshly condemnation, and refusing to punish a man God has already crucified. It means we stop trying to feel our way into a new identity and start reckoning true what heaven has already declared over us in Christ. When my brother says he is hard on himself in the wrong ways, the mature response is not to soften into self-indulgence nor harden into self-loathing, but to receive the Father’s discipline as a son and the Father’s acceptance as a gift. We grow up not by becoming better versions of the old man, but by putting him off as an obsolete garment and putting on the καινός (kai-NOSS) man created according to God in true righteousness and holiness.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

The obedience this calls forth is the obedience of reckoning — the daily, deliberate act of counting ourselves dead to sin and alive to God, agreeing with the verdict of the cross even when our feelings dissent. It calls my brother, and me, to stop scrubbing the filthy garments and instead let the Lord clothe us in the robes He is already extending. It calls us to keep the appointment, to open the Word together, to bring our honest exhaustion into the secret place rather than performing a strength we do not have. And it calls us to carry each other’s questions, since obedience in the Kingdom is never a solitary act.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

To abide prophetically is to watch for what the Spirit is doing in the sleepless night, in the missed gathering, in the raw confession of a brother on the phone — and to name it faithfully rather than reach for the flesh’s easy remedies. It is to discern that the past holding a man hostage is a captor already defeated, and to testify to that finished victory until faith catches up with fact. It trains us to wait upon revelation rather than manufacture identity, to yield our schedules and our pride so the Body can be built, and to speak the Lord’s verdict over one another when the accuser is loud. The prophet does not affirm the old man; he announces the new one into being with the word already given from heaven.

## A Closing Prayer

Lord Jesus, I thank You for the miraculous work You have already begun in Samuel and in me, and I bring this to You not as a beggar at the gate but as a son standing inside the finished work of Your cross. You crucified the old man; You did not ask me to. You spoke the new creation into being; You did not ask me to construct it. So I lay down every attempt to forgive myself, every effort to flog the man You have already put to death, and I reckon myself dead to sin and alive to God in You. For my brother, I intercede as one servant for another: take away the filthy garments the accuser keeps pointing to, and clothe him in the rich robes You have prepared, that he may see himself as You see him — accepted in the Beloved, hidden in Christ, sealed by the Spirit. Meet him in the sleepless nights as You met Jacob at the Jabbok, and let him walk away with a new name and a blessed limp. Teach us both to embody what You have declared rather than to strive for what we already possess. Guard the study we are about to share; let Your Word interpret our lives and not the reverse. I make agreement with Your will over us — that the παλαιός man is finished and the καινός man is ours — and I seal this prayer with thanksgiving between the heavens and the earth. In the name of Jesus Christ, who is Himself our righteousness, amen.
