# 07-09 Call Reflection: Sanctification, Identity, and God’s Faithfulness in Weakness **Title:** 07-09 at 10:12 — The Blue Flame and the Heartbeat: On Sanctification, Identity, and the God Who Holds What We Cannot **Recorded:** 2026-07-09 10:12:43 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother walk through the wreckage of a night he could not fully remember, and a morning that met him with a cruelty he had not anticipated, something in me recognized the deeper architecture beneath the chaos — the ancient pattern of a man being confronted simultaneously by his own weakness and the faithfulness of a God who refuses to abandon the work He has started. The conversation moved the way real life moves: jagged, honest, sometimes funny, sometimes devastating, and underneath it all, surprisingly saturated with the things of heaven. What arrested me most was not the pain or the mess, but the moment my brother said, *above all else, crave proximity with Christ* — because that word did not come from a sermon or a study; it came through the bruising of a very hard night. **Why This Matters:** The Body of Christ in this generation is filled with people who know the language of salvation but have never been introduced to the long, quiet, often unglamorous work of sanctification. We celebrate the moment of crossing the threshold and then leave the saints standing in the doorway, unsure what to do next. What emerged in this conversation was something I believe the Spirit pressed through the rawness of real relationship: that the life of faith is not a single dramatic transformation but a heartbeat — rhythmic, oscillating, sometimes frighteningly irregular — and that the God who saves is also the God who sanctifies, and He is faithful to finish what He started. This matters for every believer who has ever fallen, gotten back up, fallen again, and wondered whether the falling disqualifies them from the getting-back-up. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation A natural reading of this conversation would reduce it to something fairly familiar: two friends sorting through the emotional wreckage of bad decisions, offering each other some encouraging words before going back to their lives. The world would categorize my brother’s struggle as a behavioral problem — poor sleep, substance use, unresolved relational trauma, unhealthy coping mechanisms — and would prescribe accordingly: better habits, better boundaries, perhaps some therapy and a nutritionist. It would acknowledge the emotional pain of the Instagram incident as a normal consequence of a broken relationship and would encourage him to focus on himself, work on his patterns, and eventually arrive at some version of stability through sustained personal effort. The theological conversation woven through the call would be noted, perhaps appreciated, but ultimately filed under “personal belief” — meaningful to the individuals involved, but finally reducible to the psychological comfort that religious frameworks sometimes provide for people under stress. The world is not cruel in this reading; it is simply limited to what it can measure. **The Limitation:** The difficulty is that the natural mind, however well-intentioned, cannot perceive that the oscillation my brother described — the bright orange flame and the small, intensely focused blue one — is not a mood cycle or a behavioral pattern but a spiritual reality with theological substance. It cannot see that the moment of cruelty he woke up to was not merely relational injury but a kind of wilderness confrontation, the sort that has always preceded deeper formation in the lives of those God is actively shaping. What looks to the natural eye like a man barely holding himself together is, in the light of Scripture, a man being held — and being slowly, patiently, one step at a time, conformed into something he did not have the capacity to manufacture for himself. The natural mind cannot hold salvation and sanctification in the same hand without collapsing one into the other, and that collapse is precisely where the confusion lives. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sat with this conversation afterward, what first surfaced was the simple, almost startling honesty with which my brother named his own brokenness. *Only through Christ am I clean. Me — I am actually completely fucked up.* That is not self-flagellation; that is, in the most precise theological sense, the confession of a man who has begun to understand what the Apostle Paul meant when he wrote in Galatians 2:20, *“I have been crucified with Christ; it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me.”* The Greek verb there is συσταυρόω (systauroō, soos-tow-ROH-oh) — to be co-crucified, jointly nailed to the same cross — and it carries the weight of a completed action with ongoing implications. My brother was not being self-deprecating. He was, perhaps without knowing the vocabulary, articulating the fundamental premise of sanctification: that the self which must be surrendered has already been put to death in Christ, and the life now being lived is not the old life dressed up but a genuinely new life, authored from outside the self. This is where the conversation about emotion and the image of God became, for me, unexpectedly weighty. When my brother asked why God gave us such a spectrum of emotion, I found myself saying what I genuinely believe: *He feels things too.* We are made in His image — and that image, בְּצֶלֶם אֱלֹהִים (bətselem ʾĕlōhîm, beh-TSEH-lem eh-loh-HEEM) — in the image of God — is not merely intellectual or moral but affective. The God of Scripture grieves (Genesis 6:6), delights (Zephaniah 3:17), is moved with compassion (Matthew 9:36), and burns with holy jealousy (Exodus 20:5). Our emotional capacity is not a flaw to be managed but a reflection of the One who fashioned us. The problem is not that my brother feels deeply; the problem is that without the anchoring of the Spirit, feeling deeply becomes exposure to every wind that moves — and the enemy knows exactly which winds to send. What struck me most about my brother’s description of the two flames was how accurately it mapped onto something the Scripture does not often name with that image but consistently assumes: the difference between what we generate in ourselves by spiritual enthusiasm and what the Spirit sustains in us by grace when we have nothing left of our own to burn. The large orange flame — warm, bright, fruit-bearing, visible — is what the life in the Spirit looks like when circumstances are aligned and energy is available. But the small, almost invisible blue flame — the one that burns hotter, more precisely, with a kind of laser focus — that is something else. I recognized in that image the still small voice of 1 Kings 19:12, the sound that came not in the wind, not in the earthquake, not in the fire, but after all of those — קוֹל דְּמָמָה דַקָּה (qôl dəmāmāh daqqāh, KOLE deh-mah-MAH dak-KAH) — a sound of thin silence, a still small voice. Elijah was at his most depleted when that voice spoke most clearly. My brother was not describing spiritual failure when he described the blue flame. He was describing the conditions under which God tends to do His most concentrated work. The theological thread that kept weaving itself through our exchange was the relationship between salvation and sanctification — and I kept returning to it because it is one of the most practically misunderstood distinctions in the Western church. Salvation is received; it is the gift that Ephesians 2:8 names with precision: *“For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God.”* The Greek word for grace there is χάρις (charis, KAH-rees) — unmerited, freely given favor — and it is entirely God’s initiative. But sanctification is the ongoing work of being made holy — ἁγιασμός (hagiasmos, hah-gee-as-MOSS) — the process by which the χάρις (KAH-rees) of salvation is worked out through the cooperation of the human will with the divine Spirit. You can be delivered and still not be liberated. You can cross the Red Sea and still die in the wilderness. Israel was saved out of Egypt, but it took forty years and an entirely new generation to enter the land — not because God changed His mind, but because sanctification requires the sustained death of the habits, fears, and identities formed under slavery. My brother named his patterns plainly: bad habits, addiction, the cycle of two steps forward and five steps back. And I want to be careful here not to spiritually bypass what is also a genuinely physical and practical struggle — exhaustion, poor nutrition, substance use, unresolved grief. But I also cannot pretend that these things exist in isolation from the spiritual realities he himself named in the same breath. Paul addresses this directly in Romans 7 — the very letter we spoke about — when he writes, *“For what I will to do, that I do not practice; but what I hate, that I do”* (Romans 7:15). This is not a confession of spiritual failure; it is a diagnosis of the warfare between the flesh and the Spirit that every believer who is actually growing will encounter. The person who never feels this tension is almost certainly not moving toward anything. And yet the answer Paul arrives at is not self-improvement. It is *“O wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death? I thank God — through Jesus Christ our Lord!”* (Romans 7:24–25). The deliverance comes not from resolving the tension but from recognizing who stands on the other side of it. This is what I was pressing toward when I told my brother: it is His work. Yes, you have a role — your obedience matters, your choices matter, the small steps matter — but the transformation itself is authored by the One who began it. Philippians 1:6 is not a vague encouragement; it is a covenantal promise: *“He who has begun a good work in you will complete it until the day of Jesus Christ.”* The verb ἐπιτελέω (epiteleō, ep-ee-teh-LEH-oh) — to complete, to bring fully to its intended end — is not contingent on the consistency of your effort. It is contingent on the faithfulness of the One who started it. The image I offered — of the heartbeat monitor — landed badly in the moment, and I will not pretend it landed gracefully. But the truth underneath it holds: sanctification is not a flat line of consistent upward progress. The EKG of a living heart is not a straight line moving diagonally upward; it is a pattern of peaks and descents, of systole and diastole, of contraction and release. The straight line on the monitor is the line of death. The irregular, sometimes alarming, sometimes irregular rhythm is the evidence of life. A righteous man falls seven times and rises again (Proverbs 24:16) — and the rising is not optional, but neither is the falling shameful when the rising follows. This is not a permission slip for recklessness; it is a description of what actual sanctification looks like from the inside when you are honest about it. What my brother said near the end of the conversation was perhaps the most spiritually precise thing spoken in the entire exchange: *I haven’t stood up on my own in a long time.* He framed it almost apologetically, as though it were an admission of weakness. But I heard it as the beginning of wisdom. The Greek word for weakness in Paul’s vocabulary is ἀσθένεια (astheneia, as-THEH-nee-ah) — and it appears most powerfully in 2 Corinthians 12:9–10, where God says to Paul, *“My grace is sufficient for you, for My strength is made perfect in weakness.”* The word translated “perfect” there is τελειόω (teleioō, teh-lay-OH-oh) — to be brought to its intended completion, its telos. God’s strength does not merely tolerate our weakness; it finds its completeness in it. The man who has not stood up on his own in a long time is not failing. He is, in a real and scripturally grounded sense, in the most fertile ground for the kind of standing that does not depend on his own legs. I also want to sit for a moment with the thing my brother named about worship and motivation — the clarification he was working toward about *why* we glorify God. He was reaching for something he could not quite articulate, and I offered a simplification: it is not so you can be loved; it is *because* you are loved. That distinction is not semantic; it is the difference between religion and relationship, between performance and response, between the law and the gospel. The Hebrew concept of hesed (חֶסֶד, hesed, HEH-sed) — covenantal lovingkindness, steadfast mercy — is not a reward distributed to the obedient. It is the posture of God toward His people before, during, and after their faithfulness or unfaithfulness. Our worship is the echo of His prior movement toward us. We love because He first loved (1 John 4:19). We do not generate devotion in order to earn His proximity; we draw near because He has already torn the veil, already closed the distance, already seated us in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus (Ephesians 2:6). The craving for proximity with Christ that my brother received was not a new religious obligation; it was the Spirit pressing toward what is already true. Finally, what I noticed in the closing moments of our conversation was something my brother said almost as an aside, in the middle of his exhaustion and physical disorientation: *Every morning I wake up, I thank God. Genuinely.* In the midst of a forty-eight-hour stretch of labor, four hours of sleep, relational devastation, physical symptoms, and the wreckage of the night before — in the middle of all of that — gratitude. Not manufactured gratitude, not performance gratitude, but the simple, daily acknowledgment that waking up is a gift. This is, in its way, a form of the practice Paul describes in 1 Thessalonians 5:18 — giving thanks in everything — and it is not a small thing to do it from an air mattress in the middle of a season of loss. It is, in fact, one of the most counter-cultural and spiritually formative habits available to any person in any season: to meet the morning with *thank you* before the day has given you a reason. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge I feel the Spirit pressing through this conversation is the one most Western church culture consistently avoids: **we must stop preaching salvation without sanctification, and we must stop confusing the two.** When the church celebrates the new convert and then provides no framework for the long, unsexy work of becoming holy — no theology of suffering, no understanding of the dark night, no honest reckoning with the habitual patterns that do not dissolve at the altar — we leave people stranded between two worlds. They know they are saved, but they do not know what to do with the fact that they keep sinning. The result is either false assurance that nothing needs to change, or false guilt that convinces them they were never really saved at all. Neither is the gospel. The second challenge is the church’s chronic inability — and this is what I was pressing toward in our exchange about Calvinism — **to hold things in paradox.** You are saved by grace through faith, *and* faith without works is dead. God’s sovereignty is absolute, *and* your obedience genuinely matters. These are not contradictions to be resolved; they are tensions to be inhabited. Denominational rigidity tends to collapse the tension in one direction or the other — either so emphasizing grace that obedience becomes optional, or so emphasizing obedience that grace becomes conditional. The canon does not allow either collapse. Both things are true. Holding them in productive tension is not theological weakness; it is theological maturity. The third challenge is more personal and perhaps more uncomfortable: **many of us have never truly received our identity in Christ.** My brother’s reflexive self-condemnation — *I’m not good; I’m not doing well* — is understandable given what he was walking through. But it reveals a deeper root: the habit of measuring one’s standing before God by one’s current performance rather than by the finished work of the cross. Galatians 2:20 is not a verse for the spiritually advanced; it is the baseline identity of every person in Christ. *It is no longer I who live.* The “I” that must be stopped from holding everything, judged, weighed, and found wanting — that I has already been crucified. The task is not to improve it but to stop identifying with it. The fourth challenge is the role of the body in spiritual formation — **a theme the conversation embodied simply by happening.** My brother said something that stopped me: he had been comparing answers, lining up what different faithful people said, watching for convergence. That is not naivety; that is the ancient instinct of the Body of Christ operating as designed. In 1 Corinthians 12, the Spirit distributes gifts *to each one* for the profit of *all* (v. 7). No single member holds the whole counsel. The murmuration — the collective movement of the Spirit through many lives simultaneously — is how the Body discerns, corrects, confirms, and strengthens. The institutional church in the West has largely replaced this with platforms and personalities, and the result is dependency on a single voice rather than the organic, distributed confirmation that comes when the same Spirit speaks through multiple people across distance, denomination, and circumstance. The fifth challenge is about **the theology of the body — the physical — as a site of spiritual warfare and spiritual formation.** My brother’s physical struggles — exhaustion, substance use, dietary neglect — are not separate from his spiritual life. Paul writes in 1 Corinthians 6:19–20 that the body is the temple of the Holy Spirit and that we are to glorify God *in* our bodies. The discipline of the body — sleep, nourishment, sobriety, physical care — is not vanity; it is stewardship of the vessel through which the Spirit moves. At the same time, the grace extended here must be genuine: the habits that grip most tightly are not evidence of weak willpower but often of deep wounds that have been medicated rather than healed. The Spirit tends to work on these one at a time, in the order He chooses, and patience — both with God’s timing and with one’s own progress — is not passivity. It is faithfulness in the waiting. A final grace-shaped challenge: **gratitude is a discipline before it is a feeling.** The fact that my brother, in the depths of a very dark forty-eight hours, was waking up and genuinely thanking God is more spiritually significant than it might appear. That practice, sustained and deepened, is one of the most powerful antidotes available to the patterns of anxiety, self-condemnation, and spiritual numbness that threaten every serious disciple. It is worth naming, worth affirming, worth building upon — because the person who can thank God from an air mattress in the middle of loss is already practicing something that many comfortable Christians have never learned. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This conversation calls me — calls us — to develop an honest, scripturally grounded theology of the long middle. Not the dramatic beginning of faith, and not the eschatological completion of it, but the years and decades in between, where the patterns are slow to change and the Spirit is nevertheless faithful. Maturity is not the absence of falling; it is the development of the habit of rising — and the growing recognition that the rising itself is not self-generated but Spirit-sustained. It also calls us to hold our own identity firmly in what Christ has declared rather than what our current behavior confirms. The gap between who we are in Christ and who we are in practice is not an indictment; it is the precise territory in which sanctification operates. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this calls forth is not primarily dramatic. It is the obedience of the small step — the face washed, the water drunk, the rest taken, the gratitude spoken in the morning. It is the obedience of returning to the Word when the flame feels low. It is the obedience of staying in community, of allowing the Spirit to speak through brothers and sisters, of remaining in conversation with God even when the conversation feels one-sided or silent. And it is the obedience of accepting the full scope of what Christ has done — not only the forgiveness of sin but the completed crucifixion of the old self — and learning to stop managing and defending the very self that has already been given over. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this calls forth is the posture of a witness who can say with credibility: *I have been battered and I have not been destroyed; I have fallen and I have been raised; I have not stood up on my own, and I am not ashamed of it.* That testimony — not of a man who has arrived, but of a man who keeps getting up — is one of the most needed testimonies in the present hour. The saints are not looking for people who have never struggled. They are looking for people who have struggled and found that God was faithful in it. That is the prophetic word this conversation carries. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who has also been in the middle of the long work — who has had to learn, and is still learning, that the surrender is not a single moment but a daily, sometimes hourly, returning. I thank You for my brother, for the rawness and honesty of what You allowed me to witness and to speak into, and for the way Your Spirit moved through the mess of a very human conversation to press real truth into both of our hearts. I ask that the work You have begun in him be completed — not according to his timeline or mine, but according to Yours — and I ask that every chain of habit, every pattern formed in pain, every identity built on performance rather than sonship, be broken by the same Spirit that raised Christ from the dead, who now lives in him. Let the blue flame burn. Let the still small voice be the loudest thing in the room. And let us both grow in the grace and knowledge of Jesus Christ — not because we are holding ourselves together, but because we have finally agreed to stop. To You be all glory, all honor, all authority, in the name of the One who declared it finished — and meant every word of it.
