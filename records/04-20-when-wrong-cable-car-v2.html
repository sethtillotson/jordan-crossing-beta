# 04-20 Personal Meditation: Theology of the Ordinary — Sacred Encounters in a San Francisco Day **Title:** 04-20 at 11:21 — When the Wrong Cable Car Leads to the Right Table: The Sacred Hidden in the Ordinary, the Bent Made Straight **Recorded:** 2026-04-20 11:21:57 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Personal Meditation ## Opening Observation I am sitting with three distinct but inseparable threads from a single day in San Francisco — a day I was not fully present to as a spiritual event while it was happening, yet which has since refused to release me. The first thread is the sheer, unguarded texture of ordinary human life captured mid-breath: laughter, rain, cable cars, ducklings, Chinatown, a Walgreens, four people trying to fit into a selfie. The second thread is the roundabout journey itself — the wrong cable car, the closed restaurants, the canceled Uber, the wet shoes — and the moment someone named all of it as blessing with a phrase that carries the weight of Moriah: *Yehovah Yair, the Lord who blesses has blessed us.* The third thread is the one that surfaced almost accidentally, walking through parts of the city the tourist maps do not linger on — when the bent bodies we passed on those streets called up, through a companion’s casual observation, the woman in Luke 13 who had been bound by a spirit of infirmity for eighteen years. Three threads from one day, and the Spirit will not let me treat any of them as coincidental or separate. **Why This Matters:** It matters because the modern Western Church has quietly accepted a theology of the ordinary that impoverishes everything it touches. We have consigned sacred encounter to sanctuaries and structured devotionals, blamed the roundabout for being the roundabout, and learned to avert our eyes from the bent and broken on our city streets — filing each of these under the category of *spiritually irrelevant*. But the whole testimony of the canon runs the other direction. The burning bush was in the wilderness while a man was doing his job. Yehovah Yireh was named on a mountain where nothing had gone according to plan. The woman Jesus loosed was not in a revival meeting; she was simply present in the room where He was teaching. If the Church does not recover the capacity to find God in the rain, to name blessing inside the chaos, and to see the captive through covenant eyes rather than social-services eyes, we will keep walking past burning bushes, past Moriaths, and past daughters of Abraham with our sandals still on — because we never thought to look down. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, encountering the raw material of this day, produces three separate verdicts with practiced efficiency. It looks at the ordinary ambient noise — the laughter, the selfie, the cable car debates, the rain — and files it under *irrelevant private chatter*, the kind of content that passes no meaningful filter. It looks at the roundabout journey — the wrong transit line, the closed doors, the soaked shoes, the hunger — and labels it *unfortunate inconvenience*, a charming travel mishap whose meaning begins only when the meal finally arrives. And it looks at the bent and hollowed-out bodies in the Tenderloin and in Kensington, Pennsylvania, and reaches immediately for the language of policy and public health: opioid crisis, pharmaceutical negligence, poverty cycles, systemic failure, harm reduction. These analyses are not wrong as far as they reach. They describe real conditions with real causes. The natural mind at its most compassionate produces organizations, documentaries, needle exchanges, and awareness campaigns. It reframes the addict as victim, which is partly true and partly merciful. It celebrates the meal when the restaurant finally opens, and it calls that the blessing. **The Limitation:** The difficulty is that the natural mind can only perceive meaning where meaning has been visibly labeled — and it can only address bondage at the depth it is able to see. It searches for the sacred by searching for the elevated, and it treats inconvenience as prologue to be skipped rather than as the terrain where something is being formed. But there is a dimension of each of these three threads that lies entirely beyond the reach of natural analysis: the Spirit does not require a label to work, laughter over a one-legged duck is not beneath divine notice, the roundabout is not the unfortunate preface to the real story, and the bodies bent at strange angles in our city streets are not merely victims of chemical chemistry — they are, in an older and more terrible sense, *captive*, bound by something that neither naloxone nor policy reform can address at its root. The flesh reads a transcript and finds noise; the Spirit reads lives — the laughing, the wet, the hungry, the bent — and finds the fingerprints of a God who is already present and already working, whether anyone in the recording knew it or not. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I want to begin where the day began — not with the theological weight, but with the laughter, because I believe the Spirit has pressed me to honor it before I go anywhere else. The opening moments of that recording are simply people laughing: at ducklings, at a one-legged duck that someone said was “just showing off,” at someone nearly dropping their phone, at the rain coming harder than expected. The Church has sometimes been so focused on forming people into sobriety of spirit that we have quietly made laughter suspect, as though joy and holiness occupy opposite ends of a spectrum. But Ecclesiastes 3:4 grants laughter its own appointed season, and Psalm 2:4 imagines the Lord Himself laughing — not cruelly, but from the posture of One who holds all things and is not undone by any of them. Galatians 5:22 names joy as καρπός (karpos, KAR-pos) — fruit of the Spirit, not manufactured sentiment. What I heard in those opening minutes was not spiritual laughter reported from a revival meeting. It was the ordinary, unguarded, completely human laughter of people together, and it was no less a fruit of the Spirit for being ordinary. Something else arrests me in those early moments: the selfie. Four people trying to get into one frame, the phone nearly falling, several attempts, the final triumphant “there we go.” It is a small thing, and I do not want to spiritualize it past its own natural beauty. But there is something in the impulse to capture a moment together — to say, *we were here, in this, together, and we want evidence of it* — that reflects something genuinely and irreducibly human. Genesis 1:26 tells us the image-bearing declaration was spoken by a community: “Let Us make man in Our image.” The word צֶלֶם (tselem, TSEL-em) — image, representation — carries within it the relational character of the One in whose likeness it is borne. The Trinity does not exist in isolated divine selfhood. The Father, Son, and Spirit exist in eternal mutual beholding, eternal mutual glorification, eternal love given and returned. And the creature made in that image reaches for its own imperfect version of this — in the rain, laughing, with a phone that nearly falls — but reaching nonetheless. The selfie, absurd and glorious, is downstream of the Trinity. The rain enters the transcript in a way I find quietly instructive on multiple levels. It was not welcomed — the group scrambled, someone noted it was raining hard, the cable car plan was disrupted — and yet the rain came anyway, interrupting, reshaping, pressing the group toward shelter and toward one another. In Hebrew thought, מָטָר (māṭār, mah-TAHR) — rain — carries the weight of divine provision and divine interruption both. Joel 2:23 speaks of the early and latter rain as covenantal promise. James 5:7 frames the farmer waiting for the early and latter rain as the model for patient faith. Matthew 5:45 places rain beyond human scheduling: the Father sends it on the just and the unjust alike. The rain that fell on the cable car outing did not consult the schedule, did not ask permission, and insists that the creature reckon with something larger than its own logistics. There is a word here for the planning culture of the contemporary Church — a culture that has quietly declared war on divine interruption by mistaking unbroken execution for spiritual alignment. The theological pivot of everything I heard that day arrives in one nearly-laughed-away sentence: *“Yehovah Yair — the Lord who blesses has blessed us. Roundabout trips, closed businesses, umbrellas not found in stores on rainy days. Such blessings.”* I keep returning to this. It is not spiritual bypassing. It is not the pretense that the hunger was not real, or that the wet shoes were not wet, or that the frustration at closed door after closed door was not felt. What it is — what it unmistakably is — is a direct echo of Abrahamic faith. The compound names of God in the Hebrew Scriptures did not emerge in the palace. They emerged in the crisis. יְהוָה יִרְאֶה (Yehovah Yireh, yeh-ho-VAH yir-EH) — “The LORD will provide,” literally “The LORD will be seen” — was the name Abraham gave to Moriah *after* the long walk up, the altar built, the knife raised, and the voice from heaven intervening at the last possible moment. Abraham did not know what God would do when he began climbing. He knew only that the Lord would see to it, and he climbed on that basis. What is striking is that in the recording from that San Francisco street, the same spirit of naming God in the midst of apparent failure — not after the resolution, not when the restaurant finally opened, but in the middle of the wet and the hungry and the closed — is a participation in that same Abrahamic posture. Faith that confesses blessing only after confirmed outcomes is not faith; it is grateful accounting. But faith that names the Lord’s goodness while still soaking wet is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not yet seen (Hebrews 11:1). Psalm 23 does not begin with the still waters. It begins with the shepherd and the acknowledgment that the valley of the shadow of death is part of the journey, not an interruption of it. The table prepared in the presence of enemies is not set in a comfortable hall — it is set in the middle of the hostile terrain, before the enemies leave, not after. I am sitting with the Filipino restaurant that finally opened its door and the conversation that unfolded inside it, and I see this image made incarnate: a tired, wet, hungry family walking into a space where someone who had known her own hardship — surrendered a store, waited for payment from workers who came to her bus stop, navigated the cultural complexity of cooks who would not receive correction — had kept cooking. The table was not found in the aftermath of perfect planning. It was found at the end of a roundabout, and it held more than food. It held testimony. It held the kind of κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-noh-NEE-ah) — fellowship, participation in shared life, mutual contribution of story — that Acts 2:42 describes as one of the four things the early believers devoted themselves to. κοινωνία (koy-noh-NEE-ah) is not primarily social warmth. Its root is κοινός (koinos) — *common*. It is what happens when lives touch and share what they carry. The Holy Spirit does not need an ideal setting to produce real fellowship. He seems, across the entire canon, to prefer the unexpected ones. Romans 8:28 is often quoted as reassurance, but I want to press into the Greek: συνεργέω (synergeō, sin-er-GEH-oh) — cooperative working, multiple elements functioning together toward a unified result. “All things work together for good” is not passive good luck. It is active divine cooperation among every element — the wrong cable car, the rain, the closed doors, the canceled Uber, the shared table, the restaurateur’s testimony, the wet walk, the extra rice — cooperating under the sovereign hand of God toward something neither logistically planned nor accidentally stumbled into. The small phrase that emerged near the end of the recording lands with the weight of a compressed theology of providence: *“Nothing about this trip has been logistically correct. But it all works out.”* Two sentences. συνεργέω (sin-er-GEH-oh) in miniature. Purposefully woven. The synergy of the divine kind. Then there is the moment the day pressed into darker and more theologically urgent territory. Walking through parts of the city the tourist maps do not linger on, past vape shops and through streets that carry their own kind of grief, my companion drew a thread from the street directly into the Gospel of Luke without preparing to. The bent bodies we passed — what my companion called the Fedi walk, the fentanyl stance, the hollowed-out posture of those who have been chemically captive long enough that the captivity has become their entire architecture — surfaced a spontaneous comparison: *“Kind of like that lady in the Bible, Jesus heals. She’s bent over for like eighteen years.”* That was not a sermon illustration. It was not a rhetorical flourish. It was an involuntary recognition, the kind that only happens when the Word has already been deposited in a person deeply enough that the world around them becomes legible through it. And the Spirit will not let me pass it by. Luke 13 is precise in a way that demands attention. Jesus is teaching in the synagogue on the Sabbath. A woman is present who has had a πνεῦμα ἀσθενείας (pneuma astheneias, PNYOO-mah as-THEN-ay-as) — a spirit of infirmity — for eighteen years. She is bent double and cannot straighten herself at all. Jesus sees her. He does not wait for her to come forward; the text does not say she called out to Him. He is teaching, she is present, and He *saw* her — and called her to Himself. He lays His hands on her and speaks: *“Woman, you are loosed from your infirmity”* (Luke 13:12, NKJV). The word for *loosed* is λύω (lyō, LOO-oh) — to unbind, release, unfasten. She is immediately made straight: ἀνορθόθη (anorthōthē, ah-nor-THO-thay), aorist passive indicative — the action was instantaneous, complete, and done *to* her by an external agent. She did not gradually improve. She was made straight. And the theological axis on which the entire healing turns is the phrase Jesus uses when challenged about the timing: *“So ought not this woman, being a daughter of Abraham, whom Satan has bound — think of it — for eighteen years, be loosed from this bond on the Sabbath?”* (Luke 13:16, NKJV). He names her covenant identity before He names her condition. She had not stopped being a daughter of Abraham during eighteen years of being bent. The bondage had obscured it, pressed it down toward the earth, made it invisible to everyone around her — perhaps even to herself. But Jesus sees through the posture to the person, and He names her prior identity as the theological basis for the healing, not the other way around. I am pressing into this because the bent bodies we walked past in San Francisco — the bodies my companion described from Kensington, Pennsylvania — are not merely victims of chemical chemistry, though the chemistry is real and the neurological damage is real and the poverty and trauma pathways are real. Ephesians 6:12 names what lies beneath and through all of it: our struggle is not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the κοσμοκράτωρ (kosmokratōr, koz-mo-KRAH-tore) — the world-rulers of this present darkness. The fentanyl is the visible instrument. The spiritual architecture of captivity beneath it is what the natural mind, no matter how compassionate, is not equipped to see — and therefore cannot address at its foundation. Isaiah 61:1, the passage Jesus quotes in His home synagogue to announce His own mission, uses a word for liberty that carries Jubilee weight: דְּרוֹר (derôr, deh-ROAR) — the specific liberty of the Year of Jubilee, the appointed release of every captive, the return of each person to their inheritance. This is not a social program Jesus is announcing. He is naming His own Spirit-anointed mission. And the commission He gives His disciples in John 20:21 uses the word καθώς (kathōs, kah-HOCE) — *exactly as, in the same manner* — the Father sent Him, He sends us. The commission is not a reduced symbolic version of His mission. It is a direct extension of it in the same mode and authority. What stops me here is not the analysis. It is the image of what happened to the woman in Luke 13 when she was made straight. She had been facing the ground for eighteen years. When the λύω (LOO-oh) of Christ released her — when ἀνορθόθη (ah-nor-THO-thay) happened *to* her — she suddenly saw the full height of the room, the face of the One who had called her forward, the ceiling of the synagogue, the sky beyond it. The axis of her entire perception shifted in an instant. That is the image the Spirit will not release me from when I think about the bent bodies in those streets: not that Jesus healed a woman long ago, but that the release He spoke is still moving through human vessels He has commissioned, filled, and sent — and that the daughter of Abraham in Kensington has not stopped being a daughter of Abraham, and the son of Adam frozen on a San Francisco sidewalk has not stopped being someone for whom Christ died. The question is not whether God can heal. The canon has answered that irrevocably. The question is whether the Church is willing to be the instrument through which He does — not from the comfort of organized ministry programming, but from the willingness to simply be present in places that require something of us. There is a moment near the end of the recording when someone says, simply and without drama, *“I’m gonna go home now.”* And then again: *“I’m gonna go home now.”* The repetition feels like someone genuinely oriented — not restless, but ready. The outing has been had. The rain has come. The cable car has done its work. The table has been found. And now the direction is home. I hear in this the quiet eschatological pulse that runs beneath all Christian existence. Hebrews 11:13 and 16 describe the great cloud of witnesses as those who “confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth” and “desired a better, that is, a heavenly country.” The mundane ache to return home after a long, wet, roundabout day in the city is a small echo of the deepest ache of the redeemed soul. *Maranatha* — come, Lord Jesus — is not so structurally different from *I’m gonna go home now*. We are a people in motion — through cities, through seasons, through confusion about which transit line to take, through rain and closed restaurants and bent strangers and unexpected tables — and the Lord is present in every turn of it, already preparing the table we have not yet found, already setting the feast we have not yet arrived at. As I close this unfolding of what I heard in and through that day, what the Spirit has most insistently pressed upon me is this: God is not embarrassed by the ordinary, and He is not absent from the roundabout, and He has not forgotten the bent. He is in the laughter over the one-legged duck that was just showing off. He is in the rain that nobody planned for. He is in the phrase spoken mid-chaos — *such blessings* — that named Him correctly before the resolution arrived. He is in the Filipino restaurant that kept cooking through hardship and set a table that held more than a meal. He is in the cable car gripping an underground cable it did not generate, being pulled uphill by power already moving beneath it. He is in the companion who looked at a bent stranger on a San Francisco street and what I said without preparing to: *kind of like that lady in the Bible, Jesus heals.* And He is in the moment someone finally says *I’m gonna go home now* — and means it with everything they are. This is not a lowering of theological standards. This is the Incarnation, extended forward in time — עִמָּנוּאֵל (ʿImmānûʾēl, ih-mah-NOO-el), God-with-us, not merely in Bethlehem but in San Francisco, in the rain, on the wrong cable car, at an unexpected table, on a street where the bent are waiting to be seen and called forward and loosed. ## Implications and Challenges The first challenge this meditation presses upon me is the challenge of *seeing* — and the willingness to let what we see become an interior event rather than a passing discomfort. Jesus saw the woman in Luke 13. The text does not say she came to Him; He was teaching and He *saw* her and called her to Himself. The contemporary Church has cultivated a kind of selective spiritual vision — we see what we are prepared to engage, and we avert our eyes from what would cost us too much to address. The bent and addicted bodies in our cities are, in many cases, quite literally invisible to the comfortable Church. The first act of Kingdom obedience is not a program or an initiative. It is a willingness to actually look — to see as Jesus saw — and to allow the sight of the captive to become intercession, and to allow intercession to become presence, and to allow presence to become the occasion for the λύω (LOO-oh) of God to move through His people. The second challenge is what this day exposes about our theology of circumstance. Much of contemporary Christian culture has quietly equated smooth outcomes with spiritual alignment: open doors mean God is blessing, closed doors mean God is redirecting, and frictionless circumstances mean divine favor is present. But the entire arc of this recording disrupts that equation. The closed restaurants were not the absence of God’s provision — they were the route toward a table that held more than food. The wrong cable car was not evidence of misalignment — it was the vehicle for something that a perfect itinerary could not have produced. We must recover a more honest and more biblical theology of circumstance: one that holds friction, inconvenience, and the roundabout as legitimate and frequent theaters of divine activity, not as problems to be solved before the spiritual part begins. Third, the moment of spontaneous blessing-confession in the recording — *Yehovah Yair, such blessings* spoken in the middle of chaos — exposes a gap in most believers’ daily practice. We tend to reserve thanksgiving for resolution. We treat gratitude as a mood that emerges naturally when circumstances cooperate, rather than as a discipline practiced in the middle of unresolved conditions. But Philippians 4:6 was written from confinement, not from a comfortable afternoon. And 2 Chronicles 20:21–22 records that the singers went *before* the army, praising God for a victory not yet won. The prophetic model of thanksgiving is confession that precedes confirmation. The invitation is to develop the habit — not as performance, but as genuine perception — of naming the Lord’s goodness while still wet, while still hungry, while the door is still closed and the shoes are still soaked. Fourth, I want to sit with the restaurateur’s observation about the Filipino cooks who would not receive correction because they already believed they knew how to cook. She said it plainly: *you’re not good at cooking yet; you still need to learn how to cook properly.* This is a parable for discipleship that I cannot release easily. The ones who have been in their tradition the longest — who carry the most accumulated practice, the most inherited confidence — are sometimes the most resistant to the correction that would actually produce the fruit they want. Spiritual pride is not always dramatic. Sometimes it looks like a cook who won’t adjust seasoning because they learned it from their mother. And the invitation this moment extends is to remain genuinely teachable, to hold our spiritual inheritance with gratitude but without defensiveness, to receive correction as the grace it is rather than the insult it can feel like. Fifth, this meditation challenges me about the ease with which we can name the crisis and return to the hotel. There is a moment in the recording where real suffering is observed at close range — the swollen hands, the zombie-like posture, the bodies frozen in the fentanyl stance — and the right comparison surfaces through a companion’s mouth, and then the conversation moves naturally back toward practical concerns. I am not condemning that movement. That is also simply human. But I notice it, because I think it names something the Church must be honest about: we can be moved, we can make the right identification, we can recognize the spiritual reality at work, and still go back to the hotel. The meditation is only fully faithful when recognition moves from the mind into the hands and feet — when what we have seen becomes the shape of our intercession, and when intercession eventually becomes the shape of our action in the places where the bent are living and dying within arm’s reach. Sixth and finally, I want to name the beauty embedded in the ordinary companionship of that day — and call the Church to recover what it has largely outsourced to programming. The κοινωνία (koy-noh-NEE-ah) of people physically present to one another, navigating the same confusion, sharing the same cold, arguing about the same cable car route, eating from the same table — this is not beside the life of faith. It is not the warm-up before the spiritual content begins. It is, in its most elemental form, the life the Body of Christ was always meant to inhabit. The early believers did not devote themselves to κοινωνία (koy-noh-NEE-ah) because it was efficient or productive. They devoted themselves to it because shared life is the environment in which the Spirit of God moves most freely, and because the creature made in the image of a Triune God cannot be fully human alone. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity is not the achievement of permanent elevation above the ordinary. It is the capacity to find God *within* the ordinary — to carry holy attentiveness into the cable car, the rain, the laughter, the roundabout, the unexpected table, and the streets where the bent are waiting — without either spiritualizing everything artificially or compartmentalizing God away from the mess. Maturity looks like a person who laughs fully at the one-legged duck *and* names the Lord’s goodness in the middle of wet shoes and closed restaurants *and* sees the bent stranger through covenant eyes rather than social-services eyes — without feeling the need to separate those three moments into different categories of existence, because they belong to the same God, the same day, and the same formation. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Kingdom obedience in the face of this day’s testimony means giving thanks before the resolution arrives, naming the Lord in the middle of the mess, and refusing to wait for confirmed outcomes before confessing divine goodness. It means letting the sight of the captive become intercession we carry until it bears fruit in encounter. It means being willing, when the Spirit prompts, to move toward rather than past — to speak rather than observe, to lay hands rather than step around, to hold the identity of the person before God in prayer even when we have no other means of intervention available. And it means receiving the unexpected table — the extra rice, the restaurateur’s testimony, the wet walk that became a shared memory — as the gift it is, from the One who sets tables in the presence of enemies and provides abundantly for those who walk the long way around. **For Prophetic Posture:** Those who would carry a prophetic word in this generation must first learn to read the Lord’s hand in the ordinary — in the wrong cable car, in the rain, in the closed door that becomes an open one two blocks further, in the companion who says without preparing to: *kind of like that lady in the Bible, Jesus heals.* The prophetic gift is not primarily about seeing spectacular things. It is about seeing *truly* — perceiving the weight and purpose in what appears accidental or inconvenient, naming the Lord correctly in the middle of chaos, and refusing to let the condition of the captive be the final word, because the canon that frames all of this ends with every captive released, every bent back made straight, every name restored. The faithful prophetic servant is the one who, at the end of a roundabout day in a rain-soaked city, can say with authority and gratitude: *the Lord was here, and we knew it.* ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as a co-heir of the Kingdom, a servant of the Word, one who stands between the heavens and the earth in agreement with Your will and Your ways — and I bring to You the whole of this day: the laughter over ducklings, the rain nobody planned for, the selfie in the wet, the wrong cable car, the closed restaurants, the canceled Uber, the small and almost-laughed-away confession that You are Yehovah Yireh, that You provide, that You see to it before we begin climbing. I thank You that Your faithfulness is not contingent on our logistics, and that the table is prepared not in the aftermath of perfect planning, but in the presence of our enemies — in the middle of the rain and the hunger and the roundabout and the closed door. Lord Jesus, You are the same yesterday, today, and forever, and the λύω (LOO-oh) that You spoke in the synagogue has not lost its power or its currency. I stand in agreement with what You have already declared: that the captive shall be released, that the bent shall be made straight, that every daughter and son of Abraham shall be loosed from every bond that a spirit of infirmity has fastened upon them — in Kensington, in the Tenderloin, in every street where the bent are waiting, whether they know it or not, for the One who sees them and calls them forward. Let this moment of recognition become intercession. Let intercession become presence. Let presence become the occasion for Your healing hand to move through Your people. Teach us to give thanks in the middle of the unresolved, to kiss in the rain, to name You correctly before the resolution arrives, to see the covenant identity of the person whose posture tells only of their bondage. And let the deep ache of *I’m gonna go home now* be honored in us — not as escape, but as orientation, the compass that keeps us faithful and loving and present in every rain-soaked, roundabout, ordinary, beautiful moment between now and the day Your Son makes all things new. To the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit — all glory, now and to the ages of ages. Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Ecclesiastes 3:4 — “A time to laugh” | — | Laughter is granted its own appointed season;
