# 08-19 Call Reflection: Sanctifying Speech and Integrity in a Changed Man **Title:** 08-19 at 09:46 — The Vocabulary of a Changed Man **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I hear a brother caught between who he has become and how he still sounds, and it stops me. Samuel has changed in the hidden places, but his mouth still carries the cadence of his old self, and two people felt the gap and called it a lack of integrity. What arrests me is the ache underneath his voice — “What if I’m just not cut out for it, man?” — and the way an entire conversation about finances and reassignments became, without warning, a conversation about a soul. **Why This Matters:** This matters because sanctification is rarely as neat as we want it to be. We imagine that when the heart turns, everything downstream turns with it — the words, the reflexes, the delivery, the way we land on other people. But this call reveals that a man can be genuinely transformed and still be misread, still be wounding others through a vocabulary that has not yet caught up to his repentance. That is a mercy to understand, because it protects us from two errors: the pride that says “I’ve changed, so any offense is theirs,” and the despair that says “I keep failing, so I must not be cut out for this.” Between those two lies the narrow work of the Spirit, who conforms not only the inner man but the very speech of our mouths. For the Body of Christ, this is the daily labor of discipleship — that we would be sanctified even in our sentences, even in what we promise, even in how we make another image-bearer feel heard. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation Read naturally, this is a routine business crisis and a competent recovery. Two clients or contractors, Jaden and Jade, felt oversold and overworked; they built something they believed was already established, grew frustrated, and threatened to walk. Seth stepped in, apologized, absorbed responsibility, calmed the situation, and secured their continued participation, while diagnosing the root problem as Samuel’s tendency to overpromise. The natural reading admires the emotional intelligence on display — the de-escalation, the “listen as a friend first,” the shrewd insistence on understanding the Cleverly finance structure before making any further commitments. It would frame Samuel’s crisis of confidence as ordinary founder burnout, and Seth’s encouragement as good leadership and morale management, nothing more. **The Limitation:** But natural reasoning can measure the outcome and never touch the thing actually happening in the room. It sees a business saved and misses a brother being kept from despair. It hears “I’ll let the Lord decide” and files it as a figure of speech, when in truth it is the hinge of the whole exchange — a man laying his vocation on an altar rather than his ledger. The worldly mind can teach a person to sound heard; it cannot make the difference between a manipulated calm and a genuine reconciliation, between telling someone what they want to hear and confessing “I’m sorry” so truly that Jade says “you don’t know how much that means to me.” The flesh optimizes for the deliverable and treats the person as a variable in it. What the Spirit is unveiling here runs the other direction entirely — that the person is never the variable, and that the gap between a changed heart and unchanged speech is holy ground where the Lord is still at work. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with this call, I keep returning to the sound of it — the phone shuffling between three people, Samuel juggling Samuel Hunt and Ethan and me all at once, a man stretched thin across other people’s needs while his own need goes unspoken. And then the small collapse near the end, when all the logistics fall away and what is left is raw: “What if I’m just not cut out for it, man? I feel like I keep fucking up no matter how hard I try.” That is not a business sentence. That is the cry of a man who has wanted something more than anything, who has not stopped, and who is beginning to wonder if wanting and trying are enough. I notice how quickly the conversation about finance structures and reporting lines dissolved into this — because it was never really about the structure. It was about whether he could trust that the change in him was real. What arrests me is the precise nature of the wound Jaden and Jade named. They did not say Samuel was lying. They said they doubted his integrity — and yet the truth, as I heard it, is subtler and sadder than a lie. Samuel has genuinely changed. He has come, in my own words to him, from actual scamming to a place where “the only issue was the people weren’t feeling heard.” The transformation is real. But his vocabulary hasn’t changed. He still speaks in the register of guarantees, still overexerts the promise, still makes a thing that is new and untested sound established and proven. And so people who cannot see his heart from the inside experience the residue of a man he no longer is. The Spirit is pressing on me that this is one of the most painful seasons of any real repentance — when you have turned, but you still smell of where you came from. The first place Scripture meets me here is the mouth. “Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks” (Matthew 12:34), and we take that to mean the mouth simply reports the heart, instantly and cleanly. But James complicates our comfort: “For we all stumble in many things. If anyone does not stumble in word, he is a perfect man, able also to bridle the whole body” (James 3:2). The tongue, James says, is the last thing to be tamed — “no man can tame the tongue” (James 3:8). This is the mystery I’m seeing in Samuel: the heart can be given to God long before the tongue is bridled by Him. The abundance of his heart has genuinely shifted, but the grooves of habit in his speech are worn deep, and they still carry the old freight of overpromising even when the man himself is trying to tell the truth. The word beneath all of this is integrity itself. The Hebrew is תֹּם (tom, TOME) — completeness, wholeness, soundness, the state of a thing being all of one piece. It is what was said of Job, that he was blameless — תָּם (tam, TAHM) — not sinless, but whole, undivided, the same on the inside as on the outside. What Jaden and Jade sensed was a seam. They felt a gap between the inner Samuel and the outer delivery, and they named that seam “integrity” because integrity is precisely the absence of a seam. And here is the grace: the very thing they accused him of lacking is the thing the Spirit is actively forming in him. God is closing the seam. He is bringing the mouth into agreement with the mended heart, and the friction of this call is the sound of that work being done. I trace this thread back to the wilderness and the giving of the Law, where the very first commandments about neighbor-love turn on speech and promise. “You shall not bear false witness” (Exodus 20:16), yes — but deeper, “When a man vows a vow to the LORD… he shall not break his word; he shall do according to all that proceeds out of his mouth” (Numbers 30:2). Israel learned that a word, once spoken, was a binding reality, that heaven itself held men to their mouths. This is why Jesus later cuts beneath the whole apparatus of oaths: “But let your ‘Yes’ be ‘Yes,’ and your ‘No,’ ‘No’” (Matthew 5:37). The Kingdom man does not need to swear, does not need to guarantee, does not need to overexert the promise — because his plain word already carries the weight of his whole self. Samuel’s healing is precisely this: to speak so plainly that no guarantee is needed, because his yes has become trustworthy. And I notice that Seth’s counsel to him is, without either of them naming it, deeply Christ-shaped. “Speak plainly about it, tell people exactly what it is… Don’t make any guarantees unless it is a guarantee.” That is Matthew 5:37 clothed in business language. The instruction to present the offer honestly — “it’s deliverable but we haven’t proven that it works… we have to present it as that” — is nothing less than truth-telling about the actual state of things, refusing to let hope masquerade as fact. This is the sanctification of ambition, the moment where the Spirit teaches a man that his gift is not diminished by honesty but validated by it. Then there is the turn that undoes me every time I read it back. In the middle of counseling Samuel, I reached for my own recent failure — “just like the other night when every mistake that I made during the intercession, the deliverance, every mistake that I made was well intentioned, but it put me in a position that I didn’t have any right to be in.” I was not standing above my brother correcting him. I was standing beside him, confessing that I too had done good things badly, had meant well and still overstepped, had occupied ground I had no right to occupy. “Maybe that itself was a lesson for this.” The Spirit is showing me that true correction always comes from inside the same struggle, never from above it. Paul knew this: “Brethren, if a man is overtaken in any trespass, you who are spiritual restore such a one in a spirit of gentleness, considering yourself lest you also be tempted” (Galatians 6:1). I corrected Samuel while considering myself, and that made all the difference. Something else arrests me in how Jade received the apology. “All that Jade needed to hear was me say, I’m sorry, and she’s like, ‘You don’t know how much that means to me.’” I called it “the humanity part,” but the Spirit is calling it something older. It is the ministry of reconciliation. “All things are of God, who has reconciled us to Himself through Jesus Christ, and has given us the ministry of reconciliation” (2 Corinthians 5:18). Samuel and I had been so oriented toward solutions — the finance structure, the reassignment, the call with Anna — that we nearly missed that the wound was relational, not logistical. Jade did not need a better offer. She needed to be seen. “Don’t be so focused on solutions that you lose the person.” That is the whole gospel in a sentence about business, because God Himself, when we were the problem, did not send us a better arrangement — He came to us and bore the cost of the reconciliation in His own body. And here is the Kingdom mystery natural reasoning would step right past: the failure was the doorway. Samuel says “I feel like I keep fucking up no matter how hard I try,” and reads the whole episode as evidence of his inadequacy. But the Spirit reads it as invitation. I told him, “don’t see it as a failure so much as an opportunity to grow… it could be an invitation just to slow down with things.” The very frustration that nearly ended the venture is the paideia — παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah), fatherly training and discipline — by which God is teaching him to speak plainly, to listen first, to make no guarantee that is not a guarantee. “Now no chastening seems to be joyful for the present, but painful; nevertheless, afterward it yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness” (Hebrews 12:11). Samuel is not being disqualified. He is being trained. The heart of it lands when Samuel says he will take the day and let the Lord decide whether he is cut out for this. “I’ll take it to the place. I’ll let the Lord decide.” I hear a man refusing to render the verdict on himself, refusing both the pride that would excuse and the despair that would quit, and instead carrying the whole matter to God as one who genuinely does not know but trusts the One who does. This is faith in its plainest clothing. It is Gethsemane in miniature — “not My will, but Yours, be done” (Luke 22:42) — a vocation surrendered rather than clutched, a future released into the hands of the Father. And my role, in that moment, was simply to bless the surrender: “There you go.” For the Body, this whole exchange is a picture of how brothers are meant to carry one another. Samuel came apart under the weight, and I did not merely fix his problem — though I took real responsibility, apologized to Jaden and Jade for my own neglect, offered to understand the Cleverly structure myself. But underneath the practical help I was doing the deeper work of bearing his burden. “Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ” (Galatians 6:2). When he said “what if I’m just not cut out for it,” I did not agree and I did not empty the words of their weight; I said “it’s hard, Samuel Hunt. I know it’s hard,” and then “I know, honey,” and then “you’re doing great” only after he had been fully heard. That is the shape of discipleship — to hold a brother’s despair without being swallowed by it, and to hold his hope for him until he can hold it himself. And so the communal word is this: we are becoming a people whose yes is yes. Not a people who never fail, but a people whose speech is being sanctified into wholeness, whose promises match reality, whose corrections come from inside the shared struggle, and who never lose the person in pursuit of the solution. Jaden and Jade stayed — “luckily, we have two people who still want to work with us. They just needed a little more structure” — not because a better offer was engineered, but because they were finally heard, and honesty finally showed up in the room. The Kingdom advances not through flawless people but through reconciled ones, through mouths slowly brought into agreement with mended hearts. ## Implications and Challenges **The seam between the heart and the mouth is real, and grace covers the gap.** Much of modern Christianity teaches an instant sanctification of the whole self at conversion, and then leaves believers confused and condemned when their old reflexes keep surfacing. This call testifies otherwise: Samuel is genuinely changed and still speaks like his former self, and both things are true at once. James told us the tongue is tamed last of all (James 3:2), which means the enduring presence of old vocabulary is not proof that repentance was fake. The challenge to us is to stop reading our slow speech-sanctification as evidence of unregeneration, and to let the Spirit finish what He started, patiently, in our mouths as surely as in our hearts. **Overpromising is a spiritual issue, not merely a communication flaw.** We live in a culture of guarantees, of hype, of making the untested sound proven, and the Church has too often baptized this language in our marketing, our ministries, and even our claims about what God will do. Jesus reduced all of it to “let your ‘Yes’ be ‘Yes,’ and your ‘No,’ ‘No’” (Matthew 5:37), and Samuel’s healing runs straight through that verse. The correction is direct: “don’t make any guarantees unless it is a guarantee,” and “it’s deliverable but we haven’t proven that it works… we have to present it as that.” This is not merely good business ethics; it is the sanctification of speech, and the Body would do well to examine where our own words outrun our reality. **We are prone to lose the person in pursuit of the solution.** Samuel and I nearly missed that Jaden and Jade were not wounded by the offer but by feeling unheard, and Western Christianity, so trained in problem-solving and programs, carries the same blind spot into ministry. “Don’t be so focused on solutions that you lose the person” is a rebuke to every discipleship reduced to systems and every counseling reduced to steps. The ministry of reconciliation (2 Corinthians 5:18) begins with an “I’m sorry” that costs something, not with a better arrangement. The challenge is to treat every person we serve as an image-bearer with “real problems… their own stuff they’re dealing with,” and to listen as a friend first. **Correction must come from inside the struggle, never from above it.** I could have corrected Samuel from a posture of superiority, but instead I reached for my own recent failure during the intercession, confessing that I too had done well-intentioned things that put me where I had no right to be. This is Galatians 6:1 in practice — restoring gently while “considering yourself lest you also be tempted.” Much correction in the Body wounds precisely because it descends from a height, dispensing judgment without shared frailty. The invitation is to let all our corrections carry the humility of a fellow-stumbler who has been forgiven much. **Despair over repeated failure is not humility; it is a different lie.** Samuel’s “what if I’m just not cut out for it” feels like honesty, but it is the enemy’s counterfeit of contrition — a self-verdict that usurps God’s role as Judge. The gospel neither excuses the failure nor lets it define the man; it reframes it as παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah), training that “yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness” (Hebrews 12:11). Samuel’s decision to “let the Lord decide” instead of rendering the verdict himself is the healthy alternative, and the Body must learn to hold this line for one another — refusing both cheap encouragement and self-condemnation, and pointing every despairing brother back to the One who alone declares who is fit for the work. **Leaders bear real responsibility for the wounds under their watch.** I did not only correct Samuel; I confessed my own neglect — “I should have been more involved… I should have at least audited you more… I maybe should have reached out to the people we had as clients… before they had to come to me.” Institutional Christianity often lets those in authority stand apart from the failures beneath them, but the Spirit here shows a leader owning his portion openly and even apologizing to Jaden and Jade for it. This is a challenge to every elder, pastor, and head of any work: the burden of oversight includes the burden of confession when we have failed to shepherd well. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity is learning to distinguish a changed heart from a changed vocabulary, and to have patience with the lag between them — in ourselves and in our brothers. We grow when we stop demanding that our sanctification be instantaneous and instead cooperate with the slow, deep work of the Spirit taming even our mouths (James 3:2). The mature believer no longer reads every persistent old habit as proof of a false conversion, nor excuses it as unchangeable; he brings it, like Samuel bringing his vocation to “the place,” and lets the Lord finish forming him into wholeness — תֹּם (TOME), the undivided man whose inside and outside are finally one piece. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience here is embarrassingly concrete: speak plainly, promise only what is true, and never let the pursuit of a solution eclipse the person in front of you. It means the “I’m sorry” that costs us pride, the apology offered before we are cornered into it, the honesty that presents a thing as it actually is rather than as we hope it will be. It means, when a brother comes apart under the weight, that we bear the burden with him (Galatians 6:2) — holding his despair without agreeing with it and holding his hope until he can hold it himself. This is the law of Christ worked out in phone calls, apologies, and the daily traffic of real relationships. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture in this call is Samuel’s final surrender — “I’ll take it to the place. I’ll let the Lord decide.” To abide and discern is to refuse to render the verdict on ourselves that belongs to God alone, carrying our uncertainty to Him as an offering rather than resolving it in the flesh through either pride or quitting. It is to watch for the invitation hidden inside the failure, to receive frustration as παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) rather than as disqualification, and to testify to what the Spirit is genuinely doing in a brother even when others cannot yet see it. The watchman’s work is to say “you’re doing great” only after the truth has been fully spoken, so that the encouragement is real and not a bandage over an unhealed wound. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as Your servant and co-heir, standing between the heavens and the earth to make agreement with Your will over my brother and over myself. Thank You that You do not despise the slow work of sanctification, that You are patient with the seam between our mended hearts and our unmended mouths, and that You are even now bringing our speech into wholeness with our hearts. I confess my own overstepping, the well-intentioned mistakes that put me where I had no right to be, and I ask You to keep forming me from inside every struggle rather than lifting me above it. For Samuel Hunt I intercede — do not let him render on himself the verdict that is Yours alone; meet him in the place where he has taken this, and let Your voice, not his despair, decide who he is and whether he is called. Teach us all to let our yes be yes, to make no guarantee that is not a guarantee, to speak so plainly that hype has no room to hide. Guard us from losing the person in the pursuit of the solution, and give us the humility to say “I’m sorry” before we are forced to, that reconciliation might flow through us as it has flowed toward us in Christ. Bless Jaden and Jade, the image-bearers who were brave enough to speak their frustration; honor their courage and keep them under our care with true integrity. I surrender every venture, every word, every relationship into Your hands, trusting that You who began a good work will complete it. To You be all the glory, Father, for the failure that becomes a doorway and the discipline that yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness. In the name of Jesus Christ, who came in the flesh and bore our reconciliation in His body, amen. claude-opus-4.8 ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |--------------------|----------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Matthew 12:34 | — | The mouth speaks from the heart’s abundance, yet the transformation of speech can lag behind the transformation of the heart. | | James 3:2, 8 | — | The tongue is tamed last of all; persistent old vocabulary does not disprove a genuinely changed heart. | | Job 1:1 | תֹּם / תָּם (tom / tam, TOME / TAHM) | Integrity as completeness and wholeness — being all of one piece, undivided within and without. | | Matthew 5:37 | — | “Let your ‘Yes’ be ‘Yes’” — the Kingdom man needs no guarantee because his plain word carries his whole self. | | Numbers 30:2 | — | A vow binds; heaven holds a man to the words that proceed from his mouth. | | Galatians 6:1–2 | — | Restore the fallen from inside the struggle, considering yourself; bear one another’s burdens to fulfill Christ’s law. | | 2 Corinthians 5:18 | — | The ministry of reconciliation — God did not send a better arrangement but came to bear the cost Himself. | | Hebrews 12:11 | παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) | Fatherly training and discipline; painful now, but yielding the peaceable fruit of righteousness. | | Luke 22:42 | — | “Not My will, but Yours” — vocation surrendered to God’s verdict rather than clutched or discarded. |
