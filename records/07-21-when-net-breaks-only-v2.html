# 07-21 Call Reflection: Faith, Crisis, and the Theology of Falling — Samuel’s Story **Title:** 07-21 at 20:00 — When the Net Breaks and Only Falling Remains **Recorded:** 2026-07-21 20:00:25 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I listened to my brother Samuel walk through what sounded like the trembling edge of a professional cliff — a message from an employer that read like either a firing or a hiring, and him unable to tell which — something in me recognized the particular silence that precedes a divine rearrangement. This was not merely a late-night conversation between two men navigating uncertainty; it was a live unfolding of the narrow path, walked in real time. What arrested me most was not the crisis itself, but the theology that emerged through it — raw, unpolished, and truer for being so. **Why This Matters:** There is a moment in every believer’s life when the last earthly support gives way, and what remains is not comfort theology or inspirational scaffolding, but the bare question: is God actually there, and does He actually hold? My brother Samuel was standing in that moment as we spoke. What the Spirit pressed through that conversation is not merely applicable to him — it is the hidden curriculum of every disciple who has been stripped of alternatives and forced to discover whether the Kingdom is real or merely a beautiful idea. The Church in this hour desperately needs a theology that can survive the revocation of a job offer, the threat of homelessness, and the silence of systems that promised support but delivered only political theater. This meditation is an attempt to articulate what the Spirit was doing in the cracks of that conversation. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From a purely natural vantage point, what happened in this conversation is straightforwardly explained: two men in financially precarious circumstances processed anxiety together through mutual encouragement, leaning on religious language as a psychological coping mechanism. The world would see Samuel’s situation as a systems failure — inadequate social safety nets in first-world countries that promise support but deliver bureaucratic obstruction. It would see his reference to faith as understandable denial, a way of reframing helplessness as divine orchestration in order to preserve some sense of agency and dignity. The world would counsel him to network more aggressively, consult a financial advisor, explore housing assistance, and perhaps speak with a therapist about the stress. The spiritual language woven through the call — the talk of the narrow path, of falling on Jesus, of seeds and fruit — would be categorized as metaphor at best, magical thinking at worst. And to be charitable, the world’s concern for Samuel’s material wellbeing would not be entirely misplaced. **The Limitation:** Yet the natural mind, however compassionate, cannot perceive what was actually happening underneath the surface of that call. It cannot see that the progressive stripping of earthly security is sometimes not a failure of systems but the intentional movement of a Father who loves His sons too much to let them remain anchored to what cannot last. What looks like a net breaking is sometimes the Father cutting the net — not because He is cruel, but because He is readying the catch for something the net itself could never carry. The mind that processes only cause and effect, only risk and resource, will always miss the grammar of kairos — the appointed time in which God interrupts chronological sequence with Kingdom purpose. The conversation I shared with my brother was not just two men coping; it was two sons of the Kingdom doing theology on the edge of the cliff, which is precisely where theology becomes most alive. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sat with Samuel in that late-night hour, something I kept returning to was the peculiar mercy embedded in having no options left. He said it himself — if he gets fired, it might be the best-case scenario, because then everything opens up for outreach. And in the same breath he acknowledged that this job was his only lifeline, that without it he faces homelessness. That tension is not accidental. It is the precise geography of faith — the place where what we need and what we fear are standing right beside each other, and only trust in God determines which one speaks louder. Jesus Himself pressed this point with devastating clarity when He asked, “Which of you by worrying can add one cubit to his stature?” (Matthew 6:27, NKJV). Samuel had already internalized the answer. “I’ve probably taken years away,” he said, half laughing. And there it was — not denial, but the beginning of surrender. The laughter of a man who has started to see the absurdity of white-knuckling a life that was never his to control. That laughter, I believe, was holy. What strikes me further is the image Samuel offered — not original to him, but clearly implanted through the Spirit’s work: *Can you run to Jesus? No. Can you hold on? No. But can you fall?* That exchange, wherever he first heard it, carries more ecclesiological weight than a hundred polished altar calls. Because what it describes is not the triumphant posture of a conqueror striding toward the throne — it describes a man at the end of his own motion, whose only remaining movement is downward. And it reframes that downward movement not as defeat but as the precise posture required for grace. The Greek word for grace, χάρις (charis, KAH-rees), carries within it the sense of gift freely given — and gifts, by nature, are received by open hands, not clenched fists. You cannot catch what you are already gripping. I think of Peter stepping out of the boat in Matthew 14. The text does not say he fell — it says he walked on the water, and then, when he saw the wind, he was afraid, and *beginning to sink* he cried out. There is something important in that sequence. He did not sink immediately. Sinking was a process initiated by the shift of his gaze from Christ to circumstance. And what did Jesus do? He did not lecture him. He *immediately* stretched out His hand and caught him. The catch came before the rebuke. The arm extended before the question was asked. That is the character of the One Samuel is being asked to fall toward. Not a theological abstraction, but a hand already in motion. The disciples kept surfacing in our conversation, and rightly so — because they are the canonical archive of people who had every reason to trust and still failed to. My brother and I reflected together on how Jesus had explicitly, plainly, repeatedly foretold His death and resurrection across all four gospel accounts, and yet when it happened, even the remaining eleven did not believe. They did not believe Mary Magdalene. They did not believe the two on the road to Emmaus — the road, incidentally, whose name means *warm springs* or *warm baths*, as if even the setting of that resurrection encounter was meant to communicate comfort and restoration. And yet the disciples, even confronted with living witnesses of the risen Christ, held back. Jesus rebuked them for it — not with cruelty but with what I can only call a grief-infused directness. What the Spirit is showing me in that pattern is that hardness of heart is not reserved for the wicked. It is the default condition of even devoted hearts when the revelation exceeds what their categories can hold. The disciples had spent three years being trained, fed miraculously, witnessing healings, deliverance, the transfiguration — and still their hearts were hard enough in critical moments to resist what Jesus had plainly promised. This is not a comfortable truth, but it is a necessary one. It means that the work of softening the heart is ongoing, not completed at conversion. It means that the invitation to trust — the invitation Samuel is living right now — is not a one-time decision but a daily, sometimes hourly, discipline of returning to the word spoken and saying: I believe You. Even now. The Gethsemane moment I raised during the call continues to press me. Three times Jesus returned to find His disciples asleep, and three times He spoke — not with rage, but with a kind of intimate sorrow. “Could you not watch with Me one hour?” (Matthew 26:40, NKJV). The word for *watch* here is γρηγορεύω (grēgoreuō, greh-gor-EH-oh) — to be awake, alert, vigilant. But the texture of His question is not military; it is relational. It is the register of a friend saying, *I needed you present.* And they could not give it — not because they were uncommitted, but because their flesh had no capacity to sustain what His spirit was carrying. This is why He gave up the Spirit at Calvary: so that we might receive that same Spirit and finally be able to do what the disciples could not do in the garden — *remain awake, remain present, remain alert in the hour of His prayer being answered in us.* The seed-and-fruit thread that Samuel introduced near the end of our conversation opened something I have been sitting with since. He told the story of a conversation with his mother when he was fourteen or fifteen — she mentioned Cracker Barrel, they spoke of Tennessee, she mentioned a southern girl — and four years later, on a spontaneous trip, he walked into a Cracker Barrel and met the woman who would become his wife. He had not thought of that conversation in four years. The seed lay dormant. And then it fruited, spectacularly, without his management or intention. This is precisely what Jesus describes in Mark 4 — the Kingdom of God is like a man who scatters seed, and then sleeps and rises, and the seed sprouts and grows, *he knows not how.* (Mark 4:27, NKJV). The Greek underlying “he knows not how” is αὐτόματος (automatos, ow-TOM-ah-tos) — spontaneously, of itself, without visible mechanism. This is the grammar of Kingdom increase: invisible germination, sudden fruit. What I told my brother during the call was this: something you say or do today in obedience, prompted by the Spirit, may not be understood until years later — and when the fruit appears, it will retroactively reveal what the moment actually was. This is the prophetic rhythm of covenant life. Abraham did not fully understand the binding of Isaac until Calvary made the typology clear. Joseph did not know his suffering in the pit was preparation for the throne until he was standing on it. The road to resurrection always looks like the road to ruin from inside the middle of it. Samuel is in the middle of it. So, often, am I. What I also said to him — and what I believe the Spirit confirmed even as I said it — was that the support structures of the world are a facade, architecturally designed not for the vulnerable but for those with political proximity to power. This is not cynicism; it is discernment. Jesus never promised that Caesar’s systems would be the vehicle of Kingdom provision. He promised that the Father who clothes the grass of the field would clothe us — that the One who knows what we need before we ask would see to it (Matthew 6:30–32, NKJV). The systems fail. The Father does not. And learning to distinguish between those two is not pessimism — it is the beginning of unshakeable faith. There is also something in the word my brother received — the call to listen more than he speaks. I had spoken that word to him previously, and he told me it had lodged in him. That is not accident. The Hebrew concept of שָׁמַע (shama’, sha-MAH) — the word used in the Shema, Israel’s central confession — means not merely to hear but to hear and obey, to receive and respond with the whole person. Discipleship, at its root, is the posture of shama’. It is what the disciples failed to fully practice even in the presence of the Teacher. It is what Samuel is being formed into through loss, uncertainty, and the stripping of noise. When all the options are gone, what remains is the voice — and the question becomes whether we have been practicing the posture of listening long enough to recognize it. Finally, as I left the call to attend to my pregnant wife and the life growing in our home, I was struck by the convergence. Samuel on the edge of homelessness. Me navigating new life coming. Both of us on the narrow path, together, laughing at the end of the night about Gladiator. There is something profoundly Kingdom about that — the gravity of the hour not crushing the joy of brotherhood, the reality of the cross not extinguishing the resurrection laughter. This is what Paul meant when he spoke of the peace that passes understanding: it is not the absence of the storm; it is the inexplicable groundedness that persists *inside* it. ## Implications and Challenges The conversation between Samuel and me carries a quiet rebuke to the prosperity-adjacent assumption, common in many Western congregations, that financial stability is a sign of spiritual faithfulness and its absence a sign of lack of faith. Both of us have stood near the edge of homelessness. Neither of us would trade the depth of encounter with God that emerged from that edge for the comfort of a cushioned life. The Kingdom does not promise insulation from precarity; it promises presence within it. A Church that only preaches from abundance will produce disciples who have never learned to fall — and therefore disciples who have never learned what it means to be caught. There is also a challenge embedded in the disciples’ hardness of heart as we discussed it. The modern Church often treats doubt as a personal moral failure — something to be confessed, corrected, and moved past quickly. But the disciples’ unbelief in the face of explicit, repeated, firsthand promise and witnessed miracle suggests something more structural: that the heart requires ongoing renovation, not one-time repair. The invitation is not to pretend certainty we do not feel, but to bring our unbelief honestly to the One who said, *I believe; help my unbelief* (Mark 9:24, NKJV) — and to keep returning to the word spoken over us even when present circumstances have not yet confirmed it. The Western church has largely lost the theology of dormant seeds — the idea that obedience in one moment produces fruit in a distant season we will not see, manage, or perhaps even recognize until after the fact. We have been culturally trained toward immediate return — visible metrics, measurable growth, quantifiable impact. But Kingdom seeds do not obey these metrics. Samuel’s mother’s offhand comment about Tennessee and southern girls was not a strategic intervention; it was a word spoken without agenda into a fourteen-year-old who was barely listening — and it flowered four years later in a Cracker Barrel into the encounter that shaped the rest of his life. The Spirit does not need our full attention to plant. He needs our availability. The call to discipleship in the prophetic dimension — which I raised and which I genuinely hunger for — is worth naming directly here. The prophetic ministry is not a platform; it is a burden and a gift. Walking it alone is genuinely dangerous, not because the Spirit is unreliable but because the human vessel is. A buffer, an elder, a spiritual father — these are not signs of weakness but of wisdom. The early church had this. The New Testament epistles are full of it. The absence of it in contemporary prophetic circles has produced much damage. This is not a critique to be weaponized but a grace to be sought. Lastly, there is something to be said about the way genuine brotherhood functions as a form of theology in motion. Samuel and I were not delivering polished sermons to each other. We were stumbling through a hard night together, quoting Scripture imperfectly, laughing, cutting out, getting distracted by Gladiator 2. And yet the Spirit moved. The word was spoken. Fruit was named. This is what the early church looked like before it had buildings or programs — friends carrying each other on a narrow road, doing theology in motion, discovering the Kingdom in the cracks of ordinary, precarious life. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This meditation calls us to reframe the seasons of stripping — of job loss, housing insecurity, relational uncertainty — not as interruptions to the life of faith but as its most concentrated classrooms. Maturity is not measured by how little we need God; it is measured by how quickly we return to Him when everything else falls away. Learning to fall, rather than grip, is not regression — it is the advanced curriculum. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience required here is twofold: first, to resist the anxiety that claims worry is productive, and to replace it with the practiced discipline of prayer, trust, and the spoken declaration that what God has promised is already done; second, to speak words of obedience in season and out, trusting that seeds planted faithfully in unremarkable moments will produce fruit in appointed times that we cannot manufacture. The call is not to visible fruitfulness but to faithful planting. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic saint must learn to abide in shama’ — the posture of whole-person listening — and to release words as prompted without needing to track their germination. The prophetic life is one of sowing into tomorrow from today’s obedience, trusting that the One who ordains seasons also ordains outcomes. It is also a life that requires the covering of genuine spiritual community — not isolation, not platform, but brotherhood on the narrow road. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as a son of the covenant, a co-heir with Christ, one of Your servants whom You have called into the prophetic rhythm of Your purposes — and I come on behalf of my brother Samuel, standing at the edge of what looks like ruin but what I believe You are calling transformation. You are not unaware of his situation. You are not scrambling to address it. Before the foundations of the earth, You knew this night, this message left in drafts, this threat of homelessness, this stripping of earthly lifelines — and You ordained it as part of the formation of a man You are clearly not finished with. I ask that You would do what only You can do: show up in the specific, the practical, the material, and the miraculous — that his faith would not be a theological idea but a living testimony to those watching. Steady his heart against anxiety and fear, and let the peace that passes understanding garrison his mind in Christ Jesus. For me also, Lord — train me in the posture of shama’, the discipline of abiding, the humility of receiving correction and covering. Let the seeds spoken in the Spirit through this season produce fruit in the appointed harvest You have already set. We do not lean on systems, on political favor, or on the structures of this passing age — we lean on You, the One who holds the world together and who holds Your children’s names in Your hand. Let what You have spoken come to pass. Let what You have planted grow. Let the narrow path feel less narrow because You are on it with us. To the Lamb who was slain and who lives and reigns — be all glory, honor, and power, from this moment into the ages of ages.
