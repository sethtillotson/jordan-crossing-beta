# 04-06 16:48 Reflection: Roses With Thorns **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation (Live Capture of a phone/voice call between Seth and a brother named Samuel, with no separate post-reflection segment) **Reflection: Roses With Thorns — On Idolatry, Heartbreak, and the God Who Removes What We Exalt Above Him** ## Opening Observation As I sat with my brother Samuel in the middle of his pain, something far deeper than a breakup was unfolding before me. What presented itself as heartbreak was, in the Spirit, something more ancient and more serious — the grief of a man who had placed a created thing in the throne room of his heart where only the Creator belongs. I could hear the rawness in him, and I did not want to rush past it, because I recognized something of myself in it, and I recognized something the Lord was doing in real time. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Church is full of people who are genuinely in love with something — or someone — more than they are with Jesus, and who have not yet been confronted with that reality in a way they could not avoid. Samuel said it himself with devastating honesty: *“I gave this girl more than I’ve ever gave Christ.”* That confession is not merely a personal wound to be dressed — it is a prophetic mirror. The Lord does not remove what we love to be cruel. He removes what we have exalted in His place to be merciful. The entire story of Israel is the story of a people who kept giving their hearts to things that could not hold them, and a God who kept calling them back. If Samuel’s pain can become a doorway rather than a tomb, then what appeared to be destruction is actually deliverance. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the vantage point of natural human reasoning, this conversation is simply about a young man suffering the aftermath of a failed relationship — unrequited love, unanswered messages, emotional devastation, and the disorienting grief that follows the loss of a significant person. The world would see this as a psychological wound requiring time, self-care, new routines, and perhaps therapy. It would say: *You loved too much, too soon, too openly — set healthier boundaries next time. Protect yourself. Rebuild your confidence. Move on.* The culturally literate advice would be to delete the contact, grieve the loss, and eventually re-enter the dating landscape better equipped. At best, the world might affirm the depth of Samuel’s love as something noble, even if misdirected. At worst, it would reduce what is happening in his soul to a serotonin deficit and a cognitive distortion to be corrected. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind, however compassionate its framing, cannot see what the Spirit sees — and what the Spirit sees here is not primarily a failed romance. It is a misplaced altar. The flesh reads “heartbreak”; the Spirit reads “idolatry being dismantled.” This distinction cannot be arrived at through emotional intelligence alone. There is a dimension to what was happening in that conversation that only the illumination of Scripture can name. When Samuel said, *“I gave this girl everything — everything I could have gave to a woman,”* the Spirit did not respond with grief but with surgery. And natural wisdom, no matter how refined, does not perform that kind of surgery. It administers comfort where God administers correction — not because God is not compassionate, but because He is far more committed to Samuel’s wholeness than to his temporary comfort. ## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation As I listened to Samuel describe what he had given this woman — every door opened, every sacrifice made, more love than he had ever offered to anyone — I found myself pressing into something I have learned through my own journey: that the depth of pain we feel when a relationship ends is often proportional to the height of the altar we built for it. Samuel was not simply heartbroken. He was experiencing the spiritual consequence of devotion poured into a vessel that was never designed to hold it. And I say that with the utmost tenderness, because I have been there. I know what it is to give everything to someone and discover that the giving itself had become a form of worship. Scripture does not flinch from this reality. When the people of Israel entered the land, one of the primary warnings the Lord gave was against exalting the creation above the Creator — against giving to another what belongs only to Him. But this is not merely an Old Testament warning about literal stone idols. The apostolic letters of the New Covenant are equally unsparing. Colossians 3:5 names covetousness — the disordered longing that clings to something with the grip that belongs only to God — as idolatry. The issue is not love itself. Love is good. Love is commanded. The issue is the ordering of love, the hierarchy of devotion, the question of what sits at the center of a man’s soul. What I said to Samuel in that moment — *“The reason why the Lord removed her is because you exalted her above Christ”* — I did not say to wound him. I said it because I am convinced that the most loving thing I can do for a brother is to help him see what the Spirit is doing beneath what his emotions are telling him. And what the Spirit was doing was consistent with the nature of God as revealed across the entire canon: He is a jealous God — not with the jealousy of insecurity, but with the jealousy of a covenant-keeping Father who refuses to let His children remain in bondage to a lesser love. The Hebrew word קַנָּא (qannāʾ, kah-NAW) — used in Exodus 34:14 when God declares, *“for the LORD, whose name is Jealous, is a jealous God”* — does not describe petty emotional competition. It describes the fierce, protective, exclusive love of One who knows that nothing else can satisfy the soul He made. Samuel said something that arrested me entirely: *“Surely my love is enough. My love isn’t enough for anyone. My love’s not enough for my family. My love’s not enough for my wife.”* I did not rush past that. There was revelation in the wound. He had spent himself trying to prove his worth through the measure of love he could extend — and every rejection confirmed the lie that he was, at his core, not enough. But this is precisely the place where the Gospel becomes either academic doctrine or living power. The Good News is not that Samuel needs to love better or love more carefully or protect himself more wisely. The Good News is that there is One before whom Samuel’s love is not only *enough* — it is *received*, *cherished*, and *answered* with a love that does not change when he sends one too many messages, does not ignore him when he is desperate, and does not grow cold when he is at his worst. This is the place where I am seeing the deeper structure of what the Lord was engineering in this conversation. Samuel looked at his father for the first time through new eyes — saw in his father the same flinching away from Christ that he himself had known, the same offense at the Gospel — and something broke open in him. He said, *“I don’t want to end up like this.”* That is the beginning of repentance. Not the emotion of remorse, but the μετάνοια (metanoia, meh-TAH-noy-ah) — the genuine turning of the mind, the reorientation of the entire inner compass. Samuel was not simply grieving a woman. He was seeing, perhaps for the first time with terrifying clarity, the trajectory of a life organized around things that will not hold. There is also something deeply significant in the image that came up in that conversation — the image I offered Samuel: *“Roses look pretty, but when you grip them, the thorns hurt.”* That is not merely a cliché. That is a prophetic parable for the condition of the soul that grasps at beauty before counting the cost of what is required to hold it. Throughout Scripture, the wilderness — the painful in-between, the place of stripping — is not punishment. It is preparation. Israel was brought into the wilderness not because God abandoned them but because God was dismantling the Egypt inside of them. The forty years were not wasted years; they were years of μετάνοια (meh-TAH-noy-ah), of learning what the people could not learn in comfort. Samuel is in a wilderness right now, and I wanted him to know — this is not demolition. This is construction. When I mentioned Lot’s wife, I was not being cold. I was being earnest. The instruction to delete the messages, to block the numbers, to not leave a trail of breadcrumbs back to the place from which the Lord has called him out — that is not emotional advice. It is covenantal wisdom. Lot’s wife looked back not because she wanted to be a pillar of salt but because her heart had never fully left. And that is the danger Samuel faces — not that he will consciously choose to return, but that boredom, loneliness, and the seductive pull of familiar pain will lead him back to a city the Lord has already determined to bring down. Genesis 19 is not a story about God’s harshness. It is a story about what becomes of those who cannot release the Babylon within them even as they are being led out. I also want to name something about the labor Samuel described — the striving to bring everything back to Christ through his own effort. *“I’ve tried so hard to bring myself back. To bring my marriage back to Christ. To bring everything back to Christ, and he just throws it back in my face.”* The pain in that sentence is real, but so is the theological confusion embedded in it. Samuel was doing something that the religious flesh does constantly: substituting sincerity for surrender, effort for faith. Galatians 3:3 cuts to the heart of this — *“Are you so foolish? Having begun in the Spirit, are you now being made perfect by the flesh?”* The answer to everything Samuel is facing is not harder trying. It is deeper yielding. The difference between those two things is the difference between the law and the Gospel. That is why my instruction to him at the end of the call was what it was: *Take everything to the secret place. Speak it all to Jesus as if you are kneeling at His feet. Surrender it. And then — praise.* This is not a spiritual technique. This is the ancient pattern. The Psalms — the entire Psalter — move through lament into praise not because the pain disappears but because the soul re-orders itself around the One who is higher than the circumstances. Psalm 22 opens with *“My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?”* — the very cry Samuel echoed when he asked, *“Why do I still feel forsaken?”* — and closes with *“For He has not despised nor abhorred the affliction of the afflicted; neither has He hidden His face from Him; but when He cried to Him, He heard.”* The path from forsaken to heard runs through the secret place. I am also aware that what I shared about my own marriage was not incidental. Samuel needed to hear that the discipline of ordered love — loving the Father in heaven more than even the gift of a wife — is not a restriction. It is the very foundation upon which a godly marriage can be built. I told him that I will not allow myself to give my wife more than what I give to the Father. That is not coldness toward my wife. It is faithfulness to the covenant that makes all other covenants possible. Matthew 6:33 is not merely a verse about provision — it is a blueprint for the ordering of all love: *“But seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things shall be added to you.”* When God is first, everything else finds its proper place. When anything else comes first, everything — including the thing exalted — begins to collapse under the weight of a devotion it was never designed to bear. I think of how the Lord spoke through Hosea, pursuing an unfaithful Israel with relentless love — *“Therefore, behold, I will allure her, will bring her into the wilderness, and speak comfort to her”* (Hosea 2:14). The wilderness is where God speaks. Not the mountaintop of achievement. Not the fullness of a relationship that answers every longing. The wilderness — the stripped place, the silent place, the place where there is nothing left to grip — is where the voice of the Lord can finally be heard without competition. Samuel is being allured into exactly that wilderness right now. And my prayer is that he does not fight his way out of it before the Lord has finished speaking. Finally, I want to sit with the sign of Jonah that I mentioned in that conversation. Not everyone is chosen to carry that sign. The sign of Jonah — as Jesus interpreted it in Matthew 12:39–40 — is the sign of burial and resurrection, of three days in the deep, of a descent that looks like death and emerges as proclamation. Samuel’s pain is not without purpose. The suffering of the co-heir of the Kingdom of God is never without purpose. Romans 8:17 says we are *“heirs of God and joint heirs with Christ, if indeed we suffer with Him, that we may also be glorified together.”* The *“if indeed”* is not a conditional of doubt — it is a conditional of identity. The suffering is the mark of the co-heir. Samuel is being marked. ## Implications and Challenges There is a habit in much of modern Christianity of treating emotional pain as something to be relieved as quickly as possible — as though discomfort is always an indication of spiritual failure or divine absence. But the witness of Scripture tells a different story entirely. The prophets were not comfortable people. The apostles did not preach from seasons of ease. The pain Samuel is experiencing is not evidence that God has abandoned him; it is evidence that God is close enough to operate. We must resist the pastoral instinct to rush a brother out of the wilderness before the Lord has completed the work He brought him there to do. We must also reckon honestly with idolatry as a New Covenant reality. It is far too easy to relegate idolatry to the realm of ancient paganism — stone altars, golden calves, Asherah poles. But the New Testament is unambiguous: wherever the heart’s supreme devotion is given to something other than God, idolatry is operative. The modern Western Christian world has largely made peace with relational idolatry — with the cultural expectation that romantic love will be the primary source of meaning, identity, validation, and wholeness. Samuel’s confession — *“I gave this girl more than I ever gave Christ”* — is not an unusual confession. It is a remarkably honest version of what most people in the church have never said aloud but have lived without apology. The grace here is that Samuel named it. There is also a challenge embedded in the counsel I gave about striving. The impulse to *do more, try harder, love better, intercede longer* in order to produce a spiritual outcome is deeply religious and deeply fleshly at the same time. It is possible to use the language of prayer and intercession — even the correct language, the language of decreeing and declaring — as a form of self-reliance dressed in spiritual vocabulary. I know this because I told Samuel: *“Real prayer, real intercession — knowing what to decree, what to declare — none of that worked either. It’s God’s will.”* Sometimes God’s will is not the outcome we were interceding for. Surrender is not the abandonment of faith. **It is the perfection of it.** We must also take seriously the generational dimension of what Samuel glimpsed in his father. The moment he saw his father’s offended flinching from the Gospel and recognized himself in it — that was a moment of extraordinary prophetic clarity. The patterns of distance from God do not begin with us and do not end with us unless they are broken by the deliberate grace of God working through a willing vessel. Samuel’s willingness to be that vessel — even in his brokenness, even in his grief — is precisely the kind of weakness through which the Lord displays His strength. The instruction to go to the secret place — to pray, to lament, to praise — is not a spiritual bandage. It is a prescription for the re-ordering of the soul. Matthew 6:6, the Gethsemane of Jesus, the entire Psalter, the book of Job — all of it points in the same direction: the secret place is where transformation happens. Public declaration is important. Corporate worship is essential. But the secret place is where the idol is dismantled and the throne is restored to its rightful occupant. A Christianity that has abandoned the secret place is a Christianity that will continue to be surprised by idolatry, because it has no place where the ordering of the inner life can be examined and corrected. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** The depth of our pain in any loss reveals the height of the altar we had built. This is not a cause for shame — it is a cause for honest inventory. Spiritual maturity is not the absence of deep love; it is the right ordering of love, so that every good gift is received and held in its proper place beneath the One from whom all gifts come. Samuel’s willingness to say *“I gave this girl more than I ever gave Christ”* is not a confession of weakness — it is the first act of a man who is learning what it means to love God with all his heart. That re-ordering is the lifelong work of discipleship. **For Kingdom Obedience:** What the Lord is requiring of Samuel — and of every one of us who has misplaced our deepest devotion — is not penance but repentance. μετάνοια (meh-TAH-noy-ah): a genuine turning. The practical expression of that turning is this: take what you have been bringing to a person — the need, the longing, the desperation, the hope, the love — and bring it instead to the secret place. Pour it out before the Lord. Let nothing be withheld. And then — this is the key — begin to praise. Not because the pain is gone, but because the One who is worthy of worship is still standing when everything else has fallen. **For Prophetic Posture:** The Church needs men who have passed through the wilderness of misplaced devotion and come out the other side with ordered hearts — men who can sit with a brother in his 11 p.m. darkness and speak not only comfort but truth. Samuel’s season of suffering, if he yields to it rather than fighting it, will produce in him exactly this kind of capacity. The sign of Jonah is not merely a sign of personal survival. It is a sign that is meant to be shown to a generation. **He who goes into the deep and comes back up speaks with an authority that cannot be manufactured in the comfortable places.** ## A Closing Prayer Father, I stand in this moment as one who belongs to You — not as one who has arrived but as one who has been found, called out, and marked as a co-heir of the Kingdom through the shed blood of Your Son. I bring Samuel before You now with everything he is carrying — the grief, the rejection, the bewilderment, the love that had no right home, the questions that have no easy answer. You are not distant from his pain. You are present in it, working in it, speaking through it. Lord, let the stripping be complete. Let the idol fall cleanly. Let nothing of that false altar remain standing in his heart that would compete with Your rightful place upon the throne of his life. I decree and declare, in agreement with Your revealed will, that this wilderness will speak — that the voice You have been waiting to have without competition will be heard clearly, deeply, and lastingly in the secret place. Let the μετάνοια (meh-TAH-noy-ah) be real, and let it be lasting. Let the praise that follows the lament be the beginning of a new season, a new ordering, a new love — the first love, restored. And let the suffering of this season become the proclamation of a generation that is waiting for someone to emerge from the deep and speak. You are faithful. You are good. You are the only One whose throne cannot be shaken, and it is to that unshakeable throne that I surrender my brother now, with thanksgiving and with confidence in Your unwavering covenant love. In the name of Jesus Christ, who is the same yesterday, today, and forever — Amen. ## Scripture References & Original Language Notes | **Passage (NKJV)** | **Original Language** | **Theological Insight** | |------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | Exodus 34:14 — “for the LORD, whose name is Jealous, is a jealous God” | קַנָּא (qannāʾ, kah-NAW) — jealous / zealous in covenant love | God’s jealousy is not insecurity but the fierce exclusivity of covenantal devotion;
