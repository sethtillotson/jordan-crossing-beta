# 04-22 Call Reflection: Samuel’s Grief, Sobriety Decision, and Spiritual Warfare **Title:** 04-22 at 09:50 — When the Drinking Stops and the Grief Remains **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I sat with this conversation afterward, something in me recognized the unmistakable weight of a man standing at a threshold — not yet through the door, but no longer comfortable on the old side of it. My brother Samuel was not simply confessing a bad night of drinking; he was, in his own halting, honest way, describing the spiritual erosion that grief unchecked can produce in a man. Something arrests me here: the honesty itself is the first sign of life, and I want to honor that without romanticizing the territory he still has to cross. **Why This Matters:** The saints of God are not immune to the slow pull of substances, heartbreak, and the illusion of belonging that wrong company offers. What happened in this conversation is not an edge case or a private struggle unworthy of meditation — it is a portrait of how the enemy exploits legitimate pain. When a man loves someone, loses her, and does not yet know how to grieve before God, he will find something to fill the space: alcohol, tobacco, the company of strangers whose loyalty is defined by the favela rather than the Father. This matters because the Body of Christ desperately needs a theology of grief that is honest enough to sit with the ache and anchored enough not to drown in it. If the church cannot offer that, young men will find other altars. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the vantage point of natural human reasoning, what this conversation represents is fairly legible: a young man processing a painful breakup the only way he currently knows how, numbing himself with alcohol and cigarettes, reaching out compulsively to someone who has already withdrawn, and seeking belonging in whatever corner of the world will receive him. His father’s advice — “get a rebound” — is the world’s standard prescription for heartbreak, a kind of emotional ibuprofen meant to reduce the swelling without addressing the fracture underneath. The Brazilian men he encountered offer a different but equally worldly solution: replace the intimacy of romance with the intensity of brotherhood-by-force, loyalty enforced by hierarchy and history rather than love. Even the recognition that drinking and smoking are “killing him slowly” is framed, at the ground level, in purely physical and consequentialist terms. The world can see that addiction is destructive. What it cannot see is why. **The Limitation:** The natural mind can diagram the problem with impressive accuracy — the grief, the compulsion, the unhealthy coping — and still remain entirely blind to the deeper architecture of what is happening in a man’s soul when he is unmoored from the love of God. What I heard beneath Samuel’s words was not merely a behavioral problem requiring better habits or a psychological wound requiring better processing. There is a spiritual hunger in him that neither Emily nor alcohol nor the favela brotherhood can satisfy, and the natural mind has no category for that kind of hunger. It can see the symptoms and prescribe management; it cannot perceive the sanctuary that the man actually needs. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sat with what Samuel said — “it was pulling me farther away from everything I need to be closer to” — I felt something shift in me. That sentence did not come from self-help language or therapeutic vocabulary. That came from somewhere deeper. Samuel may not have known it consciously, but he was articulating what the Scriptures call being drawn away from the τέλος (telos, TEL-oss) — the end, the goal, the completion — toward which a man was created. He knew something was wrong not merely because of the physical toll, but because on some level, even in his inebriation, he could feel the distance from what he was made for. The Proverbs speak plainly about this kind of distance: “Wine is a mocker, strong drink is a brawler, and whoever is led astray by it is not wise” (Proverbs 20:1, NKJV). The word translated “led astray” carries within it the idea of staggering, of losing one’s footing on a path that should be walked with clarity. Samuel was not merely drinking too much; he was staggering away from his own τέλος (TEL-oss), and somewhere underneath the haze, the Spirit had not let go of him. The fact that he picked up the phone sober and said “I need to quit” is itself a mercy — it is the grace of conviction, not the condemnation of punishment. I also want to sit with the Emily situation, because I do not believe it is peripheral to the spiritual conversation at all. Samuel said, “I keep thinking she’s coming back and she’s not, and I need to get okay with that.” There is a theology of loss embedded in that sentence that the church often handles too quickly. We rush to Romans 8:28 before the man has been allowed to weep before the Lord. But the Psalms do not do that. David writes in Psalm 31:9, “Have mercy on me, O LORD, for I am in trouble; my eye wastes away with grief, yes, my soul and my body” (NKJV). The allowance for grief in Scripture is vast and largely unclaimed in Western evangelical Christianity, which tends to conflate faith with emotional resolution. What Samuel needed in that conversation was not first a strategy but first a permission — permission to grieve rightly, to bring the ache to the altar rather than to the bottle. The Hebrew concept of לֵב (lev, LEV) — heart, in the full sense of the seat of will, emotion, and intellect — understands that grief, when it is not processed before God, calcifies into something that the flesh will then manage by whatever means are available. Alcohol numbs the לֵב (LEV). A rebound distracts it. New loyalty structures temporarily replace its sense of belonging. But none of these carry the weight the heart was designed to release only at the feet of the Father. Samuel’s instinct that a “rebound” did not feel ethical is, I believe, more prophetic than he realized. He framed it as an intuition that it was not what God would want — and then asked the right question: *why* does God not want it? My answer in the moment was partial: because it is not good for us. But I want to press deeper here, now that I have had time to sit with it. The reason God guards against the rebound is not merely consequentialist — it is covenantal. Marriage and the deep bonding between a man and a woman in the covenant context participates in something Scripture calls חֶסֶד (hesed, HEH-sed) — loyal, unfailing, steadfast covenant love. When that bond is broken and a man immediately replaces it with another body and another emotional attachment, he is not healing; he is fracturing the soul’s capacity for חֶסֶד (HEH-sed) still further. He is training himself that love is expendable and replaceable, which is precisely the opposite of what the Father’s love models. This brings me to the moment Samuel asked, almost offhandedly, “Did it get you any closer to God?” — and I said yes. I pointed him to the meditations I had sent about questions of *how much of life am I actually living*, community, and rest. These are not incidental themes. They are precisely the themes that crack open when a man is sober enough to look at his own soul and honest enough to admit what he sees. The question of *how much of life am I actually living* is, in the language of John 10:10, the question of abundance — “I have come that they may have life, and that they may have it more abundantly” (NKJV). The Greek word there is περισσός (perissos, peh-rees-SOS) — exceeding, superabundant, beyond the measure of ordinary expectation. Samuel, in his grief and his numbing and his frantic grasping, was living at barely a fraction of what the Father has designed for him. I think about the Brazilian men and the favela brotherhood, and I do not want to simply dismiss it as danger — though danger is certainly present. There is something real in what drew Samuel toward them: the need for brotherhood, loyalty, belonging, protection, being *known* in community. These are not sinful desires. They are, in fact, deeply biblical ones. The early church in Acts 2 was described as having κοινωνία (koinōnia, koy-no-NEE-ah) — participation, fellowship, shared life — that was so tangible and so total that people sold property to sustain one another. What the favela brotherhood offers is a distorted and dangerous shadow of that genuine need. The enemy does not create new desires; he counterfeits the God-designed ones. Samuel needs brotherhood. He simply needs it from the right source and under the right covenant. I also notice something in the conversation’s structure itself: Samuel was the one who brought God into it. Unprompted, he asked whether San Francisco had drawn me closer to God. Unprompted, he said a rebound did not feel like something God would want. These are not the words of a man who has abandoned faith; these are the words of a man whose faith is being pressed upon from the inside even while his external behaviors are moving in a contradictory direction. This tension — the Holy Spirit pressing inward while the flesh reaches outward for relief — is what Paul calls the warfare in Romans 7: “For the good that I will to do, I do not do; but the evil I will not to do, that I practice” (Romans 7:19, NKJV). Samuel is not a reprobate. He is a man in the middle of that Romans 7 war, and the fact that he can name it at all is the work of the Spirit. The resolution of that war does not come through willpower alone, as Samuel himself intuited when he said “once and for all, no more” — understanding that the decision had to be decisive, not negotiated. This echoes the language of the cross: the aorist tense in which Paul speaks of crucifying the flesh suggests a decisive, punctiliar act rather than an ongoing process of gradual reduction. “Those who are Christ’s have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires” (Galatians 5:24, NKJV). The Greek verb is ἐσταύρωσαν (es-TOW-ro-san) — they crucified, a completed act. Samuel’s instinct that moderation was not the answer — that zero was the only viable posture — was more theologically sound than it may have sounded in the moment. But I want to hold this carefully: decisive action and sustained grace must walk together. The crucifixion of the flesh in Galatians 5 is not a one-time willpower event but rather the life lived in the Spirit that follows the decisive orientation toward Christ. Samuel will need more than a resolution; he will need a community that holds him, a practice of bringing his grief to God rather than to the bottle, and a vision of the τέλος (TEL-oss) that is compelling enough to sustain the no when the ache returns at two in the morning and Emily’s name is still in his phone. What I want to say to him, and what I want to carry myself, is this: the pain of losing someone is not evidence of spiritual failure. It is evidence of having loved. And God does not waste love. He collects it. He knows what to do with a man who is broken open by grief, far better than that man knows what to do with himself. The question is not whether Samuel will recover; the question is whether he will allow the Father to be the one who recovers him. ## Implications and Challenges The conversation Samuel and I shared exposes something the Western church often handles poorly: we know how to celebrate sobriety decisions when they are made at an altar call, but we are frequently underprepared to walk with a man through the long, unglamorous weeks and months that follow a decisive moment made in an ordinary phone call. Samuel said “once and for all, no more” — and that declaration deserves to be received by a community, not simply affirmed by a friend and then left unwitnessed. The challenge here is structural: if the Body of Christ cannot provide the daily accountability, the genuine brotherhood, and the honest grief-processing that Samuel needs, he will fill those spaces with whatever is available. The favela brotherhood is not the anomaly; it is the symptom of an absence. There is also a theological imprecision in how modern Christianity tends to respond to heartbreak that deserves honest attention. The advice to “get a rebound” — while coming in this case from Samuel’s father rather than from the church — is not far removed from how some Christian communities encourage the newly single: move on, trust God, stay busy, look forward. This is not the counsel of the Psalms or the prophets. Lamentations 3:20 says, “My soul still remembers and sinks within me” (NKJV) — and then, in the very next verse, finds hope not in moving on but in remembering the Lord’s mercies. The church must recover the full arc of that movement rather than rushing past the sinking to arrive prematurely at the hope. There is something worth naming in Samuel’s intuition that a rebound did not feel ethical. Many believers suppress those intuitions because they lack theological language to articulate them. The pastoral and prophetic responsibility of those who walk alongside young men is to give language to what the Spirit is already pressing — to say, “What you are feeling is a covenantal instinct; it is the image of God in you refusing to treat another person as merely a salve for your own wound.” That naming does something powerful: it validates the instinct as spiritual rather than sentimental, and it roots the ethical decision in love of God and neighbor rather than mere self-restraint. The question of belonging is perhaps the most urgent implication here. The church must ask itself, plainly: if a young man on the edge of a serious decision can find more tangible brotherhood in a chance encounter with strangers from the favelas than he can find in the local assembly of believers, what does that reveal about the quality of our κοινωνία (koy-no-NEE-ah)? This is not an accusation; it is a diagnostic question that requires honest engagement. The favela brotherhood offers structure, loyalty, protection, and identity — all of which are legitimate spiritual needs that the Body of Christ is supposed to provide in abundance and in righteousness. Where those needs go unmet, the enemy has a ready supply of counterfeit provision. Finally, I want to hold gently but clearly the pattern of the drunk message and the attempted call to Emily. Samuel named it with embarrassment, and that embarrassment is actually healthy — it is the conscience working. But the deeper issue is what he said next: “I keep thinking she’s coming back and she’s not, and I need to get okay with that.” The phrase “get okay with that” is almost unbearably honest. It is not a request for advice; it is a confession of the distance between where he is and where he needs to be. The ministry of presence in those moments is not to fix the distance but to accompany the man across it — slowly, truthfully, with the word of God and the warmth of genuine love, trusting the Holy Spirit to do what no friendship, rebound, or resolution can do by itself. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Spiritual maturity is not measured by how quickly we recover from grief but by how honestly we bring our grief before the Lord rather than before the bottle or the phone screen. For those of us walking alongside men like Samuel, maturity means resisting the impulse to resolve their pain quickly and instead learning to bear witness to it — sitting in the discomfort long enough to let the Spirit do surgery rather than applying a bandage and declaring the patient healed. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Kingdom obedience in this context begins with the decision Samuel is leaning toward: zero. No more drinking, no more smoking. That is a posture of surrender that, when brought under the Lordship of Christ rather than simply pursued as personal discipline, becomes an act of worship. Kingdom obedience also means building the kind of community where men like Samuel can say “I was flying drunk last night and I sent her another message” without shame silencing them before grace can reach them. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture this meditation calls me to maintain is one of attentiveness to what is stirring beneath the surface of ordinary conversation. Samuel asking “did it get you any closer to God?” in the middle of a conversation about Golden Gate Bridge walking times and favela encounters — that is a man whose spirit is reaching even when his circumstances are tangled. The prophetic posture is to hear that reach, to honor it, to speak to the Samuel who is becoming rather than only to the Samuel who got drunk last night. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You now not only for myself but for my brother Samuel, and for every man and woman who is, in this very season, drinking too much, missing someone too desperately, and accidentally entangling themselves in loyalties that will not hold in the day of trial. You see all of it — the drunk messages, the unanswered calls, the Brazilian strangers offering a shadow of the brotherhood that only Your Kingdom can truly provide — and You are not shocked, and You have not withdrawn. You are the God who met Hagar in the wilderness and said *I see you*, and You see Samuel now. I ask, as a co-heir of this Kingdom and a servant standing between the heavens and the earth in agreement with Your revealed will, that You would be the answer to the ache he cannot yet name — that You would make Yourself so real in his life that the bottle loses its appeal not because he is willpowering it away but because he has found something better, something true, something that satisfies the לֵב (LEV) at a depth no substance ever could. Let his grief be hallowed, not hurried. Let his decisive “no more” be a seed of consecration, watered by the Spirit and grown in community. Let the brotherhood he was created for find him, and let it be the kind whose loyalty does not depend on what he can offer in return but on the חֶסֶד (HEH-sed) of a Father who loved us while we were yet stumbling. And Lord — thank You that none of this conversation was accidental, and none of it is outside Your reach. You are faithful, and we trust You.
