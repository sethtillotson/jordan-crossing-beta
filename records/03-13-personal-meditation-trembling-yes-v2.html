# 03-06 Personal Meditation: The Trembling Yes — When Christ Calls the Unworthy # **Personal Meditation:** The Trembling Yes — When Christ Calls the Unworthy --- ## ⚡ Opening Observation I find myself sitting with the memory of a moment I never saw coming—the moment I realized I had been doing the work of discipleship without even naming it, and the Holy Spirit was now asking me to name it, to step into it deliberately. Something has been pressing on me from that secret place encounter: the absolute terror and relief of saying, "Lord, I cannot do this," and hearing Him whisper back, "That's the point." **Why This Matters:** This revelation strikes at the heart of what it means to be called into Kingdom work—not as the competent, the credentialed, or the confident, but as the trembling, the inadequate, the utterly dependent. In a church culture that often glorifies platform, polish, and performance, the Spirit is revealing something ancient and essential: the posture of childlike humility isn't just a nice spiritual accessory. It's the very doorway through which Christ's power flows. If we miss this, we miss everything. --- ## 🌍 The Worldly Mind's Interpretation From a purely human perspective, this sounds like classic imposter syndrome—the psychological phenomenon where competent people doubt their abilities and fear being exposed as frauds. The natural mind would diagnose this as a confidence issue, a need for affirmation or better training. Self-help frameworks would prescribe positive self-talk, skill-building, mentorship programs, perhaps some therapeutic work around worthiness wounds. The pragmatist would say: "You've been doing the work already. You're qualified. Just own it." **The Limitation:** The natural man cannot receive the things of the Spirit of God, for they are foolishness to him (1 Corinthians 2:14, NKJV). What looks like a confidence deficit to the world is actually the threshold of true spiritual authority. The worldly mind sees inadequacy as something to overcome; the Spirit sees it as the precise qualification for usefulness. Human wisdom says, "Believe in yourself." Kingdom wisdom says, "Stop believing in yourself—that's what's been in the way all along." The secular framework has no category for a calling that begins with trembling, no vocabulary for a commissioning that sounds like, "I can't do this," and hears back, "Good. Now we can start." --- ## ✝️ What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation I am seeing something stunning in this moment of transparency before the Lord—this moment when I finally stopped performing and just spilled everything at His feet like a child who doesn't know how to fix anything but knows exactly where to bring broken things. The Greek word for this kind of confession is *ἐξομολογέω* (exomologeō)—not just admitting facts, but a full outpouring, a public declaration of utter dependence. It's the same word used when creation will one day confess Christ as Lord (Philippians 2:11), but here it's happening in the secret place, in the *κατ᾽ ἰδίαν* (kat' idian)—that sacred alone-time where masks fall off and pretense dies. I notice something arresting in the progression of this prayer: "Lord, I need you to confirm... I can't do it alone... I am not moving until you go before me... I don't know anything... I barely know your word... I don't feel worthy." This isn't false humility or self-deprecation. This is *πραΰτης* (prautēs)—the meekness that Moses carried (Numbers 12:3), the gentleness that Jesus promised would inherit the earth (Matthew 5:5). It's strength under control, yes, but more than that—it's strength that has finally stopped trying to control anything and has surrendered the reins entirely. The Hebrew concept behind this is *עָנָו* (anav), which doesn't mean weak or timid; it means someone who has been broken and rebuilt by God, someone who has learned that their greatest strength is their dependence. As I sit with this passage, I am struck by the echo of John's vision in Revelation 5. When the angel cries out, "Who is worthy to open the scroll and to loose its seals?" (Revelation 5:2, NKJV), the word *ἄξιος* (axios) means worthy in the sense of equivalent weight—who has the gravitas, the substance, the authority to handle what God has sealed? And John weeps because no one—*οὐδεὶς* (oudeis), absolutely no one—is found worthy. Not the competent. Not the educated. Not the experienced. No one. Until the Lamb appears, looking as though it had been slain. Only He. Only ever Him. Something is being revealed here about the nature of calling itself. The evangelical industrial complex has taught us that calling requires qualification—degrees, ordination, years of study, personal holiness metrics, platform-building. But the Spirit is showing me that the only true qualification is the same one that qualified the Twelve: "They had been with Jesus" (Acts 4:13). That's it. Not that they had mastered theology. Not that they were sinless. Not that they felt ready. They had been *with* Him. The Greek *σὺν αὐτῷ* (syn autō) means more than proximity—it means companionship, intimacy, shared life. This is the Living Tapestry at work: discipleship isn't a curriculum; it's a relationship that transforms from the inside out until your very posture—what you think, what you watch, what you listen to, how you speak—begins to bear His image. I am seeing now why the prayer had to begin with "I can't do it." Because the entire scaffolding of Western Christianity is built on a subtle lie: that God helps those who help themselves. That He equips the called, meaning He upgrades our natural capacities so we can perform Kingdom tasks with our own strength. But that's not what Scripture shows us. Moses said, "I am slow of speech and slow of tongue" (Exodus 4:10, NKJV), and God didn't fix his stutter—He sent Aaron and then told Moses, "I will be with your mouth" (Exodus 4:12). Gideon was threshing wheat in a winepress, hiding from enemies, calling himself the least of the least (Judges 6:15), and the Angel of the Lord called him a mighty warrior—not because of what Gideon was, but because of what God intended to do *through* the weak. Paul had a thorn in the flesh, begged for its removal, and heard, "My grace is sufficient for you, for My strength is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9, NKJV). The word *τελειοῦται* (teleioutai) means brought to completion, to fullness, to mature purpose—God's strength doesn't just work *despite* our weakness; it reaches its *full potential* precisely in our weakness. This is the schism of revelation at work. To the hardened heart, to the one who insists on self-sufficiency, this teaching is offensive. "You're saying I'm not good enough?" Yes. Exactly. None of us are. But to the receptive heart, the one broken open by grace, this is breathtaking freedom: "You mean I don't have to be good enough? You mean the weight of souls doesn't rest on my competence?" Correct. The entire burden shifts. "It is not you that saves, it's Me," the Spirit whispers. This demolishes the evangelical anxiety machine that keeps ministers striving, performing, measuring, burning out. The Greek word for striving is *ἐρίζω* (erizō)—to quarrel, to compete, to strive contentiously. But Jesus said, "He will not quarrel nor cry out" (Matthew 12:19, NKJV), quoting Isaiah's Servant Song. The Messiah doesn't strive. He abides. He obeys. He rests in the Father's timing and the Father's power. Something arrests my attention in the metaphor of the billion-dollar debt. This isn't hypothetical—this *is* the gospel. The word *ὀφείλημα* (opheilēma) in Matthew 6:12 means a debt, something owed that cannot be repaid. We didn't just sin a little; we accumulated an infinite debt against an infinitely holy God. And Christ didn't negotiate a payment plan—He *paid it in full*. *Τετέλεσται* (tetelestai)—"It is finished" (John 19:30). Paid in full. So when He later asks, "Read My book. Teach My sheep. Go where I send you," is that even a question? The natural response isn't obligation in the grudging sense—it's the glad obedience of someone who knows they are loved beyond measure. It's the Apostle Paul writing, "The love of Christ compels us" (2 Corinthians 5:14, NKJV)—the word *συνέχει* (synechei) means to hold together, to press upon, to constrain. We aren't driven by guilt or duty; we are held together by love so massive that refusing to respond would be like refusing to breathe. I notice the warning about teachers: "Let not many of you become teachers, knowing that we shall receive a stricter judgment" (James 3:1, NKJV). The Greek *κρίμα* (krima) means a judicial decision, a verdict. Teachers bear more responsibility—*βαρύτερον* (baryteron), a heavier weight—precisely because words shape eternity. And yet, this heavier responsibility is itself a gift. Why? Because it drives us back to dependence. The teacher who knows the weight of the calling won't add to or take away from Scripture. They won't freelance. They won't trust their own cleverness. They will tremble, check, recheck, test the spirits, and beg the Holy Spirit to guard every word. This is the opposite of the casual Bible teacher who wings it, who prioritizes charisma over precision, who values entertainment over truth. The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom (Proverbs 9:10), and that fear—*יִרְאָה* (yirah)—is not terror but reverent awe that keeps us humble, dependent, listening. The Spirit is revealing something wild about abiding versus striving. Jesus said, "Abide in Me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, unless it abides in the vine, neither can you, unless you abide in Me" (John 15:4, NKJV). The word *μείνατε* (meinate) means to remain, to stay, to dwell continuously. It's not an event; it's a posture. The branch doesn't *try* to produce grapes—it simply stays connected, receives life, and fruit happens as a natural overflow. But Western Christianity has turned this into a productivity model: "How many did you save this year? How many did you disciple? What's your growth strategy?" All striving. All self-reliance dressed in religious language. But Kingdom work doesn't function like that. Kingdom work is *response*. It's obedience to what the Spirit is already doing. It's showing up when He says show up, speaking when He says speak, and being silent when He says wait. One step at a time. Focus on what's in front of you. I am struck by the imagery of the Holy Spirit as persistent annoyance. Not conviction-as-condemnation, but a gentle, relentless, "Read your Bible." This is *makrothumia* (μακροθυμία)—the patience of God, His long-suffering that doesn't give up. Romans 2:4 says it's the *χρηστότης* (chrēstotēs), the kindness of God, that leads to repentance—not shame, not guilt, but relentless, patient kindness. The Spirit pursued until the heart was receptive enough to hear. That's the scandalous grace of God: He doesn't wait for us to clean up our act first. He meets us in the mess, speaks into the chaos, and slowly, gently, persistently draws us toward truth. And when we finally respond—even if it's just reading one verse—He celebrates. He builds. He reveals. The progression from spirit to mind to words is shattering in its complexity. The Spirit downloads understanding directly—*γνῶσις* (gnōsis) that bypasses the intellect initially because spiritual things are spiritually discerned (1 Corinthians 2:13-14). It's not first processed through worldly wisdom; it drops into the heart, the *καρδία* (kardia), the center of personhood. Then comes the struggle to translate it into the mind—*νοῦς* (nous)—and finally into *λόγος* (logos), communicable words. This is why true prophetic speech is so difficult and why much of what passes for prophecy today is shallow: it skips the wrestling, the translation work, the careful stewarding of revelation. Real revelation is hard-won. It requires time in the secret place. It requires humility to say, "I don't fully understand this yet." It requires patience to wait until the Lord clarifies before speaking. The final revelation about atmosphere and territory is breathtaking. When we enter a room, we can sense *in the Spirit* what is reigning there. This is spiritual discernment—*διάκρισις* (diakrisis)—the ability to distinguish between spirits (1 Corinthians 12:10). We feel heaviness or lightness, oppression or freedom, the Spirit's presence or His absence. And critically, *what comes out of the mouth* reveals what's in the heart. Jesus said, "Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks" (Matthew 12:34, NKJV). The Greek *περίσσευμα* (perisseuma) means overflow, surplus—whatever fills the heart to overflowing will spill out, especially under pressure. This is why Jesus said, "What comes out of the mouth, this defiles a man" (Matthew 15:11, NKJV). Not what goes in—external religious performance. What comes *out*—the unguarded word, the reflexive response, the thing you say when you're tired or stressed. That's the diagnostic. And instead of hiding it, instead of managing our image, we bring it to the secret place. We let Jesus judge it. We let Him execute the unclean thing. We steward the revelation, trusting that He alone can transform the heart. --- ## 🤔 Implications and Challenges Beloved friends and fellow laborers in Christ, what the Spirit is uncovering here carries profound implications for how we understand calling, ministry, and spiritual authority in the Body. I share these not as accusations but as invitations—gentle corrections born from Scripture, offered in the same spirit of trembling dependence that marked this meditation. **First:** Much of modern evangelical culture has built an entire infrastructure around the idea that calling requires human qualification—seminary degrees, ordination processes, personality assessments, ministry training programs. While education and preparation have their place, Scripture consistently shows God calling the disqualified, the stammering, the hiding, the weak. If we make human credentials the gatekeeper to Kingdom work, we risk sidelining the very people the Spirit delights to use. The question isn't, "Are you qualified?" but "Have you been with Jesus?" (Acts 4:13). **Second:** The doctrine of "God helps those who help themselves" is nowhere in Scripture—it's Benjamin Franklin, not the Bible. Yet it has so infiltrated our thinking that we've turned discipleship into self-improvement and sanctification into a performance metric. The Spirit reveals here that God's strength is *made perfect* in weakness (2 Corinthians 12:9), meaning our inadequacy isn't a problem to solve but the very condition in which His power operates best. If we keep trying to fix our weakness, we may inadvertently push His strength away. **Third:** The evangelical emphasis on evangelistic striving—measuring success by conversions, attendance, growth—runs counter to the abiding, responsive posture Jesus modeled. "The Son can do nothing of Himself, but what He sees the Father do" (John 5:19, NKJV). If Jesus, the incarnate Son of God, operated in total dependence and only did what the Father showed Him, how much more should we? The call is not to strive but to abide, not to manufacture results but to faithfully steward what He assigns. **Fourth:** The fear of stricter judgment for teachers (James 3:1) should produce reverence, not paralysis—but it should definitely produce *something*. In an age of casual Bible teaching, where charisma often trumps accuracy and entertainment value overshadows theological precision, the Spirit is calling us back to the weight of the Word. Adding to or taking away from Scripture (Deuteronomy 4:2; Revelation 22:18-19) isn't just an Old Testament warning—it's a perpetual standard. Every teacher, every leader, every discipler must tremble at this and hold the text with holy carefulness. **Fifth:** The Western church's discomfort with dependence—our cultural idolatry of self-sufficiency—has seeped into our theology until we've created a Christianity that looks more like Stoic resilience than cruciform surrender. But the cross isn't about pulling yourself together; it's about dying so Christ can live through you (Galatians 2:20). This isn't weakness. This is the scandalous power of the gospel. And if it makes us uncomfortable, perhaps that discomfort is the Holy Spirit performing the surgery Scripture promises—cutting first, then healing (Hebrews 4:12). Friends, these corrections come with deep affection. Priscilla and Aquila took Apollos aside—not publicly, not with condemnation—and "explained to him the way of God more accurately" (Acts 18:26, NKJV). That's the spirit here. An invitation to reconsider. An invitation to return to the simplicity and purity of devotion to Christ (2 Corinthians 11:3). May we receive it with the same childlike humility this meditation reveals. --- ## 🚀 What This Means For Us: **For Your Spiritual Maturity:** This revelation calls us to stop hiding our inadequacy and start confessing it—not as defeat but as the doorway to God's power. Spiritual maturity isn't arriving at competence; it's arriving at deeper dependence. Every time you feel "I can't do this," that's not a sign you're disqualified. That's the threshold. Walk through it trembling, but walk through. **For Kingdom Obedience:** Obedience looks like showing up when He says show up, even when you don't feel ready. It looks like one step at a time, focusing on what's in front of you, refusing to add or subtract from His instructions. It looks like testing the spirits, checking your heart, stewarding revelation carefully, and bringing every unguarded word back to the secret place for His judgment. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture isn't the confident declaration from the platform. It's the trembling yes whispered in the secret place. It's the abiding branch that doesn't strive to produce fruit but stays connected and lets overflow happen naturally. It's the teacher who fears the weight of words enough to handle them with reverence. It's the disciple-maker who knows they save no one but points everyone to the only One who can. --- ## 🔥 A Closing Prayer Lord, I come back to this place again—the place where I spill everything and You hold it all without flinching. Thank You for not requiring my competence. Thank You for calling the inadequate, the stammering, the trembling. Thank You for making Your strength perfect in the exact places I am weakest. Forgive us, Father, for the ways we've turned Your gospel of dependence into a gospel of striving. Forgive us for the ways we've measured Kingdom work by human metrics. Teach us to abide. Teach us to listen. Teach us to obey one step at a time, trusting that You go before us and You walk with us and You've already prepared the works You've called us to walk in. Brothers and sisters, may we together lay down the crushing weight of self-sufficiency and take up the easy yoke of childlike dependence. May we steward what He entrusts with holy carefulness. May we remember: only the Lamb is worthy. And in Him, by His blood, we are made worthy not by our works but by His grace. All glory, all honor, all power to the One who sits on the throne and to the Lamb forever and ever. Amen. --- ## 📝 Scripture References & Original Language Notes | Passage (NKJV) | Greek / Hebrew / Aramaic | Theological Insight | | --- | --- | --- | | 1 Corinthians 2:14 | φυσικὸς ἄνθρωπος (physikos anthrōpos) - "natural man" | Describes one operating purely from human wisdom without the Spirit's illumination; contrasts the *pneumatikos* (spiritual) person. | | Philippians 2:11 | ἐξομολογέω (exomologeō) - "confess openly" | Full outpouring and public declaration; used for both confession of sin and confession of Christ's lordship. | | Matthew 5:5 | πραεῖς (praeis) - "meek/gentle" | Strength under God's control; not weakness but power submitted to divine authority.
