# 08-22 Call Reflection: Suffering, Peace, and a Father’s Cancer Diagnosis **Title:** 08-22 at 18:36 — I’ll Catch You on the Other Side **Recorded:** Not provided **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation I called my brother tonight carrying news I could barely speak: my earthly father, Michael, has cancer, and the words for it were a wilderness — pancreas, bones, glands, Mayo Clinic, eight weeks of radiation. My body was already broken from a week of welding, burns swelling my left hand past the knuckles, and now my heart was breaking too. And somehow, in the middle of both breakings, I heard myself say I had never suffered more and never been more at peace. That contradiction is the whole meditation. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Church in the comfortable West has quietly taught its people that peace is the absence of suffering, that a good God shields His children from the cup rather than handing it to them. But the testimony of the whole canon, and the testimony that came out of my own mouth on that call, is that peace and agony can occupy the same heart at the same hour without canceling each other. If I flatten this into either despair or false triumph, I lose the actual gift the Holy Spirit is pressing into me: the fellowship of Christ’s sufferings, the man of sorrows who is Himself the peace that passes understanding. Every disciple eventually stands where I am standing — asked whether they will drink the cup — and how we answer shapes whether we merely believe in Christ or actually live from His indwelling life. This is discipleship at its rawest edge, and it is worth attending to slowly. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation Seen through natural reasoning, this is simply a man overwhelmed. A young husband, expecting his first child, is stretched past his limits — a father dying, a body burned and bruised from a brutal new job, seven dollars of stress in a friend’s account, a struggling business, cats getting kicked out of a room because there was nowhere left to put the pressure. The tears every day in intercession look, to the natural mind, like a nervous system under too much load. The talk of God “preparing” him with a fast, the plan to forbid anyone from touching his father’s body so the Lord can raise him, the anger at “big pharma” as temple-desecrators — all of it reads as the psychology of a man reaching for meaning and control while the ground gives way. Even the peace can be explained as a coping mechanism, the mind’s merciful buffer against a blow it cannot yet absorb. The natural eye sees a strong person cracking, and it is not entirely wrong about the cracking. **The Limitation:** But natural reasoning can only measure the load; it cannot weigh the One who carries it with me. It can see the tears and diagnose them as strain, yet it has no category for tears offered as intercession, no instrument to detect the grief of the Holy Spirit being shared with a willing vessel. It looks at a fast I could not explain and calls it disordered eating; it cannot perceive that I was being braced by heaven for news I did not yet know was coming. The flesh reads the peace as denial because it has never met a peace that is a Person, and it reads the anger as bitterness because it has never seen the zeal of the Lord’s house consume a son. What is actually happening here is spiritually discerned, and the mind of the flesh, for all its compassion, stands outside the door of the secret place where the real transaction occurs. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I keep returning to the sound of the call itself — the Wi-Fi stuttering, my brother apologizing for the connection, and then the flat weight of my own voice saying *my earthly father has cancer*. There was nothing dramatic in how I said it. He is not a man who gets emotional, and I had inherited enough of that to deliver the worst news of my year without theatrics. But underneath the calm delivery my left hand was throbbing, swollen past the knuckles from arc-welding burns, and I remember thinking that my body and my heart were failing in exactly the same week. The physical and the spiritual grief had fused into one ache that I could not separate even if I tried. What arrests me, as I listen back to it, is the sentence I spoke to my wife the night before and repeated on the call: *I’ve never suffered more in my life, and yet I’ve never been more blessed.* I did not construct that as a nice paradox to comfort anyone. It came out of me because it was simply true, and the truth of it frightened me a little. I said *I feel at peace*, and my brother named it before I could — *that’s the Holy Spirit.* Something in me recognized that the peace was not mine to manufacture and not mine to lose; it had been set inside me like ballast before the storm was announced. And that is when the first Scripture opened quietly under the whole conversation. *Peace I leave with you, My peace I give to you; not as the world gives do I give to you* (John 14:27). The world gives peace by removing the trouble; Christ gives peace by giving Himself in the middle of the trouble that remains. I had been handed the cup — a dying father, a burned body, a fragile business, a lonely brother on the other end of the line — and none of it was taken away, yet the peace was real. The world’s peace is subtraction. His peace is Presence. The word beneath that promise deepens it. The peace He names is His own — εἰρήνη (eirēnē, ay-RAY-nay) — peace, wholeness, the state of being knit together rather than torn apart. But behind the Greek stands the Hebrew He would have breathed, שָׁלוֹם (shalom, shah-LOME) — completeness, welfare, nothing missing and nothing broken. That is the astonishing thing: I could speak of שָׁלוֹם with a body that was literally broken and burned, because the wholeness He gives is not the wholeness of my circumstances but the integrity of a life hidden in Him. The burns on my hand and the shalom in my chest were both true at once, and only His kind of peace could hold them together. I hear this thread running the whole length of Scripture. Job, stripped of children and health, falling down not to curse but to worship: *the LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD* (Job 1:21). I told my brother plainly that this would not destroy my faith, that *we’ll still bless His name, I know God is still good.* That is Job’s confession pulled forward into a farmhouse with a snack drawer and a broken laptop charger. The shadow in the Old Testament — a righteous sufferer who blesses the Name from the ash heap — finds its substance in the Son who, in the garden, prayed *not My will, but Yours, be done* (Luke 22:42). When I said *nevertheless, I’ll drink the cup He gives me*, I did not realize I was echoing Gethsemane until now. There is a real theological tension I have to sit inside honestly. I said on that call that if my father goes through the radiation and it fails, I don’t know how I will ever forgive the world — *these lying, greedy people* — and I compared my anger to the anger Jesus held toward those who desecrated the temple (John 2:15–17). Some of that is righteous zeal; the Lord’s house was made a den of thieves and He did not smile at it. But I also feel the Spirit pressing a caution into me that I could not fully receive in the heat of the call. The same Christ who overturned tables also prayed *Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do* (Luke 23:34). My brother saw it more clearly than I did when he said *guilt is heavier than forgiveness; that’s why we should forgive.* The zeal is real, but if it hardens into unforgiveness it becomes the very thing I warned my father about — strength curdled into a hardened heart. Because that was the other word I spoke, almost prophetically against myself. When my brother called me one of the strongest people he knows, I said *strength can turn into a hardened heart.* I said it about my father, who told me he didn’t think weeping daily in intercession was healthy for me. But the Spirit turns that mirror back on me. The tears my brother blessed — *your tears come from love, and that’s what matters* — are the safeguard against my own strength becoming stone. A man who can still weep in intercession cannot yet be hardened, and the gift of bearing the Father’s grief is precisely what keeps my zeal from becoming venom. Here the Kingdom mystery uncovers itself, the thing the natural mind would never guess: the fast came before the news. I told my brother *I didn’t know why, but I was fasting this week,* and only yesterday ate a full meal again — and now I see it was *preparation, so this news didn’t crush me.* The Spirit was setting the ballast before the swell. This is how the Father shepherds His own; He braces us for a blow we cannot foresee, so that when it lands we discover we were already being held. The fast I could not explain was the mercy I could not yet see, and the peace I felt was the fruit of an obedience I did not understand while I was obeying it. And then there is the exchange that undoes me every time I return to it — the way my father and I ended our call. He said, *I’ll catch you on the other side,* and I said, *I’ll see you there.* Whether he meant Mayo Clinic or the resurrection, the words became a threshold. My whole posture toward his death is built on that threshold: I told my mother, if he passes, let no one touch him until I arrive, because *the Lord can raise him — I don’t just believe it, I know it.* I do not want to correct my own faith here, but I want to hold it before the Lord honestly. Whether or not He raises Michael’s body in that room, the *other side* my father named is the very resurrection Christ secured, and my confidence must rest finally in the empty tomb rather than in a miracle I demand. For the resurrection is where my faith actually stands, and it is where the man of sorrows becomes the risen Lord. I told my brother about seeing Him — at Golgotha, then in the upper room, still bearing the holes and yet restored — saying *wipe your tears, I told you I would rise on the third day.* My weeping over what they did to Him becomes weeping *for other things,* and He meets both with the same command and the same wound. We forget, I said, the entirety of Scripture that speaks of Him as *the man of sorrows and acquainted with grief* (Isaiah 53:3). The One who tells me to wipe my tears is the One who wept at Lazarus’s tomb knowing He would raise him. He does not despise my grief; He shares it, and then He answers it with His own scarred, living hands. My own encounter in all of this is a kind of surrender I have never made so completely. When my brother said he wished he could be half the man of faith I am, I answered honestly: *I wish I wasn’t so strong; some of these burdens I don’t want.* That is not false humility — it is Gethsemane again, the human recoil from the cup that is nevertheless swallowed. I confessed to him what I have long believed: that every true disciple is eventually asked *would you die for Me?*, and that if we are honest the answer is usually *no* — until He does the transformation and *you come out of that encounter already dead, and Christ lives in you* (Galatians 2:20; Colossians 3:3–4). Tonight I am living the cost of that death, and I would not trade the indwelling life for a lighter cross. What this means for the Body pressed on me all through the call, because the whole thing was two brothers who have never met carrying each other through marriages, divorces, dying fathers, and seven-dollar bank accounts. My brother said *we speak about Him like He’s not here,* and I answered *every time we speak of Him, He’s here* — *where two or three are gathered in My name, I am there in the midst of them* (Matthew 18:20). That is the communion of the saints made concrete: he is lonely in a new house across an ocean, I am burned and grieving in an apartment, and Christ stood in the middle of a glitchy phone call and made it holy ground. And when he made me promise to return to my secret place before I lose my temper with my pregnant wife, he was doing the office of a brother — *exhorting one another daily* (Hebrews 3:13) — guarding my marriage before it could crack. That is finally what I want the brethren to see in the way I handle conflict at home, because I said it out loud tonight and I want to be held to it. When my wife and I begin to argue, I walk away — not in cold avoidance but *I come in here and I pray and repent for not honoring my wife, and then I go back and apologize.* But the order is everything: *before I even apologize to her, I apologize to my Lord.* Most relationships deteriorate because each party waits for the other to move first; the disciple breaks the deadlock by moving first toward Christ. And the fruit is uncanny — *most of the time, by the time I go out there, she’s apologizing to me first* — because we both remember the fight was never against each other (Ephesians 6:12). Good communication in marriage is not the absence of fighting; it is fighting, and then refusing to let the fight metastasize into resentment, looking in the mirror to see who we are really wrestling. ## Implications and Challenges **The comfortable gospel cannot hold this.** Much of Western Christianity has been trained to expect that faith produces relief — that if I pray hard enough, the cancer lifts, the burns heal, the money arrives. My call testifies to something the health-and-comfort framework cannot metabolize: that the Lord handed me the cup *and* the peace in the same breath, and never promised to remove the first in order to give the second. The challenge to the beloved brethren is to stop measuring God’s goodness by the absence of suffering. Job blessed the Name from the ash heap; I blessed it from a burned hand and a dying father. If our faith only survives when circumstances improve, it is not yet the faith of Gethsemane. **Zeal must not be allowed to harden into unforgiveness.** I meant what I said about the anger toward those who profit from lies, and there is a righteous edge to it that mirrors the cleansing of the temple. But my brother’s words — *guilt is heavier than forgiveness* — and the caution of the Spirit press on me here with real weight. If my father’s treatment fails and I let my grief crystallize into *I’ll never forgive the world*, I will have become the hardened heart I warned him against. The correction is sharp because I need it sharp: righteous anger that refuses to travel toward *Father, forgive them* is no longer righteous. The brethren who carry justified anger must guard it daily, lest zeal become the very poison Christ came to draw out. **Faith for resurrection must rest on the empty tomb, not on a demanded miracle.** I told my mother to let no one touch my father’s body so the Lord can raise him, and I said I *know* it, not merely believe it. I do not want to shame that faith, and I will not — but I want to hold it in the light. The temptation, even for the most fervent, is to fasten our hope to a specific outcome and call that hope faith. Yet Christ’s resurrection has already secured the *other side* my father named; my confidence must finally rest there, so that whether the Lord raises Michael’s body in that room or receives his spirit into glory, my faith stands unshaken on Christ risen. Faith that survives only if God performs the miracle I scripted is faith placed in the miracle, not in the Man. **Intercessory tears are a gift the productive age despises.** My father, a strong and unemotional man, told me weeping every day in intercession was not healthy. The world — and much of the modern Church that has adopted the world’s stoic efficiency — agrees with him. But I told my brother the truth: *not everybody can bear the weight of the Father’s grief, and to be given that is an invitation to intercede.* The challenge is that a Christianity optimized for productivity and emotional composure has no place for the ministry of tears (Psalm 126:5–6; Romans 8:26). The brethren should not medicate away the grief the Spirit shares with them; they should offer it back as intercession. **The communion of saints is not optional infrastructure — it is where Christ appears.** Two men who have never met in the flesh carried each other tonight through the worst news of the year, and Christ stood in the middle of the call. This rebukes the isolated, self-sufficient discipleship that treats fellowship as a nice addition to a private faith. My brother forbade me from taking my pregnant wife’s irritation personally and commanded me back to my secret place; that is not intrusion, it is the Body functioning (Galatians 6:2). The saints who try to endure alone are attempting something Scripture never asked of them. Where two or three gather in His name — even over a stuttering Wi-Fi connection — He is there, and He is not there merely to be discussed but to be present. **Repentance to God must precede reconciliation with people.** I told my brother that I apologize to the Lord before I ever apologize to my wife, and that the fruit of it is a marriage that heals instead of festering. This challenges the therapeutic model that treats conflict resolution as a horizontal negotiation between two parties keeping score. The disciple breaks the cycle not by winning and not merely by conceding, but by going vertical first — repenting to Christ for failing to love as He commanded (Ephesians 5:25). My brother’s honest confession, that with Emily his apologies lost their weight because he apologized without believing he was wrong, exposes the counterfeit: reconciliation that skips repentance is manipulation. The brethren are called to move first toward the Lord, and let Him reorder the room before they enter it. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This changes how I understand strength itself. I have been called strong all my life, and tonight I confessed that I wish I weren’t, because some of these burdens I would never choose. Maturity is not the capacity to feel less pain; it is the capacity to hold agony and peace together without letting either one lie about God. The mature disciple learns that the fast before the news, the tears in the secret place, the burned hand and the shalom in the chest are not contradictions to be resolved but the very shape of a life hidden in Christ. We grow up not by escaping the cup but by drinking it while still blessing the Name. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this calls forth is concrete and immediate. It is going to lay hands on my father next weekend even though *that’s about all I can do*, because obedience does what it can and leaves the rest to God. It is walking away from an argument to repent before I apologize. It is receiving the gift of intercessory grief instead of medicating it, and refusing to let righteous anger curdle into unforgiveness. It is being honest with Jaden and Jade about money we do not have, because integrity — *delivering on whatever we promised them* — is itself an act of worship. Obedience, here, is small, daily, and costly, and it is exactly what the Kingdom is built from. **For Prophetic Posture:** This trains me, and the saints with me, to watch for the Lord’s preparation before we understand it. I was fasting and did not know why; He was bracing me for a blow I could not foresee. The prophetic posture is not primarily foretelling the future — it is discerning the Father’s hand in the present, blessing His name from the ash heap, and interceding through tears for those still living whom we can still reach. My brother said *it’s insane how far we’ll drive for those who have passed, but we won’t have to go that far for those we can still save.* That is the watchman’s heart: to abide in the secret place, to carry the Father’s grief, and to move toward the living with the urgency the dying have taught us. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come to You as a son who has been handed a cup he did not ask for, and I bless Your name from the middle of it. I thank You that before I knew the news was coming, You were already fasting me for it, bracing me with a peace I could not manufacture and cannot lose. I lift my earthly father Michael before Your throne — touch his prostate, his bones, his body, and above all draw him into the secret place he promised to seek, that suffering would become the very doorway to a direct encounter with the living Christ. I confess the anger in me toward those who profit from lies, and I ask You to keep that zeal from hardening into the stone heart I fear; teach me, even here, to pray *Father, forgive them.* Guard my marriage as I move first toward You before I move toward my wife, and thank You for the child You are knitting who will one day look into these tears and see they came from love. I thank You for my brother across the ocean, for the fellowship that made a glitching phone call into holy ground, and I ask You to provide for him down to the seven dollars and the broken charger. As one of Your servants making agreement with Your will between the heavens and the earth, I hold the resurrection before You — not demanding my scripted miracle, but resting on the empty tomb where my father and I will one day catch each other on the other side. Man of Sorrows, You who told me to wipe my tears because You had risen, wipe them again tonight, and let Christ live in me who is already dead. To You be all glory, from glory to glory, forever.
