# 07-20 Personal Meditation: Blood, Covenant, and the Mystery of New Life **Title:** 07-20 at 14:57 — The Blood We Do Not Yet Know **Recorded:** 2026-07-20 14:57:18 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Personal Meditation ## Opening Observation A simple question about blood type — asked by my wife in the ordinary rhythm of a morning commute — carried more weight than either of us paused to notice in the moment. We do not know our blood types. And there is a baby coming whose blood will carry the mystery of both of ours. Something in that small, practical exchange is pressing on me now as something far deeper than a medical checklist item. **Why This Matters:** There is a reason blood runs as one of the most theologically charged substances in all of Scripture. From Abel’s blood crying out from the ground, to the blood of the Passover lamb brushed across doorposts, to the blood of the Eternal Covenant poured out at Calvary, blood is never merely biological in the biblical imagination. It speaks of life, of identity, of covenant, of inheritance. When Gladys asked me what my blood type was and neither of us could answer, I heard beneath that ordinary question an invitation to sit with a profound spiritual reality: that there are things about our own nature — our origins, our composition, our very life-substance — that we carry without fully knowing. A child is coming. And that child will bear, in their very blood, a heritage neither parent can fully trace or define. This is not a problem to solve before a hospital visit. This is a parable of how life — and new life in particular — always arrives bearing mysteries larger than our own self-knowledge. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation From the vantage point of purely natural reasoning, this exchange is unremarkable and the reflection it stirs is easily dismissed. Blood type is a medical data point — A, B, AB, or O; positive or negative — known at birth and retrievable with a simple lab order. The world would say: schedule the test, record the result, file it in the prenatal chart, and move on. There is nothing mysterious here, only a gap in personal health records to be filled. The anticipation around a coming child, in the natural mind, resolves into preparation checklists, nursery logistics, insurance paperwork, and parenting books. The not-knowing is simply an oversight, not an invitation. The conversation in the car becomes background noise in the machinery of responsible adult preparation, and the tenderness of “Love you — Love you” at the end of a call is sweet but unremarkable, a social pleasantry of a functional marriage. The natural mind is efficient, and efficiency tends to flatten the sacred into the procedural. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind, for all its efficiency, is constitutionally unable to hear what is being whispered beneath the surface of ordinary moments. It can measure blood but it cannot hear blood speak. It can record a blood type but it cannot perceive that blood is, in the language of Scripture, the very seat of life — נֶפֶשׁ (nephesh, NEH-fesh), the living soul, carried in the blood according to Leviticus 17:11: *“For the life of the flesh is in the blood.”* The natural mind does not ask why a God who needed no blood chose to ratify every major covenant in history through it. It does not wonder why the Son of God, who was life itself, poured that life out through a body that bled. The gap in our medical records is a small thing to the natural mind. To the spirit that is listening, it becomes a doorway. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It I am sitting with the smallness of this moment and finding it unexpectedly wide. Gladys asked me something simple, and I did not know the answer. Neither did she. We laughed — *okay lang*, it’s alright — and moved on into the morning. But I cannot quite move on entirely, because I keep hearing the reason she asked: *it’s essential to find out for the baby’s blood type.* A new life is coming, and that life will carry something of me and something of her, woven together in ways that no genetic counselor can fully predict and no blood test can fully interpret. The child will be their own person, bearing their own blood type, which may or may not match either parent’s. And yet that child will belong to us, will be ours, will be a covenant expression of a union that was itself sealed before God. This is where Scripture begins to press in on me. In Psalm 139, David declares that God knit him together in his mother’s womb — the Hebrew word סָכַךְ (sakak, saw-KAKH), meaning to weave or to interweave, to cover and enclose with intentional craftsmanship. The child Gladys carries is already being woven. That weaving is not random. Blood type, bone structure, temperament, the particular shape of a soul — none of it is accidental in the economy of God. The fact that two parents do not yet know their own blood types does not slow the work of the One who knew this child before the foundation of the world. *“Before I formed you in the womb I knew you”* — Jeremiah 1:5. The knowledge of God precedes the data of man. This child is known before they are measurable. But I want to stay with the not-knowing a little longer, because I think the Spirit is doing something in it. There is a grace in not knowing everything about yourself. Gladys and I have been married, have lived together, have built a life — and we do not know something as basic as our own blood types. This is not negligence; it is simply the honest condition of embodied life. We carry more than we have catalogued. We are more than we have measured. And this is equally true spiritually. Paul writes in 1 Corinthians 13:12, *“For now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face. Now I know in part, but then I shall know just as I also am known.”* There is a partial-knowing that is the honest condition of this side of eternity. The baby coming will be a mystery to us in ways we cannot yet anticipate, and we ourselves remain partial mysteries — to each other and, in some genuine sense, to ourselves. What arrests me, though, is the covenant context of blood in Scripture. Blood is not neutral. It speaks. Abel’s blood cried out from the ground — Genesis 4:10 — and God heard it. The author of Hebrews draws a stunning contrast in chapter 12, verses 24, noting that the blood of Jesus *“speaks better things than that of Abel.”* Abel’s blood cried for justice, for reckoning, for a response to violation. The blood of the New Covenant speaks something more — it speaks peace, reconciliation, access, and inheritance. The word used in Hebrews for “speaks” is λαλέω (laleō, lah-LEH-oh) — not merely to make sound, but to communicate, to be in living discourse. The blood of Christ is not silent. It is presently speaking. And every time a new child enters the world through the blood and water of birth — echoing, perhaps not accidentally, the blood and water that poured from the side of Christ at Calvary — something of that speaking covenant is being enacted again in the theater of creation. I think about the Passover. The blood on the doorpost was not there for the family inside to see — it was there for the angel of death to see, and for God to see: *“When I see the blood, I will pass over you”* — Exodus 12:13. The blood was the sign of covenant protection. It spoke on behalf of those sheltered beneath it. And I wonder, quietly, whether the blood of this coming child — unknown blood type, mystery carried in the womb — is already covered by a blood more ancient and more authoritative than any laboratory result. Before the first breath, before the cord is cut, before the birth certificate is filed, the blood of the Lamb has already spoken over this life. This is not sentimental theology. This is the logic of covenant inheritance that runs from Abraham through the Passover through Calvary into the New Covenant household of God. There is something deeply covenantal about the question itself — Gladys asking me about blood, with a child between us, while I am still on the way. The scene is almost typological. She is waiting — literally waiting on her Uber, waiting in the threshold of the day — and I am not yet where I am going. *I’m not even to Castleton yet.* And in between us, this unborn mystery. The New Covenant people of God are always in this posture: the promise has been made, the blood has been shed, and we are between the already and the not yet — not yet at the destination, but moving toward it, with something holy being formed in the meantime. Hebrews 10:19 speaks of the boldness we have to enter the Holiest by the blood of Jesus — the word for blood there is αἷμα (haima, HY-mah), the very substance of life poured out and now the substance of our access. We enter by blood. We are sustained by blood. And new life comes through blood. The ease of the conversation also strikes me. There is no panic in not knowing. We laugh — *it’s another mystery, wow, so fun* — and the lightness of that response is not flippancy. I think it is the natural posture of people who have learned, in some measure, to hold the unknown without terror. This is itself a spiritual formation. The opposite of faith is not doubt; it is the compulsive need to have all variables resolved before moving forward. Gladys and I are moving forward — into the day, into the commute, into the work, into the pregnancy — without having every medical detail secured. And the love at the end of the call — *Love you. Love you.* — is the covenant sign that makes the not-knowing bearable. Love does not require complete information. Love moves toward the other through the fog of the partial and the incomplete. This is precisely what the Father did in Christ. Romans 5:8 — *“But God demonstrates His own love toward us, in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us.”* Not after we were known completely. Not after we had proved ourselves. Not after our spiritual blood type had been typed and matched and cleared for compatibility. While we were yet in the mystery of our own lostness, while we could not have told you what we were made of, the blood was shed. The covenant was ratified in the very midst of human unknowing. This is the shape of divine love — it moves first, it gives first, it covers first. The blood was applied before we could ask what it was. I find myself thinking about the phrase *okay lang* — a Filipino expression meaning something like “it’s alright, it’s fine, no problem.” There is a deep spiritual truth packed into that cultural idiom. The Spirit has been forming in me, and I hear it in Gladys, a kind of sanctified okayness with the things that are not yet resolved. Not denial. Not resignation. But a resting in the knowledge that the One who holds the answer also holds the child, and that His holding is more reliable than our data. Philippians 4:7 speaks of the peace of God that surpasses all understanding — ὑπερέχω (hyperechō, hoo-pehr-EH-khoh), exceeding, surpassing, rising above — and that peace guards the heart and mind in Christ Jesus. The guarding is active. The peace is not passive ignorance; it is militant rest. Okay lang is not laziness. It is the fruit of a trust that has been tested and found solid. The conversation ends with both of us moving toward our separate obligations — she is going to work, I am going to work, we are both still traveling. And yet the love exchanged at the end of a brief, ordinary, medically inconclusive phone call is the thread that holds all of it together. This is what covenant looks like in the daily. It does not require resolution of every unknown. It requires only the continuous, quiet choosing of one another — and, beneath that, the continuous choosing of the God whose blood has already spoken better things over this household than any of us have yet had ears to fully hear. The child coming will, in time, have a name. Will have a blood type on file. Will have a history that neither Gladys nor I can fully write in advance. But I am persuaded that the blood of the Eternal Covenant — that αἷμα (HY-mah) of Jesus, spoken of in Hebrews 13:20 as the blood of the everlasting covenant — is already at work in the forming of this life, and that the mystery we carry in our own bodies is not an obstacle to God’s purposes but the very medium through which He delights to work. He has always worked through blood. He has always worked through the unknown. He has always worked through waiting women and traveling men and the holy space between the already and the not yet. ## Implications and Challenges There is a tendency in Western Christian culture to spiritualize only the dramatic and to dismiss the ordinary as theologically inert. We look for God in the mountaintop moments and miss Him in a brief call from a commute, in a wife asking a practical question, in the laughter of two people who don’t know their own blood types. The challenge here is to recover a sacramental attentiveness to the ordinary — not in a way that manufactures meaning where there is none, but in a way that remains genuinely open to the Spirit’s voice in the texture of daily life. The burning bush was, after all, beside a road Moses had already walked many times. There is also a challenge to the modern compulsion toward complete self-knowledge and total preparedness. Productivity culture and even certain streams of discipleship culture have made the unresolved feel like failure. But Scripture consistently places God’s people in conditions of partial knowledge, calling them forward by promise rather than by sight. *“We walk by faith, not by sight”* — 2 Corinthians 5:7. The not-knowing of our blood types is a tiny, almost comic instance of a far larger truth: we do not know everything we would like to know about ourselves, our future, or our children, and we are invited to hold that not-knowing without shame, releasing the pressure of total self-comprehension into the hands of the One who fully knows. For those who are pregnant, parenting, or anticipating new life: the child you carry is already known by God in ways that exceed your knowing. This is not a platitude. It is a covenantal reality with deep roots in Psalm 139, Jeremiah 1, and the entire arc of Scripture’s testimony that new life is always, from God’s perspective, a purposeful act of divine creativity. The mystery you are holding is held by Someone who does not find it mysterious. Rest in that asymmetry. There is a gentle corrective here for those who have reduced the significance of blood in the New Covenant to mere metaphor. The blood of Jesus is not a symbol that we interpret; it is a substance that speaks — presently, actively, and with covenantal authority. Hebrews 12:24 is not past tense in its implication. The blood is still speaking. To receive this only as historical fact and not as present spiritual reality is to miss the ongoing intercessory and covering power of the Covenant. The blood type of Jesus, if we may press the metaphor, is the universal type — it covers every human blood type under the sky, and it is always compatible with the soul that cries out in need. Finally, there is a word here about the covenant of marriage and the way it mediates life. Gladys and Seth are on separate roads, moving toward separate obligations, and yet they are one — one household, one covenant, one anticipation of new life. The exchange between them, brief as it is, is saturated with the ordinary holiness of faithful covenant love. Modern culture tends to evaluate marriage by its emotional intensity or its romantic fulfillment. Scripture evaluates it by its covenant fidelity, its fruitfulness, and its capacity to reflect the mystery of Christ and the Church — Ephesians 5:32. Even a commute call, ending in “Love you,” is covenant language. Do not underestimate the cumulative spiritual weight of those small faithfulnesses. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** This meditation calls me toward a practiced attentiveness to the holy in the ordinary — not manufactured meaning-making, but a trained ear for the Spirit’s voice moving through the texture of daily life. Maturity here is not achieving complete self-knowledge but learning to rest in partial-knowing without anxiety, trusting that the One who fully knows is both good and near. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The obedience this calls forth is simple but not small: show up, stay in covenant, speak love even in brief exchanges, and carry the unknown with grace rather than with grip. Kingdom obedience in this season may look like scheduling a blood test — and also like recognizing that the child forming is already covered by a covenant more ancient than any prenatal appointment. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture here is one of patient abiding in the between-place. Not yet at Castleton. Waiting on the Uber. The Kingdom often finds us mid-commute, mid-waiting, mid-not-yet-arrived — and it is precisely there, in the in-between, that the Spirit is speaking, forming, covering, and preparing what only He can see to completion. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I thank You that You are the God who knows what we do not — that before a question is asked, You have the answer; that before a child is born, You have already knit them and named them and covered them with the blood of Your Eternal Covenant. I receive this moment — this small, laughing, ordinary call — as a gift of Your grace and a parable of Your ways. Let me carry the not-knowing with the peace that surpasses understanding, guarded in Christ Jesus, moving forward in covenant faithfulness with my wife, my family, and my God. I declare that the blood of Your Son is already speaking over this household — better things, peace things, access things, inheritance things — and I receive that speaking as the ground on which we stand. Let the life forming in the womb be fully known by You, fully held by You, and fully surrendered to Your purposes before the first breath, before the first cry, before the first name is written in any register on earth. You are the Author of life. You are the Covenant God. And we are Yours — okay lang, trusting, and moving forward in love.
