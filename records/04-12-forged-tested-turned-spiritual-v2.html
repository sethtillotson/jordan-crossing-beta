# 04-12 Call Reflection: Spiritual Warfare, Purifying Fire, and Forward-Facing Discipleship **Title:** 04-12 at 12:07 — Forged, Tested, and Turned: Spiritual Warfare, the Purifying Fire, and the Forward-Facing Disciple **Recorded:** 2026-04-12 12:07:10 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture — Phone or Video Call) **Classification:** Call Reflection ## Opening Observation As I sat with the full weight of what the Spirit had been threading through multiple conversations with my brother Samuel — across the spiritual ambush in his household, the question of what we are building upon the foundation of Christ, the running Father meeting the hiding child, and the gravitational pull of backward-looking grief — I found myself standing before something larger than any single exchange. These were not disconnected fragments. They were one sustained word, arriving in layers, each layer pressing deeper into the same irreducible reality: the disciple who is becoming dangerous to the kingdom of darkness will be pressed from every direction simultaneously — through his acknowledged weakness, through his nearest relationships, through the backward pull of what was lost, and through the very simplicity of what is required to remain. The enemy does not need to destroy such a man. He only needs to redirect him long enough, erode him slowly enough, and keep him looking backward and inward rather than upward and forward. **Why This Matters:** This matters because the Body of Christ in this hour is filled with people who have accepted the foundation — who genuinely confess the name of Jesus Christ — yet are being systematically worn down by a war of attrition they have not learned to name. They mistake spiritual warfare for family dysfunction. They mistake the condemning voice of fear-driven religion for pastoral concern. They mistake the backward pull of grief and nostalgia for emotional health that simply needs more time. They mistake their acknowledged weakness for disqualification rather than for the very terrain the enemy exploits strategically. And they are building, day after day, with materials that will not survive the fire of testing — not because they are wicked, but because no one has told them that the fire is coming, that the foundation holds, and that what matters with urgency now is both the posture of the heart and the material of the building. Every serious follower of Jesus Christ will eventually stand exactly where Samuel has been standing — pressed from within and without, longing for what was, uncertain about what is being built, and needing not a therapist but a brother who can hold the weight of the full canonical word over the moment. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation A natural reading of everything Samuel and I walked through would produce a fairly comprehensive therapeutic and sociological analysis: a young man with acknowledged substance use struggles, grieving the loss of a marriage while navigating a household steeped in its own unresolved dependencies, asking big questions about God and worthiness and whether intellectual belief is enough to settle the matter of eternity. The world would recognize the honesty, perhaps even commend the vulnerability, and would reach for its full toolkit — grief counseling, boundary-setting, codependency frameworks, emotional regulation, staged grief processing, and perhaps medication to stabilize the interior weather. It would hear the language of spiritual warfare and reframe it as metaphorical coping, hear the language of condemnation and call it religious trauma, hear the backward-looking grief and call it healthy mourning that simply needs more time and technique. It would not be entirely wrong about any of it; the psychological categories of projection, codependency, attachment wound, and grief stage are real descriptions of real mechanics operating in real families every day. **The Limitation:** But there is an architecture to what was happening across these conversations that the natural mind simply cannot perceive, because it operates within a closed system that has no category for the intelligence behind the timing. The world can assess sincerity and measure emotional health, but it cannot receive the knowledge that comes only when a human spirit made alive by the Spirit of God is given access to the mind of Christ. What looked like a young man struggling with family conflict, grief, and religious doubt was actually a man being strategically targeted at the exact moment he was beginning to press deeper into the things of God — targeted through his most vulnerable relationship, through his acknowledged weakness, through the backward-pulling gravity of unresolved loss, and through the disorienting question of whether his faith had ever been more than intellectual furniture. The flesh-mind sees the argument, the drinking, the nostalgia, the guilt. The spiritual eye sees the archer, the strategy, and the particular weapon chosen: not blunt force, but the slow erosion of daily bread — the war of attrition against the abiding, against the forward-facing posture, against the daily gathering of the manna that cannot be stored. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I have sat with the full sweep of what passed between Samuel and me, the first thing the Spirit pressed me to name is this: the shape of what Samuel was experiencing across every front has a name in Scripture, and that name is not dysfunction — it is the ancient pattern of preparation. Joseph descended into the pit before he ascended to the palace. Moses spent forty years in the wilderness before the burning bush appeared. David lived years of exile before the throne was given. Elijah won his greatest victory at Carmel and then, at Jezebel’s threat, fled into the wilderness and asked God to take his life. The enemy did not destroy Elijah with a frontal assault; he waited until after the victory, until the exhaustion was deepest, and then sent weariness, isolation, and the crushing weight of feeling entirely alone. And God’s response to His prophet was not rebuke — it was bread. Twice. The angel of the Lord came and said: *“Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you”* (1 Kings 19:7). God fed before He questioned. And when the question finally came — *“What are you doing here, Elijah?”* — it was not a geographic inquiry. It was a covenantal one. The assignment is elsewhere. The rest was legitimate; the settlement was not. I heard the same question pressing toward Samuel through our exchange, and I heard it pressing toward me as well. The intensity of the assault against Samuel is itself a form of testimony, and I told him so with everything in me. The enemy does not spend this kind of layered, strategic energy — attacking through a mother’s fragility, through a young man’s acknowledged weakness, through the domestic environment, through the gravitational pull of a broken marriage — against someone whose spiritual development is irrelevant to the kingdom of darkness. He attacks the dangerous ones. And the weapon he chose was not blunt force. It was κατάκριμα (katakrima, kah-TAH-kree-mah) — a judicial verdict of guilt, a sentence pronounced without the authority or the posture to pronounce it. Romans 8:1 opens like a banner over everything that follows: “There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus.” This is not emotional reassurance; it is a forensic declaration. The verdict has been rendered — not guilty — and no secondary court, no fear-driven household voice, no accusing spirit operating through religious language, may overrule it. The Spirit of God convicts, which draws a man toward God; He does not condemn, which drives a man away. What Samuel’s household was producing was the latter. And yet — and here is where I had to press carefully — recognizing the condemnation of others does not license us to catalog their failures with increasing precision. The temptation I watched Samuel pull toward several times was exactly the enemy’s preferred redirect: take a man whose gaze is beginning to fix itself upon the face of God and turn it horizontally, toward the failures of others, because a man gazing outward cannot simultaneously gaze upward. This is the war of attrition I named in our conversation, and I want to be precise about what it is. It is not a single devastating blow. It is a thousand small redirections — a household argument, a night without sleep, a wife frustrated about something that seems small, a dead-end season that grinds the spirit, a mother whose pain cannot be spoken aloud, a marriage that ended before it was supposed to, a memory of a McDonald’s conversation that keeps pulling backward like gravity. Each one, individually, is survivable. Together, accumulated over weeks and months, they form a pattern of erosion. The prophet Elijah understood this exhaustion. The wilderness does not feel like warfare — it feels like ordinary discouragement. But the design of it is precise. The enemy does not need to destroy the disciple; he only needs to keep him from the bread long enough that hunger gives way to desperation, and desperation gives way to compromise. This is why the image of manna — ὁ ἄρτος ὁ ζῶν (ho artos ho zōn, ho AR-tos ho ZONE), the living bread that comes down from heaven — is not a metaphor I invented. It is the architecture of Exodus 16 and the architecture of John 6 simultaneously. The manna could not be stored. It had to be gathered fresh each morning. Those who tried to hoard it found it rotting by the next day (Exodus 16:20). The provision was structured to produce daily dependence, daily return, daily encounter. The enemy’s primary goal in this season against Samuel — and against every disciple in the critical early stages of formation — is to cut off that daily gathering. Fill the morning with noise. Fill the evening with conflict. Fill the quiet with anxiety about outcomes not yet arrived. The question Samuel asked with genuine bewilderment was this: why is the enemy pressing so hard against him specifically, when he knows Christians who have been raised in the faith from childhood with none of this warfare? I asked him a question in return that I believe landed: are they true disciples? Not better or worse as human beings — that is not the measuring stick. But are they genuinely seeking encounter with the living God, or are they settled into religious comfort? The word for “believer” in the New Testament — πιστεύων (pisteuōn, pis-TYOO-on) — carries the force of active, present-tense trust. But the institutional church has largely reduced it to a past-tense transaction: I believed once, I said the prayer, I signed the card, and now I occupy a pew. A μαθητής (mathētēs, mah-thay-TACE) — a disciple, a learner, one who shapes his entire existence around formation under a master — is something categorically different. Discipleship is not an event; it is an ongoing surrender to the refining process. And the refiner’s fire does not burn in comfortable places. The demon knows the theology too (James 2:19). The question has never been what you know about God. The question has always been whether you have encountered Him — whether your daily life is oriented around genuine communion with the living Christ or around the maintenance of a convincing religious identity. This is where the conversation pressed into the territory of what we are building. Paul’s image in 1 Corinthians 3:12–15 is among the most mercy-soaked and most sobering in the entire Pauline corpus simultaneously. No other foundation can be laid than Jesus Christ — that is settled, that is the ground that cannot be moved. But what is being built on top of that foundation, and with what materials? Gold, silver, precious stones survive the fire. Wood, hay, straw do not. The fire will test every work. What does that fire reveal? It reveals whether what was built on the name of Christ was built through genuine encounter — through the παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) of a Father who trains His sons, through the patient suffering of discipline, through the willingness to sit in the refiner’s fire and let it do its work — or whether it was built through religious performance, emotional habit, inherited tradition, and doctrinal correctness that never required anyone to actually die to themselves. And verse 15 contains the mercy hinge: *“But he himself will be saved, yet so as through fire.”* The work is burned. The person is not. The foundation holds. But the loss — the loss of what could have been built, what could have endured, what could have been the reward — that is the cost of a life spent performing rather than encountering. Samuel asked about parents and elders whose walk had been largely performance, and I told him the truth: the foundation holds, but the loss is real and irreversible. That is not condemnation. It is urgency. The question that God first asked in the entire canon of Scripture is not “What have you done?” It is “Where are you?” (Genesis 3:9). The Hebrew beneath it is אַיֶּכָּה (ayyekkāh, ah-yeh-KAH) — not a request for coordinates but a covenantal summons. The force of it is: “How have you come to be here, in this condition, in this hiddenness?” God came to the garden — to the appointed place of communion — and found silence instead of fellowship. And He, who is omniscient and certainly knew exactly where they were, asked the question not for information but for invitation. *Come out. Come back. I am still here. Where are you?* I felt that question pressing over the entirety of what Samuel and I were walking through. The hiding had taken many forms — the drink that dulled the pain, the backward-looking grief that curated and re-curated the story of the marriage, the interior Babylon of memory that kept being rebuilt in his mind while Egypt’s leeks and garlic (Numbers 11:5) still tasted sweeter than the manna of daily encounter. The body had been moving through the present moment while the interior life remained encamped in what was. And the Father was still asking: Where are you? Not as accusation. As orientation. As the deeply loving voice of the One who already knows the answer and is asking only because He wants His son to know it too. When I brought the image of the running father into our exchange, it was not as consolation. It was as theological reorientation. The father in the parable of the prodigal son — and I believe Jesus is revealing the very heart of the Father Himself — does not wait at the threshold with arms crossed and a ledger open. He sees the son when he is still a great way off, and τρέχω (trechō, TREH-khō) — runs. With urgency. With exertion. With purpose. God does not walk toward the returning sinner. He runs. And yet here is where Samuel’s word struck so deep: the grief at that moment will not come from the Father’s face. It will come from our own memory of all the days we did not run back. This is a sorrow more devastating than condemnation, because it is born of love finally received in full. To stand in the arms of a Father whose faithfulness you are seeing clearly for the first time, and to realize simultaneously how long you withheld yourself from that embrace — that will be its own undoing. Not punitive. Revelatory. The Father will call for the robe, the ring, the sandals, the feast. But the son will know. And that knowing is itself a kind of purification that the saints who run toward Him in this life begin receiving now, in small and willing increments, through repentance and surrender, rather than all at once in the shock of final unveiling. What I said to Samuel about his forward-looking — or rather, the absence of it — was the most practically urgent word I pressed through our exchange. There is a pull in the human heart, ancient and almost gravitational, to look behind at what was lost rather than forward into what is promised. And I want to be precise about what backward-looking costs, because it is not merely psychological — it is a spiritual orientation that shapes what we receive, what we expect, and ultimately what we become. ἐλπίς (elpis, EL-pees) — hope in the biblical sense — is not wishful thinking. It is a confident expectation of a guaranteed future, rooted in the faithfulness of God rather than in the cooperation of circumstances. When Samuel said he had been raised to always look back, there was no defensiveness in it — there was relief in the naming. And in that moment I heard the Spirit press me to do more than notice the pattern: to speak the Revelation 18:4 word over it. *“Come out of her, my people.”* Babylon, in the prophetic imagination of Scripture, is not merely a political system — it is a spirit, an orientation toward the world that substitutes earthly security, pleasure, nostalgia, and accumulated comfort for the living God. The command to come out is a command to stop partaking in the logic of the fallen world, which includes the logic that the past holds more life than the future, that what was contains more substance than what is promised. I also have to be honest about what the Spirit was pressing toward me personally across this same season. My wife was frustrated with me because I had not come to bed when I said I would — I had been drawn into Scriptures and meditations deep into the night, and by the time I surfaced, she was asleep and right to be frustrated. The same enemy who was pressing against Samuel through his household was pressing against me through mine. The elder brother is not exempt from the war. The one trying to help another stand is simultaneously being pressed through his own nearest relationships, his own unresolved obedience, his own undelivered calls and testimonies. I disclosed this to Samuel before we ended our exchange, not as a confession of spiritual failure but as a declaration of solidarity: I am not reporting this from a safe distance. I am in the middle of it too. And what I am finding, in the middle of it, is that the call to abide does not produce frictionless domestic comfort — it produces the kind of rootedness that can sustain the friction without breaking. That is a different promise, and it requires a different preparation than what most of us were offered in the Christianity of our formation. The architecture of genuine abiding — μένω (menō, MEH-no), to remain, to dwell continuously, to stay — is the whole of John 15. The vine-and-branches discourse is governed by a single imperative: abide in Me. And I said something that genuinely arrested Samuel in the moment: the reason most people will not stay on the narrow path is because it is so deceptively simple. This is right, and it is worth sitting with why that simplicity is so offensive to the religious mind. There is no program, no achievement ladder, no accumulation of spiritual credentials that replaces the simple, daily, intentional act of remaining in Christ. You cannot out-work the call to abide. You cannot substitute theological knowledge or church attendance for the interior posture of remaining. This is also the reason the distinction between γινώσκω (ginōskō, ghee-NOH-skō) — intimate, experiential knowing — and mere doctrinal familiarity is not academic. The Pharisees had theological knowledge; they lacked ginōskō-knowing, the knowing that comes only from having run to the Father and been held. Jesus said to them plainly: “I never knew you” (Matthew 7:23). The demons have correct theology too (James 2:19). The distinguishing mark has never been what you know about God. It has always been whether you are abiding — remaining, dwelling, staying — in Him. The identity question that surfaced in our exchange must also be addressed with precision, because it governs the gravitational center of Samuel’s entire spiritual life in this season. I am not a πιστεύων (pis-TYOO-on) reaching for grace from a beggar’s position outside the door. I am a ἅγιος (hagios, HAH-gee-os) — a set-apart one, a holy one — who sometimes acts contrary to my own declared nature. Paul addresses the Corinthian church — a messy, divided, sinning congregation — as *saints* (1 Corinthians 1:2), not because they had achieved holiness but because their identity had been declared and sealed in the blood of Christ. When Samuel operates from the posture of sinner reaching for grace, he positions himself as a supplicant before a distant deity. When he operates from the posture of a son who is covered, indwelt, and commissioned, he receives grace from within a covenantal relationship — the way a child receives from a father. The practical implication for a man navigating the grief of a broken marriage, the pressure of a condemning household, and the pull of backward-looking nostalgia is this: if I am fundamentally a sinner, my past mistakes define me, and the backward-looking makes a kind of dark sense. But if I am fundamentally a ἅγιος (HAH-gee-os) — if that is the truest thing about me — then the past is neither my prison nor my identity. It is simply a chapter through which the faithful God was working all things together for good (Romans 8:28), even the things I got wrong. The image that has stayed with me across the whole of this conversation is the parable of the two builders (Matthew 7:24–27), and specifically what Jesus does not say about the distinction between them. He does not say the wise man was more intelligent or more virtuous or more spiritually gifted. The distinction is entirely about what each man does with the Word he has heard. Both men hear. Only one builds on the rock. And when the storm comes — the floods, the winds, the beating rain — the house on the rock does not fall. Not because the storm is gentle, but because the foundation is immovable. I told Samuel: your only anchor in this season is to remain in Christ no matter how far you feel you have descended. And I meant something precise by that. The great descents in Scripture are never descents into abandonment — they are descents into deeper encounter. Joseph descended into the pit, into slavery, into the prison — and the LORD was with Joseph (Genesis 39:21). The Hebrew beneath that is חֶסֶד (hesed, HEH-sed) — covenant lovingkindness, steadfast mercy. The LORD was with Joseph *in* the prison — not above it, not after it, but in it. Descent is not abandonment. The pit is not the final word. Psalm 16:10 — which Peter applies directly to the resurrection of Christ in Acts 2:27 — declares: “For You will not leave my soul in Sheol” — שְׁאוֹל (sheol, sheh-OLE), the deep place, the place of descent. What was true of Jesus is derivatively true of every disciple in union with Him: the descent is always followed by the raising. The weapons are being forged in the very fire the enemy intends as destruction. Let us not be the ones who arrive at the harvest having spent a lifetime in hiding, or a lifetime building with wood and hay and straw on an unimpeachable foundation, or a lifetime looking backward at what Egypt offered while manna lay uncollected in the morning grass. The fire is coming that will test every work. The Father is running toward every returning prodigal. The question αַיֶּכָּה (ah-yeh-KAH) is still being asked over every generation and every hidden heart. The call to come out, to turn forward, to gather the bread, to abide in the vine, to build with gold — that call is not a burden. It is the most loving thing the Spirit can press into a disciple who is being forged in a fire he did not choose, for an assignment he has not yet fully seen. ## Implications and Challenges The Body of Christ in the Western world has largely lost the category of spiritual warfare as a lived and discerned reality. We have either over-spiritualized suffering into a kind of charismatic theater that lacks the sobriety of genuine discernment, or we have entirely naturalized it so that pain is always only psychological, relational, or sociological in its origin. Samuel’s situation — and the situation of every serious disciple pressing into deeper formation — demands that we hold both the natural and the spiritual in view simultaneously, without collapsing one into the other. He had real struggles, and those real struggles were being weaponized by a real enemy at a strategic moment. The first challenge this meditation carries is this: we must recover the ability to read the seasons accurately, to see both the genuine human failure and the larger strategic pattern without being consumed by either. The confusion between κατάκριμα (kah-TAH-kree-mah) — the judicial verdict of condemnation — and genuine pastoral conviction is one of the most damaging operational errors in contemporary Christianity. Many households and communities operate in a spirit of condemnation while sincerely believing they are being scriptural. They invoke God’s name and then deliver shame. The result is not repentance — it is either rebellion or false compliance, and neither serves the Kingdom. The Spirit of God convicts with precision and draws with love; He does not issue verdicts against His own children. Where condemnation has become the dominant pastoral posture, it must be named and repented of — not with cruelty, but with the same clarity the Spirit brings. ---
