# 06-30 Conversation Reflection: Dirty Hands & Pure Heart — Incarnation, Spiritual Warfare, and Kingdom Living **Title:** 06-30 at 11:59 — Dirty Hands and a Pure Heart: On Angels, Demons, and the Radical Simplicity of the Incarnate Life **Recorded:** 2026-06-30 11:59:47 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Conversation Reflection ## Opening Observation Something happened in the middle of an ordinary morning — no gas money, no agenda, just two brothers stumbling awake into the day — and the Spirit threaded gold through the mundane without announcement. I heard a line drop out of a casual exchange that stopped me cold: *“Though my hands dirty, carry a pure heart”* — and I knew immediately that what sounded like a young man improvising poetry was something older, something the Spirit had been waiting to say through willing lips. That single line, and the conversation that surrounded it, cracked open questions I have been carrying for a long time about what it actually means to live rightly, what to do with the demonic, how to see the stranger across the table, and why the Kingdom so often arrives without fanfare in the middle of a morning when everyone overslept. **Why This Matters:** The conversations we dismiss as ordinary — the calls where nothing seems to be happening, the mornings we lose to exhaustion and financial pressure and pregnancy cravings and a cat nosing through a lunchbox — these are the very moments the Spirit chooses to speak, precisely because our defenses are down and our pretense is gone. If the Body of Christ only expects revelation in sanctuaries and structured devotionals, it will miss the word that comes through a brother’s offhand quote, a shared laugh about fireball whiskey, and a sudden question about whether anyone we meet might be a messenger from above. Discipleship is not a program. It is a posture of attentiveness that must survive the unglamorous hours, and learning to hear the Spirit in *those* hours is among the most urgent things I can practice. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, looking at this conversation, sees nothing particularly significant. Two young men are killing a lazy morning — broke, tired, mildly self-medicating their exhaustion, talking about business funnels and European cars and drug dealers and old girlfriends with dissociative identities and a pregnant wife demanding strawberries with salt. The natural mind catalogs this as colorful but inconsequential: a couple of entrepreneurial friends venting, bonding, philosophizing loosely in the way that young men do when there is nothing else pressing. The line about dirty hands and a pure heart gets noted as a decent aphorism. The conversation about demons and angels gets filed under anecdote. The question about living rightly gets a polite nod. None of it registers as spiritually weighty because nothing external marks it as such — no altar call, no worship set, no theological credentials in the room. The natural mind requires ceremony to certify significance, and this conversation had none. **The Limitation:** But the natural mind is constitutionally unable to perceive what the Spirit hides inside ordinary time. It cannot see that a throwaway business conversation about “one funnel, one offer” is actually a parable of abiding. It cannot recognize that a young man wondering aloud whether he is demon-possessed might be standing at the threshold of genuine self-examination and spiritual discernment. It cannot hear the theology latent in the phrase *“though my hands dirty, carry a pure heart”* — theology that reaches back through the psalms of lament and forward to the crucified hands of the Son of God Himself. The natural mind reads the surface and finds nothing. The Spirit reads the same words and finds a burning coal. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sat with this conversation — as I let it move through me rather than past me — I kept returning to a single phrase my brother dropped almost accidentally into the middle of a casual exchange. *“Though my hands dirty, carry a pure heart.”* He said it with the confidence of a man who recognized he had written something true, and he was right, even if neither of us could fully map it in the moment. I am seeing now what the Spirit was pressing into that line. It is not merely a poetic sentiment. It is a compressed theology of the Incarnation. The hands of Jesus were dirty. I mean this literally. He was a carpenter’s son — כַּגָּן (the tradition holds Him working timber and stone), a man who walked on dusty roads without shoes, who reached into the diseased and the leprous, who knelt to write in the dirt, who washed feet blackened by travel, who pressed bread and fish into thousands of reaching hands, who had the blood of others on Him before He had His own blood shed. The purity of the Son of God was never expressed through the avoidance of contamination. It was expressed through an absolute refusal to let contamination redefine identity. His hands were dirty. His heart was immaculate. These two things coexisted not despite the Incarnation but because of it — this is precisely what εἰκὼν (eikōn, AY-kohn) — image, likeness, exact representation — means when applied to the Son in Colossians 1:15. He is the visible image of the invisible God, and the visibility required touching what was unclean. This lands differently when I hold it against my brother’s question: *“What is it to live correctly?”* I gave an answer in that moment that I still believe — empty yourself of your so-called birthright, serve others, wash feet, break bread with sinners, go to the dangerous places and say *we are not all that bad* even if your head might be cut off. But I want to go deeper into that now. Because what I was describing without the precise language was κένωσις (kenōsis, keh-NOH-sis) — the self-emptying of Philippians 2:7, where Paul writes that Christ *“made Himself of no reputation, taking the form of a bondservant, and coming in the likeness of men.”* That word κένωσις (keh-NOH-sis) is not a metaphor for humility in a motivational sense. It is a description of ontological condescension — the eternal Son willingly taking on limitation, vulnerability, need, hunger, exhaustion, misunderstanding, betrayal, and death. Dirty hands. Pure heart. I notice that my brother’s question about living rightly came immediately after a conversation about radicalized children born under the Taliban — children who do not choose the ideology that forms them, any more than I chose the Disney-distributed biases that shaped my pre-Christ identity. The Spirit was pressing something there that I felt but could not fully name in real time: that the dividing line between *us* and *them* is far thinner than nationalism or theology typically admits, and that the only posture that honors the Image of God in every face is the one Jesus modeled — presence over safety, table fellowship over tribal purity. The NKJV renders it plainly in Matthew 9:13: *“I desire mercy and not sacrifice.”* Jesus was quoting Hosea 6:6, and the Pharisees who heard it understood the rebuke. Mercy — חֶסֶד (ḥesed, KHEH-sed) — covenantal lovingkindness, the loyalty that stays even when it costs everything — requires dirty hands. Sacrifice can be performed at a clean distance. Mercy cannot. Something arrests me in the moment where I mentioned that any client, any customer — anyone we encounter — could be an angel. I pulled that from something I had been meditating on through a compiled corpus of past reflections, and it dropped into the conversation like a stone into still water. Hebrews 13:2 is the explicit anchor: *“Do not forget to entertain strangers, for by so doing some have unwittingly entertained angels.”* The Greek word is ξενίζω (xenizō, kseh-NEE-zo) — to receive a stranger, to offer hospitality to the foreign and the unknown. The entire texture of that verse is built on unknowing. You do not know who stands across the threshold. Abraham did not know when three men appeared at Mamre in Genesis 18. Lot did not know in Genesis 19. The disciples on the Emmaus road did not know in Luke 24. Unknowing is the operative condition — and the text does not call us to resolve the unknowing before offering hospitality. It calls us to receive the stranger *because* we do not know. That changes the entire posture of every sales call, every conversation, every encounter with a stranger at a gas station or a job interview. If I enter each exchange as one who might be standing before a divine messenger, I do not manage the encounter — I reverence it. The conversation then turned, unexpectedly, toward the demonic — and not abstractly. I shared a past experience with a woman who carried what I would now identify as a constellation of trauma-formed dissociative identities and spiritual entities, and what I am sitting with now is the theological framework I had to develop after the fact to understand what I witnessed then. There were guardian angels visible and distinct. There were trauma demons with names and personalities and histories bound to specific wounds. There were the seven — the number itself is not incidental; Matthew 12:43–45 gives us Jesus describing an unclean spirit that leaves a man, wanders, and returns with *seven others more wicked than itself*, leaving the last state of the man worse than the first. The number seven in that passage is not rhetorical hyperbole. It is a pattern, and I saw that pattern made incarnate in a person years before I had the biblical language to name it. The Spirit is pressing on me now that the order of operations matters intensely in deliverance: you do not simply cast out and leave the house swept and empty. You fill it. You invite the indwelling of the Holy Spirit — πνεῦμα (pneuma, PYOO-mah) — breath, wind, the animating presence of God — to occupy what was cleared. An empty house is an invitation. My brother also asked aloud — almost joking, but not entirely — whether he might be a demon, whether something that comes through him on certain days is not him. I recognized that question because I have asked it about myself. And I want to honor the spiritual seriousness underneath the humor: the impulse behind that question is actually a form of discernment. Paul names it in Romans 7:15 — *“For what I am doing, I do not understand. For what I will to do, that I do not practice; but what I hate, that I do.”* That is not a man who is a demon. That is a man who is awake enough to perceive the war inside himself — which is precisely the condition the Spirit can work with. The fully deceived man does not notice the conflict. It is the man who notices the conflict who is beginning to live in the Spirit’s light. My brother is not a demon. He is a man who is starting to see, and that seeing is itself a mercy. There was a brief thread in the conversation about the pace of the world — about how society offers no space to be still, and how we reach for substances to decompress because no natural pause is given to us. My brother made a distinction I want to honor: *“It’s not the world, it’s society.”* And I clarified what I meant by *world* in the Johannine sense — not creation, not nature, but the system of organized human rebellion against the knowledge of God. And he immediately confirmed it: *“Nature goes at the perfect pace.”* That is a profound theological instinct. The κόσμος (kosmos, KOS-mos) that John warns against in 1 John 2:15–17 is not the created order — God looked at that and called it good. The κόσμος (KOS-mos) that must not be loved is the *system* — the accelerating, commodifying, dopamine-harvesting architecture of human self-assertion that positions itself as the alternative to Eden. Nature rests in Sabbath rhythms. Society does not. And we bear the cost of that in our bodies, our sleep disorders, our exhaustion that appears from nowhere, our need to reach for something just to slow the spinning down. I also want to sit with the moment I said — and I meant it — that the reason I may still be in poverty is that the Lord is waiting on my obedience in small financial things. Not as a formula or a transaction, but as a season of formation. I gave my last paycheck to my wife to hold so that I would not spend it on the things that ease the pressure but delay the harvest. That is not performance. That is the beginning of trusting that the Vine knows how to bring the branch into fruitfulness — and the branch’s role is not to strain, but to abide. My brother affirmed it from his own history: the season of greatest financial success in his young life was also the season of greatest focus and sobriety, even though it was also the season of greatest suffering. Affliction and fruitfulness are not opposites in the Kingdom. They are often simultaneous — the grain of wheat falling into the ground and dying in John 12:24, only to bear much fruit. Finally, I return to the line that started this: *dirty hands, pure heart.* What the Spirit is showing me through my brother’s improvised poetry is that the gospel does not promise clean circumstances. It does not promise ease, proximity to power, access to resources, or freedom from the mud of daily life. What it promises is an interior transformation so thorough that the mud on the outside cannot reach what is being formed within. The saints throughout Scripture were not people of clean circumstances — Moses was a murderer, David an adulterer, Peter a denier, Paul a persecutor. Their hands had been in terrible places. But the Spirit pressed through every one of them toward a purification of heart that the circumstances never touched. That is the testimony of the New Covenant: *“I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit within you”* — Ezekiel 36:26. Not new circumstances. A new heart. The hands may remain in the world’s mud for a long time yet. But the heart is being formed into something the world did not make and cannot take. ## Implications and Challenges The impulse to wait for a sacred setting before expecting revelation is one of the most quietly limiting habits in modern Western Christian practice. If I only listen for the Spirit in curated environments, I will spend most of my life deaf. The transcript before me is a breakfast-time phone call between two broke, tired, imperfect young men talking about everything from drug dealers to dissociative identities to pancakes. And the Spirit spoke clearly through it. The invitation is to cultivate what the tradition calls *nepsis* — watchful sobriety of spirit — not just in the prayer closet, but in every ordinary hour. This is not a call to mystify the mundane artificially. It is a call to remain genuinely available throughout the whole day. The question *“What is it to live correctly?”* was answered simply and radically — empty yourself, serve others, go where it is dangerous, break bread with the enemy. But the modern Western church has developed an enormous infrastructure of self-protection that makes this answer functionally optional. We have buildings and programs and theological positions and community groups designed in ways that allow us to live entire Christian lives without ever having our hands genuinely dirtied by proximity to the broken. The line *“though my hands dirty, carry a pure heart”* is not just poetry. It is a rebuke of a sanitized Christianity that has confused cleanliness with holiness. The conversation about demonic realities was casual in tone but theologically serious in content. The modern Western church tends toward one of two dysfunctional extremes: either complete naturalization of everything that Scripture attributes to spiritual agency, or an overcorrected demonology that sees possession behind every bad mood. The testimony shared in this conversation — entities with names, personalities, trauma origins, and hierarchical structures — aligns more precisely with the full biblical witness than either of those extremes. The pastoral implication is that spiritual formation cannot be separated from spiritual warfare, and that the saints need to be equipped to discern, intercede, and minister — not just theologically instructed. There is something the Spirit pressed through the simple idea that any stranger could be an angel, and the challenge it raises is this: when I approach an interaction primarily as a transaction — a sales call, a hiring process, a service encounter — I have already reduced the image of God in that person to a function they serve for me. The ξενίζω (kseh-NEE-zo) of Hebrews 13:2 is not merely an ancient hospitality custom. It is a posture that refuses to let utility determine value. In a culture that has commodified nearly every human relationship, recovering genuine hospitality — especially toward those who cannot immediately return the favor — is a prophetic act. The thread about sobriety, poverty, and the question of whether the Lord waits on obedience in small things before releasing larger provision — this needs to be held carefully. It is not a prosperity gospel formula. What I named was something more personal and more nuanced: the Spirit’s discipline toward freedom from compulsive reaching, from numbing, from the management of anxiety through consumption. That discipline is real, and it is formational. But the grace underneath it is this: the Lord does not withhold provision as punishment. He withholds the premature abundance that would confirm the habit and delay the freedom. The pruning of John 15 is for more fruit, not for less life. The intuition my brother shared — that if he can just calm down and grow, things will get better — is spiritually sound and worth defending against the culture’s relentless demand for urgency. Spiritual growth is not accelerated by striving harder. It is deepened by the kind of unhurried rootedness that Psalm 1 describes in the image of a tree planted by rivers of water, whose leaf does not wither, who brings forth fruit in season. The fruit comes in season — not on demand, not on the market’s timeline. Learning to receive that pace as grace rather than failure is one of the genuine marks of maturity. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity is not the ability to perform theology in a structured setting. It is the formation of an interior life stable enough that revelation can move through a Tuesday morning phone call about pancakes and poverty and dissociative demons without being missed. Growing into that kind of attentiveness requires the willingness to be fully present in the unglamorous hours — to bring the same reverence to an ordinary conversation that we bring to a sanctuary. The Spirit does not rank our hours. We do. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The κένωσις (keh-NOH-sis) that characterized Jesus — the willing self-emptying, the taking of the servant’s form, the readiness to have dirty hands in service of a pure mission — is not a theological concept to admire from a distance. It is the shape of Christian obedience in every domain: in business, in marriage, in financial discipline, in the willingness to sit with a stranger who might be an angel or a demonized human being or both, and to remain present rather than exit. The obedience the Spirit is calling for is the obedience of remaining — abiding in the Vine, staying in the difficult conversation, holding the difficult season without reaching for the thing that numbs it. **For Prophetic Posture:** The saints who bear prophetic testimony in this hour are not primarily those who predict the future. They are those who see the present with the clarity of the Spirit — who can sit in a meandering morning conversation and recognize that the line about dirty hands is a word, that the question about living rightly is a genuine threshold, that the stranger at the other end of the call could be a divine appointment. Remaining in that posture requires the חֶסֶד (KHEH-sed) — the covenantal faithfulness — that does not give up on the ordinary, does not wait for the extraordinary to be God’s address, and does not stop listening just because the conversation has moved on to cars and strawberries. ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one of Your servants — a co-heir of the Kingdom You established through the blood of Your Son, standing between the heavens and the earth in the covenant authority You have given to those who are called by Your Name. I thank You for the ordinary morning that held extraordinary things — for the brother whose hands are willing to be dirtied by the work of building something, whose heart reached for purity even through imperfect language, and whose question about how to live rightly is one of the finest questions a man can carry. I ask that You press the Word deeper into both of us: that the κένωσις (keh-NOH-sis) of Your Son would become the grammar of our lives, that we would learn to hold dirty hands and pure hearts simultaneously without apologizing for either, that we would receive every stranger with the ξενίζω (kseh-NEE-zo) of those who understand that angels have walked among men unrecognized. Where the demonic has been near — in the histories we carry, in the patterns we inherited, in the old strategies of numbing and escape — let the fullness of Your Spirit, the πνεῦμα (PYOO-mah) that raised Christ from the dead, fill every room that has been swept clean. And in the season of lean provision and small obediences, let patience have its perfect work in us, that we may be mature and complete, lacking nothing. May our agreement with Your will be as it is in heaven — unhurried, fruitful, and free. To the Lamb who is worthy: all honor, dominion, and glory.
