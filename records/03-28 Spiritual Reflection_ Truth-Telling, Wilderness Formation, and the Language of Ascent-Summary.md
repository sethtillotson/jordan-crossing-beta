# 03-27 Sermon Reflection: Kingdom Authority, Holy Standards, and Spiritual Violence in Faith

**Source Discernment:** E — Recorded Sermon with Personal Reflection

**Reflection: The Necessity of Holy Standards and the Violence of Kingdom Faith**

## Opening Observation

As I sat listening to Prophet Lovy L. Elias press through this portion of his sermon, something in my spirit came alive—not in agreement with every illustration, but in recognition of a fierce Kingdom truth that the Western Church has nearly forgotten: that faith without standards is sentiment, and prayer without the condemnation of darkness is merely religious noise. I am arrested by the urgency in his voice, the refusal to let comfort masquerade as faithfulness, and the insistence that God has already done His part—salvation has been given, and now the responsibility to build, to resist, and to refuse compromise rests squarely on the saints.

**Why This Matters:**

This matters because too many of us have conflated patience with passivity, and grace with the acceptance of everything that comes our way. We have been taught to endure rather than to war, to receive rather than to discern, and to call every hardship “God’s will” rather than recognize when the enemy has sent a counterfeit. If we do not learn to identify the standards of God’s Kingdom, to condemn what opposes His wisdom, and to refuse what He has never sent, we will live perpetually beneath our inheritance—praying fervently while accepting spiritually what we should be violently resisting.

## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation

From a natural perspective, this sermon could be read as empowerment rhetoric—a motivational message designed to stir personal agency and self-advocacy. The worldly mind hears this and thinks: “Set boundaries. Know your worth. Don’t settle.” It interprets the language of spiritual violence as psychological assertiveness, the condemnation of darkness as the rejection of toxic relationships, and the refusal of compromise as self-care. It sees the call to have standards as nothing more than healthy self-respect, and the insistence that God will not give a rock when you ask for bread as a prosperity-adjacent promise that you deserve better circumstances. The natural mind applauds the energy, the passion, the refusal to be a victim—but it flattens the entire meditation into therapeutic individualism dressed in Christian vocabulary.

**The Limitation:**

But the natural mind cannot perceive what is truly at stake here. It cannot see that this is not about personal empowerment, but about priestly authority; not about knowing your worth, but about knowing the worth of the One who indwells you. The mind of the flesh interprets Kingdom violence as human ambition, spiritual standards as self-assertion, and the condemnation of darkness as emotional boundary-setting. It cannot comprehend that there is an actual enemy, an actual kingdom of darkness, and an actual spiritual war in which passivity is not humility—it is desertion. What the natural mind hears as motivational language, the Spirit reveals as the sober call to take up the governmental authority of the saints and enforce the victory that Christ has already won.

## What the Spirit Reveals: A Meditation

As I listened to this brother preach, I found myself confronted by a doctrine that I have seen lived out in Scripture but rarely proclaimed with such unapologetic force in the contemporary Western Church: the doctrine of spiritual violence. Not the violence of human rage or fleshly striving, but the violence of faith that refuses to yield ground to the powers of darkness. The Lord Jesus Himself said in Matthew 11:12 (NKJV), “And from the days of John the Baptist until now the kingdom of heaven suffers violence, and the violent take it by force.” This is not metaphorical. This is not poetic exaggeration. This is the operational reality of Kingdom advancement in a world still occupied by the god of this age.

The prophet’s question arrested me: “If you don’t condemn the works of darkness, darkness will continue to reign.” I hear in that question the echo of 2 Corinthians 10:4–5 (NKJV): “For the weapons of our warfare are not carnal but mighty in God for pulling down strongholds, casting down arguments and every high thing that exalts itself against the knowledge of God, bringing every thought into captivity to the obedience of Christ.” The Greek word for “casting down” is καθαιρέω (*kathaireo*, kath-ahee-REH-oh)—to demolish, to pull down, to destroy utterly. This is not passive resistance. This is active, authoritative, governmental dismantling. And it begins not with intercession alone, but with the condemnation—the judicial rejection—of every thought, every pattern, every principality that exalts itself above the wisdom of God.

I have watched too many saints pray fervently while simultaneously embracing the very mindsets, relationships, and habits that contradict the Word they claim to believe. I have seen believers ask God for deliverance while refusing to condemn the thing they need deliverance from. And I am seeing now, as this brother presses, that there is a responsibility that belongs to us—not to God. God has given us salvation. He has given us the Holy Spirit. He has given us authority in the Name of Jesus. But He will not condemn on our behalf what we continue to consent to.

The prophet’s illustration of asking for bread and receiving rocks, asking for fish and receiving snakes, struck me with fresh force. The Lord Jesus said in Matthew 7:9–11 (NKJV), “Or what man is there among you who, if his son asks for bread, will give him a stone? Or if he asks for a fish, will he give him a serpent? If you then, being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask Him!” The implication is clear: if you are asking for bread and receiving stones, if you are asking for fish and receiving serpents, then what you are receiving is not from the Father. Someone else is sending it.

This is where discernment becomes a matter of spiritual survival. The Greek word for fish is ἰχθύς (*ichthys*, ikh-THOOS)—it became the symbol of the early Church precisely because it represented souls, covenant relationships, Kingdom partnerships. When we pray for the right people—investors, friends, spouses, ministry partners—and what shows up instead are those who bite, betray, and drain, we must not spiritualize that as “God’s will” or “a lesson He’s teaching me.” We must condemn it. We must reject it. We must recognize that the enemy is a counterfeiter, and if we do not have the standards of God’s Word in place, we will accept every counterfeit as though it were heaven-sent.

I hear the brother pressing into the question of marriage, and while some of his illustrations may land differently depending on cultural context, the core theological point is sound: God has given us agency in the choices that will shape our lives, and He expects us to exercise that agency according to His Word and His wisdom. When Adam named Eve “bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh” (Genesis 2:23, NKJV), he was exercising covenant recognition. He was not passively receiving whatever God placed before him—he was actively identifying what matched the design of his own frame. The Hebrew word for “bone” is עֶצֶם (*etsem*, EH-tsem)—substance, self, essence. Adam recognized in Eve what corresponded to his own essence. That is not passivity. That is Spirit-led discernment.

And yet, how many of us have been taught to abdicate discernment in the name of “trusting God”? How many have prayed, “Whatever You want, Lord,” while refusing to study His Word to discover what He has already revealed He wants? I am seeing now that the phrase “whatever God wants” can become a cloak for spiritual laziness, for the refusal to take responsibility for the governmental authority we have been given. God has already told us what kind of spouse honors Him—one who fears the Lord, who walks in purity, who is a fellow heir of the grace of life (1 Peter 3:7, NKJV). He has already told us what kind of friendships edify—those who sharpen us as iron sharpens iron (Proverbs 27:17, NKJV). He has already told us what kind of partnerships advance His Kingdom—those who are equally yoked, who share the same Spirit, the same mission, the same love for the truth (2 Corinthians 6:14, NKJV). We are not waiting on God to decide. We are waiting on ourselves to align with what He has already decided.

The brother’s insistence that “God made Eve for Adam, but He never told him, ‘This is your wife’”—this strikes at the heart of a deeply embedded passivity in modern Christian dating culture. We have been taught to wait for a sign, to expect a supernatural confirmation, to avoid the responsibility of covenant recognition. But Adam was not passive. He named her. He declared her. He took covenant responsibility for what God had formed. And I am seeing that this is the pattern: God forms, but man must recognize, declare, and take covenant responsibility. That is not presumption. That is obedience.

The distinction the prophet makes between soul ties and sexual sin is worth pressing into. The Hebrew word for soul is נֶפֶשׁ (*nephesh*, NEH-fesh)—the seat of desire, will, emotion, and personhood. A soul tie is not formed by physical contact alone; it is formed by covenant, by vow, by the binding of one will to another. David and Jonathan had a soul tie (1 Samuel 18:1, NKJV)—their souls were knit together, not through physical intimacy, but through covenant loyalty. The text says, “the soul of Jonathan was knit to the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul.” The word “knit” is קָשַׁר (*qashar*, kah-SHAR)—to bind, to conspire, to league together. This is covenant language. This is the language of intentional, volitional, spiritual alliance.

Sexual sin can create a perverse imitation of this—a fleshly entanglement that mimics covenant without the presence of covenant faithfulness. But the brother is right: physical contact alone does not create a soul tie. Obsession, addiction, trauma bonding—these are real, but they are not the same as the covenant binding of souls that God honors. And this distinction matters, because if we do not understand it, we will either live in fear of every past relationship or fail to recognize when we have made an actual spiritual covenant that must be renounced.

I hear the urgency in his voice when he says, “Don’t get married with baggage. Seek healing. Seek counseling.” And I recognize the pastoral wisdom in that. Too many enter covenant carrying the unhealed wounds, the unrenounced vows, the unresolved traumas of the past—and then wonder why the marriage is a battlefield. The apostle Paul says in Ephesians 5:27 (NKJV) that Christ is presenting to Himself “a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle or any such thing, but that she should be holy and without blemish.” If that is the standard for the Bride of Christ, should we not also pursue wholeness, healing, and holiness before entering human covenant?

The brother’s final crescendo—“I have accepted a certain standard and I cannot go below it”—this is not arrogance. This is covenant faithfulness. This is the same spirit that caused Daniel to purpose in his heart that he would not defile himself (Daniel 1:8, NKJV). The Hebrew word for “purpose” is שׂוּם (*sum*, SOOM)—to set, to appoint, to establish firmly. Daniel established a boundary before the test came. He did not wait to see how he felt in the moment. He decided in advance what he would and would not accept. And because of that decision, God honored him with favor, with wisdom, with promotion, and with prophetic authority that outlasted empires.

I am seeing that this is what the brother is calling us to: not to perfectionism, not to legalism, not to self-righteousness—but to the holy violence of a saint who has established the standards of God’s Word in their heart and refuses to negotiate them, regardless of pressure, regardless of opportunity, regardless of how long the wait may be.

## Implications and Challenges

This meditation confronts several deeply embedded assumptions in contemporary Western Christianity, and I want to name them clearly—not to shame, but to invite us into deeper alignment with the Word.

**First**, it challenges the theology of passivity that has been baptized as “trusting God.” We have conflated surrender with inaction, faith with fatalism, and waiting on God with abdicating the governmental authority He has already given us. But the Scriptures do not teach passivity—they teach active, violent, authoritative faith. We are commanded to resist the devil (James 4:7, NKJV), to cast down strongholds (2 Corinthians 10:4, NKJV), to take every thought captive (2 Corinthians 10:5, NKJV), to stand against the wiles of the devil (Ephesians 6:11, NKJV), and to take the Kingdom by force (Matthew 11:12, NKJV). None of this language is passive. All of it requires us to act, to choose, to refuse, and to enforce.

**Second**, it challenges the assumption that everything that happens to us is “God’s will.” This is one of the most spiritually crippling lies in the modern Church. God is sovereign, yes—but He has given dominion to man (Genesis 1:26–28, NKJV), and He will not override the authority He has delegated unless we invite Him to do so through prayer, repentance, and alignment with His Word. When a believer prays for a godly spouse and ends up in a toxic relationship, that is not God testing them—that is the enemy counterfeiting, and the believer failing to discern. When a believer prays for financial breakthrough and ends up in a predatory business partnership, that is not God teaching them patience—that is the enemy stealing, and the believer failing to condemn what God never sent.

**Third**, it challenges the romantic notion that love is primarily about feelings, chemistry, or emotional connection. The brother’s insistence that physical attraction and compatibility matter is not carnal—it is realistic. God made us embodied beings. The covenant of marriage is not a purely spiritual union; it is a covenant of spirit, soul, and body. To spiritualize marriage to the point where physical attraction is dismissed as “worldly” is to despise the body that God created and called good. At the same time, to elevate physical attraction above spiritual compatibility is to build on sand. Both matter. Both must align. And the believer has the responsibility—and the authority—to discern whether that alignment exists before making a covenant.

**Fourth**, it challenges the modern Christian dating culture that treats relationships as passive, feelings-driven, “let’s see where this goes” experiments. The brother is right: Adam did not “date” Eve. He recognized her, he declared her, and he entered covenant with her. There was no ambiguity. There was no “talking stage.” There was covenant recognition followed by covenant commitment. And while I am not suggesting we return to arranged marriages or eliminate all seasons of discernment, I am suggesting that the modern Christian dating culture has produced more confusion, more heartbreak, and more delayed obedience than the Church wants to admit. We need to return to the clarity of covenant.

**Finally**, it challenges the tolerance of mediocrity that has infiltrated the Church under the banner of humility. The brother’s declaration—“I have accepted a certain standard and I cannot go below it”—will offend those who have confused lowliness with low expectations. But lowliness is not the same as accepting less than what God has promised. Humility is not the same as tolerating what God has condemned. The saints are called to be “more than conquerors” (Romans 8:37, NKJV), to be “the head and not the tail” (Deuteronomy 28:13, NKJV), to be “a royal priesthood, a holy nation” (1 Peter 2:9, NKJV). These are not arrogant declarations—they are covenant realities. And when we lower the standard to make room for what the enemy sends, we dishonor the One who purchased us with His own blood.

## What This Means For Us

**For Spiritual Maturity:**

This meditation calls us to stop spiritualizing passivity and start exercising the governmental authority we have been given in Christ. Maturity is not learning to endure everything—it is learning to discern what to resist, what to receive, and what to refuse. It is establishing the standards of God’s Word in our hearts before the test comes, and refusing to negotiate those standards regardless of pressure, opportunity, or delay.

**For Kingdom Obedience:**

This calls us to condemn—actively, authoritatively, judicially—every work of darkness that exalts itself against the knowledge of God. It calls us to refuse counterfeits, to reject what God has not sent, and to war violently for the inheritance that Christ has already secured. It calls us to stop praying for what we will not discern, and to stop asking God to change what we will not condemn.

**For Prophetic Posture:**

This trains us to recognize that the Kingdom suffers violence, and the violent take it by force. It trains us to stand as gatekeepers over our own lives, our families, and our assignments—refusing entry to every spirit, every relationship, every opportunity that does not bear the fingerprints of the Father. It trains us to live with holy standards, covenant clarity, and the fierce, unapologetic love of a Bride who knows her Bridegroom and will not settle for less.

## A Closing Prayer

Father, I thank You that You have not left us passive, powerless, or without authority. I thank You that You have given us salvation, the Holy Spirit, the Name of Jesus, and the governmental authority to enforce the victory that Christ has already won. Forgive me for the times I have spiritualized passivity, for the times I have accepted what You never sent, for the times I have failed to condemn the works of darkness and have instead prayed for You to do what You have already empowered me to do. Teach me to establish Your standards in my heart, to discern what is from You and what is counterfeit, and to war violently—not in the flesh, but in the Spirit—for the fullness of the inheritance You have promised. Let me live as one who knows the worth of my Bridegroom, who refuses to settle, and who takes the Kingdom by force. In the Name of Jesus, the King of Kings and Lord of Lords, Amen.
