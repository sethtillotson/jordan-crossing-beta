# 05-16 Conversation Reflection: Covenant Theology, Spiritual Discernment, and Cross-Cultural Brotherhood **Title:** 05-16 at 17:42 — The Law That Could Not Save, the God Who Came Anyway, and the Brotherhood He Built in the Ordinary **Recorded:** 2026-05-16 17:42:40 **Source Discernment:** C — Recorded Conversation or Event (Live Capture) **Classification:** Conversation Reflection ## Opening Observation As I sat with my brother Samuel across the full arc of what became an extended afternoon and evening of conversation — moving through theology, laughter, logistical fumbling, payment confirmations, dark spiritual encounters, investment pitches, travel plans, and the opening verses of First Samuel — what kept surfacing beneath all of it was a single unifying thread: the question of what we are truly pursuing, and whether the God who authored the Law, ordains provision, plants covenant friendships across oceans, and dwells within His people is being pursued Himself, or whether we are chasing the traces He has left behind. The question my brother had received from someone else — *why does God not punish us when we break the Ten Commandments?* — was not a frivolous question. It opened into an entire architecture of covenant theology. And underneath even the payment negotiations, the wire transfers, the spiritual encounters Samuel described, the Tennessee Vols investment pitch, the booked transatlantic flight, and the genealogy of Elkanah, something unmistakably real was happening: a God who cannot be domesticated was threading His purposes through an afternoon that looked, to every external observer, like nothing more than two friends catching up. **Why This Matters:** The saints of God are regularly confronted with questions that seem simple on the surface but carry enormous theological weight underneath. When someone asks *why doesn’t God punish us for breaking the Ten Commandments?*, they are revealing a fundamental misread of what the Mosaic covenant was for — and by extension, a misread of who Christ is and what He actually accomplished. When a brother sees dark spiritual figures standing in his room and asks what they are, he is navigating spiritual reality in real time with insufficient framework. When provision arrives through the one who caused the deepest wound, we need a theology robust enough to receive that without calling it coincidence. And when two men shaped by radically different furnaces — different continents, different histories, different registers of danger and survival — find themselves laughing over a map and planning a road trip, the Body of Christ should be able to name what is being built there rather than filing it under casual friendship. This is a generation in danger of misunderstanding the Law, misunderstanding grace, misunderstanding intercession, misunderstanding covenant friendship, and missing the God who is moving in all of it precisely because He tends to move in the ordinary packaging of unrehearsed life. That danger is what this meditation exists to address. ## The Worldly Mind’s Interpretation The natural mind, surveying everything that unfolded in this conversation, would produce a tidy set of explanations for each of its movements. The theological exchange about the Ten Commandments would be classified as an interesting debate between friends with religious inclinations. The wire transfer and the provision through an unexpected source would be filed under renewed relationship and the natural ebb of human reconciliation. The dark figures Samuel described seeing would be attributed to optical illusion, fatigue, or the psychological residue of anxiety. The flight booked on a zero balance would be labeled impulsive generosity of spirit at best, financial irresponsibility at worst. The investment pitch would be assessed by ROI calculations. The cross-cultural bonding over Badlands photography and British passport pride would be labeled cosmopolitan friendship. And the genealogy of Elkanah — *son of Jeroham, son of Elihu, son of Tohu, son of Zuph* — would be skimmed entirely, because the natural mind has no patience for what appears to be filler on the way to the plot. Taken together, the secular frame would produce this verdict: a scattered, affectionate, occasionally profound conversation between two men navigating life, carrying their wounds with humor, and making plans they may or may not keep. There is nothing wrong with that verdict as far as it goes. It simply does not go very far. **The Limitation:** The problem is that the natural mind operates within categories that the Spirit of God regularly refuses to honor. It can only read the horizontal axis — what preceded what, who did what to whom, what emotional or financial logic explains the outcome. It cannot trace the vertical thread that runs through a wire transfer sent by the one who caused the deepest wound, through a genealogy that encodes divine ownership before the first prayer is prayed, through a flight booked in the moment of conviction before the money arrives, through the first genuinely uttered prayer of a man who has never prayed before. The mind of the flesh needs to see before it can believe, needs the safety net confirmed before it calls the movement faith, needs the dramatic packaging before it can recognize the sacred. But the God of Scripture is relentlessly, almost stubbornly ordinary in His operational methods: He works in kitchens the night before the thanksgiving is prayed, He plants His name in the lineage of a Levite man living in the hill country during a national crisis, He confirms a covenant friendship through the coordinates on a phone screen. To perceive what He is actually doing requires an interpretive capacity that no amount of natural intelligence can produce. It requires the same Spirit who authored the text to illuminate the life being read through it. ## What the Spirit Reveals Through It As I sit with everything that moved through this conversation, I find myself compelled to begin where the theological exchange began — with the question about divine punishment and the Ten Commandments — because that question is the key that unlocks the architecture of everything else. The questioner who asked my brother Samuel *why doesn’t God punish us when we break the Ten Commandments?* was not being frivolous. The question reveals a sincere engagement with moral reality and divine character. But built into it is a fundamental premise that must be addressed before any answer can land correctly: the premise that the Mosaic covenant was primarily a punitive legal code, a list of prohibitions backed by divine enforcement. And while judgment is certainly woven through the Mosaic economy, the purpose of that covenant was never punitive in the sense the questioner assumed. Judgment in the biblical sense always leads somewhere. It is not an end in itself. The Greek word μετάνοια (metanoia, meh-TAH-noy-ah) — repentance, a genuine turning of the mind and will — is the destination that biblical judgment is always moving toward. When I said to Samuel that judgment leads to μετάνοια (meh-TAH-noy-ah), I was not softening divine wrath. I was naming the telos — the purpose and design-intent of judgment as the canon itself reveals it. Even in Ezekiel 18:23, where the Lord declares *I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live*, the punitive framework opens into an invitational one. The Law does not exist to crush people. It exists to make them see that they need a Deliverer. And this is a distinction the modern church has largely lost, with devastating consequences: we have produced either people who approach the Law as a behavior management system, developing pride at their partial compliance, or people who collapse under despair at their inevitable failure — but rarely people who arrive at the one thing the Law was designed to produce, which is a soul crying out for a Savior. This is where the typological structure of the Mosaic covenant becomes genuinely staggering when the Spirit opens it. The Greek word τύπος (typos, TOO-pos) — an impression, a pattern, a prefiguring form — is used by Paul in Romans 5:14 when he calls Adam a τύπος (TOO-pos) of the One to come. The entire Levitical system — the sacrificial calendar, the purity codes, the priestly regulations, the dietary laws — functions as a vast, elaborate pattern that points beyond itself. It is not the thing. It is the shape of the thing before the thing arrives. And when you look carefully at the structure of that Law, you find something the surface reader never expects: the Law contains within it situations where a person is cornered. Two options, both of them sinful. This is not a flaw in the Torah. This is the Torah doing exactly what it was designed to do. It is demonstrating, with unmistakable clarity, that without the Lord, you can do nothing. This is the heart of what Jesus Himself said in John 15:5 — ἐγώ εἰμι ἡ ἄμπελος (egō eimi hē ampelos, eh-GOH EE-mee hay AHM-peh-los) — “I am the vine, you are the branches. He who abides in Me, and I in him, bears much fruit; for without Me you can do nothing.” The Law of Moses is the long prophetic demonstration of that final phrase. The letter kills — 2 Corinthians 3:6 says this not to demean Moses but to be precise — because it makes demands that the unregenerate human being is constitutionally incapable of meeting. Galatians 3:24 names this function directly: the Law is our παιδαγωγός (paidagōgos, pie-dah-go-GOHS) — a guardian, a tutor, the household servant who walked the child to school and made sure they arrived. The παιδαγωγός (pie-dah-go-GOHS) was never the teacher. He was the escort to the Teacher. The Law walks us to the door of Christ. It was never designed to be the destination. And then He came. The name that arrested me in conversation with Samuel, that I heard myself say with a kind of joy that surprised me even as I said it: עִמָּנוּ אֵל (Immanuel, im-MAH-noo-EL) — God with us. Not God watching from a distance. Not God managing from a throne. God *with* us. The seismic shift of the incarnation is this: the entire Old Testament is structured around the movement of return — *return to Me, says the LORD of hosts, and I will return to you* (Zechariah 1:3). Repentance, exile, restoration — it is always movement back toward God. And then in the fullness of time, God moves toward us. He does not wait at the top of the mountain for a Moses. He descends. He enters. He dwells. What Paul renders as Χριστὸς ἐν ὑμῖν (Christos en hymin, krees-TOHS en HEW-min) in Colossians 1:27 — *Christ in you, the hope of glory* — is the fulfillment of everything the Law pointed toward but could never produce. The temple cultus could bring God’s presence *among* the people, but not *within* them. It took the incarnation, the cross, the resurrection, and the gift of the Holy Spirit to accomplish what the Law could only prophesy: God dwelling in the human heart by faith, reshaping the believer from within rather than demanding obedience from without. As I turn from the theological exchange to the testimony of provision that surfaced in this same conversation, I notice that the same God who authored the impossibility of the Law also authored an answer to a prayer I had prayed for gas money on a drive home. The provision came through my brother Samuel — through a \$55 weekly arrangement that arrived precisely because he had newly come to Christ and was now learning to keep his word. What arrests me about this is not the amount. It is the instrument. And it is the timing. I am beginning to understand, through personal experience confirmed by the witness of George Mueller, that God does not begin moving when we pray. He was already moving before we prayed. The baker was already baking extra batches in the night before the prayer of thanksgiving was spoken over empty plates. The provision was already in motion. Prayer is not the mechanism that activates a previously passive God. Prayer is our alignment with what He has already purposed and is already executing in the unseen. My forgotten prayer was not forgotten by God. When He answered it, He proved something about His character that no theology textbook can fully articulate: He is the keeper of words, the treasury of petitions spoken in genuine faith, the One to whom no whispered request is lost. What compounds this is something Samuel shared about the source of the provision — that the one now paying him was the one who had caused him the deepest wound. I did not flinch at that testimony. I said, without hesitation, that this is exactly what I would expect from my God. Scripture is saturated with this pattern. Joseph’s brothers, who sold him into slavery, become the very men he feeds during famine. The Assyrian king through whom God brings discipline is called God’s “rod of anger” in Isaiah 10:5. Paul, who consented to the stoning of Stephen, becomes the apostle through whom more of the New Testament was written than any other human hand. God does not merely tolerate the instruments He chooses — He ordains them. The provision arriving through an unexpected and formerly wounding source is not irony. It is theology. It is the God who causes even the wrath of man to praise Him (Psalm 76:10). And it is Jehovah Jireh — יְהוָה יִרְאֶה (Yahweh Yir’eh, yah-WEH yeer-EH) — the LORD who sees and therefore provides, doing what He has always done: providing through instruments no human planning would have selected, at a timing no human anxiety would have chosen, through the transformed character of a brother who has been apprehended by grace. As we turned to First Samuel together near the end of our exchange, I am struck by how little that movement felt incidental. The genealogy of Elkanah — son of Jeroham, son of Elihu, son of Tohu, son of Zuph — is not filler. The name אֶלְקָנָה (Elqanah, el-kah-NAH), rooted in the verb קָנָה (qanah, kah-NAH) — to acquire, to possess, to purchase, to create — announces divine ownership before Hannah ever whispers a prayer at Shiloh. *God has acquired.* In the midst of a narrative about barrenness and longing and a household fractured by comparison, the very name of Hannah’s husband testifies to the character of God. The Lord is the One who acquires before the acquired has recognized it. This is the kind of God I am dealing with in every dimension of this afternoon — not a God who waits for ideal conditions before He works, but a God who is already working inside the brokenness, already embedding His name in the ordinary lineage of a Levite man during one of Israel’s darkest cycles. Every man preserved in that genealogy — Elihu, Tohu, Zuph — represents a life lived in relative obscurity through which God was threading a purpose that none of them could fully see from where they stood. Samuel’s very name encodes the origin story: שְׁמוּאֵל (Shemu’el, sheh-moo-EL) — heard by God, asked of God. Hannah named her son after the experience of being שָׁמַע (shama, shah-MAH) — heard, listened to, responded to in covenant faithfulness — by the Everlasting One. This matters deeply for how I read the parallel thread in this conversation: my brother Samuel’s first genuine prayer. He said it plainly — *“Seth, this is the first time I’ve ever actually done a legit prayer.”* And I did not rush to correct his language or expand his theology. I simply said, *“That’s something to know.”* Because it is. The Kingdom of God often arrives in a person’s life not through a doctrinal thesis but through a wire transfer that inexplicably goes through, and a question asked in genuine uncertainty: *Has the Lord done His job?* The answer in that moment was yes. And the right pastoral response to a man’s first genuine movement toward Heaven is not an immediate curriculum. It is quiet recognition. Let the seed land. Let the soil receive it. Trust the One who gives the increase — 1 Corinthians 3:7 says it is God who gives αὔξησιν (auxēsin, owk-SAY-sin) — the growth, the life within the seed. That life is entirely His. Our interference in the germination process, however well-intentioned, may only disrupt what the Spirit was already carefully cultivating. I cannot move past what Samuel described about the dark spiritual figures he encounters regularly — figures that appear, watch, and leave without engaging him. My counsel was layered and I stand by it: only God can condemn; a believer can rebuke in the name of Jesus; if an entity has not broken spiritual law by actively attacking or influencing, it occupies a kind of permitted observational territory that Scripture acknowledges in the category of עִיר (ir, EER) — the watchers, the wakeful ones who appear in Daniel’s vocabulary and in the broader canonical witness. But what the Spirit pressed me to voice, and presses me still, is this: Samuel could perceive this presence at all only because the Spirit within him granted him that perception. The gift of διάκρισις πνευμάτων (diakrisis pneumatōn, dee-AH-kree-sis puh-nyoo-MAH-tone) — discernment of spirits — is not a burden to be endured. It is a weapon to be deployed. And 1 John 4:4 remains the doctrinal foundation beneath every such encounter: μείζων (meizōn, MY-zone) — He who is in you is greater, of higher rank, surpassing in power, than he who is in the world. The question the Spirit always asks of every prophetic gift is not *what do you see?* but *what is God asking of you right now?* Every capacity cultivated in a believer is for Kingdom deployment, not for sustained personal anxiety about what is perceived. The testimony I offered from my own season — the car repossessed the day after I prayed for provision, my wife pressing for a second job, the logic of the flesh demanding motion — is not a small thing to name in a conversation. It is the testimony of a man learning the difference between God’s silence and God’s absence. To bear the weight of that silence without filling it with one’s own noise is the fruit of παιδεία (paideia, pie-DAY-ah) — the fatherly training of God described in Hebrews 12 — and it is not pleasant during the formation. But it yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness after it has done its full work. Hebrews 12:11 holds that tension honestly: no chastening seems joyful for the present, but painful. The word παιδεία (pie-DAY-ah) belongs to the same word-family as παιδαγωγός (pie-dah-go-GOHS) — the tutor-escort who walked the child to Christ. The discipline and the Law share a root purpose: they are both escort services. Both are designed to bring us somewhere we could not have arrived on our own. And the somewhere they are designed to bring us is always Him. What I cannot leave behind — and what I think the Spirit is pressing hardest into all of this — is the question of the geography being established between two brothers shaped by different furnaces. My brother comes from a world where leaving your door unlocked marks you as a fool, where the threat of violence is a lived and rational calculation, where he once sat watching grown men destroy themselves in environments that should have consumed him too. I come from a world where garage doors stand open all day and strangers invite you to after-parties. These are not merely lifestyle differences. These are formational realities — the kind that shape how a person reads danger, reads trust, reads the face of a stranger. And yet something is overriding the calculus of fear in both directions and drawing two men, shaped by wildly different histories, toward the same table and the same Word. I find this rhyming with Ruth’s declaration in Ruth 1:16 — spoken out of the covenant quality that Scripture calls חֶסֶד (chesed, KHEH-sed), loyal love that exceeds obligation — *where you go, I will go.* That is not a travel itinerary. That is a covenant declaration. And the willingness to cross unfamiliar terrain, to enter another man’s world, to say *I want to be where you are* — particularly when the flight is booked before the money arrives — carries that same covenantal quality of movement toward rather than away. The flight booked on a zero balance is its own theological statement. Abraham went out, Hebrews 11:8 tells us, not knowing where he was going. The Hebrew עִבְרִי (ivri, EEV-ree) — the root from which “Hebrew” itself comes — carries the notion of one who crosses over, one who passes through. The patriarch’s defining characteristic was not that he had a plan. It was that he moved when God said move, and the plan assembled itself around the obedience. My brother’s act of booking the ticket and then announcing it as a fait accompli — *it’s booked, I am serious, I am not messing with you* — carries that same quality: conviction acted upon before the conditions are favorable, faith expressed as motion rather than intention. And when the map revealed that the hotel was within walking distance of my home, that the same strip of road contained both of our next moves, there was a genuine moment of wonder in the exchange. That wonder is not superstition. It is the appropriate human response to the God who bears the name אֵל רֳאִי (El Ro’i, EL ro-EE) — the God who sees — the name Hagar gave to the well in her wilderness when she discovered she was not unseen (Genesis 16:13). Providence does not always announce itself with trumpets. Sometimes it announces itself through a map on a phone screen and the coordinates of an arranged meeting that neither party arranged. Threading through the full arc of this conversation — from the question about the Ten Commandments, through the payment confirmation, through Samuel’s encounter with dark figures, through the investment pitch and the booked flight and the planned road trip — I keep arriving at the same place: a God who did not wait at the top of the mountain for Moses but descended, entered, and dwelled; a God who does not wait for ideal conditions before He acquires; a God who keeps the prayers we have forgotten until He is ready to answer them and then reminds us of what we surrendered; a God who builds covenant friendship across the fault lines the world said were permanent; a God who bakes the bread before the prayer of thanksgiving is spoken. The sacred and the practical are not separate registers in the Kingdom. They are woven together by a God who entered history in a body, who ate fish on a beach, who turned water into wine at a wedding — and who still sees the servant who prayed for gas money on the drive home. To compartmentalize that God into the theological portion of the afternoon and leave Him out of the payment app discussion is a failure of perception, not a mark of reverence. He was present in all of it. He always is. ## Implications and Challenges The questioner who asked *why doesn’t God punish us for breaking the Ten Commandments?* deserves more than a surface answer, and the church that only offers *we punish ourselves* will produce one of two distortions: a God who is essentially passive, or a believer who never quite understands why they keep failing the standard they keep trying to meet. The typological structure of the Mosaic covenant — the Law as mirror, the Law as tutor, the Law as the structured impossibility that makes the incarnation not only beautiful but *necessary* — has been largely evacuated from modern Christian preaching. We read the Old Testament as moral instruction for contemporary life or as ancient history without present relevance. We have forgotten that the Levitical sacrifices are prophetic liturgy, that they are the Law acting out in blood and smoke what would one day be accomplished in the body of the Son of God. When we lose typology, we lose the unity of the canon. When we lose the unity of the canon, we cannot answer the questions our people are actually carrying. The testimony of provision through a formerly wounding source confronts a subtly distorted prosperity theology that operates in many Western Christian circles: the assumption that provision will come *through* our diligence, preparation, and the right spiritual habits. This is not entirely wrong — Scripture honors the diligent — but it becomes a distortion when we unconsciously conclude that provision cannot come from outside the system of our own preparation and planning. When God provides through the one who hurt us most, He demolishes our tendency to believe we have mapped the possible routes of His faithfulness. He refuses to be predictable, and this is not cruelty — it is the protection of our dependence on Him rather than on our own financial architecture. The teaching that daily prayer for the same petition must repeat in order to sustain the Spirit’s movement — the idea that prayer power resets overnight like manna — risks producing a spiritual anxiety that keeps believers from the rest of genuine faith. If my prayers expire overnight, I am never truly free to surrender an outcome to God. I am only ever in a holding pattern, recharging for tomorrow’s attempt. But Hebrews 7:25 tells us that Jesus *always lives to make intercession* — ἐντυγχάνω (entynchanō, en-toon-KHAH-no) — to intercede, to appeal on behalf of, to meet with. His priestly ministry is unbroken and eternal. The model it establishes is not daily reset but sustained, unsleeping advocacy. When I speak a request once in genuine faith and full release, and then the prayer moves so completely out of my hands and into His that my conscious mind no longer carries it — that is not a failure of spiritual discipline. That is faith completing its circuit. The Spirit who Jesus promises will bring all things to our remembrance (John 14:26) retrieved it at the moment of fulfillment. The entire arc was His. The moment when my brother Samuel said his first genuine prayer must not be trivialized or rushed past. The Body of Christ must learn the pastoral wisdom of holding space for the first step without immediately organizing it into a discipleship curriculum. Not every moment of spiritual awakening requires immediate theological expansion. Sometimes the most Spirit-led response is precisely what I offered: *“That’s something to know.”* Let the seed land. Let the soil receive it. Trust the Giver of increase. A church that cannot resist the impulse to immediately systematize the first fragile movement of a new soul toward God is a church that mistakes program management for pastoral care. The cross-cultural dimension of the friendship forming across this conversation should press the Body of Christ toward honest examination. If the new humanity described in Ephesians 2 is actually real — if the dividing wall has actually been broken down — then friendships that cross racial, national, economic, and cultural lines are not merely admirable but normative. They are the expected fruit of the gospel. The degree to which our actual relational lives remain racially and culturally segregated is not primarily a political problem. It is a Christological one. The vision of Galatians 3:28 — εἷς (heis, HAYSS), *one* in Christ Jesus — is not aspirational sentiment. It is a declaration about the nature of the new creation. The friendship being built in this conversation — across two different continents, two different registers of danger, two different histories — is not exotic. It is what normal Kingdom life is supposed to look like. Finally, the observation that American sports fans follow their teams *religiously* — offered almost as a casual aside — carries a theological rebuke that deserves more than a passing laugh. Human beings are worshipping creatures. We do not get to choose whether we will organize our devotion around something; we only get to choose the object. The stadium is a substitute sanctuary. The season ticket is a substitute tithe. The tribal belonging of the fan base is a misdirected form of the very impulse God designed for the gathering of the saints. Augustine named this before either of us was born: *our heart is restless until it repose in Thee.* The answer to religious sports devotion is not better programming in our church buildings. It is genuine covenant life — the kind being stumbled toward in this very conversation: messy, real, geographically sprawling, carrying the weight of actual human lives, and always finding its way back to the Word and the blessing. ## What This Means For Us **For Spiritual Maturity:** Maturity in Christ means learning to read the old covenant through the lens of fulfillment rather than obligation or irrelevance — to sit with a questioner genuinely confused about divine punishment and lead them not just to a surface answer but to the covenantal architecture underneath: the Law as mirror, the Law as tutor, the Law as the structured impossibility that makes Emmanuel not only beautiful but necessary. It also means developing the kind of theology that does not panic when provision is delayed or when it arrives through unexpected instruments — because we have learned, through Scripture and through personal testimony, that יְהוָה יִרְאֶה (yah-WEH yeer-EH) sees before He provides, and His timing is ordered by His purposes rather than our anxiety. Maturity further means learning to remain in the silence without filling it with our own noise, to bear the weight of the repossessed car and the empty plate with gratitude already forming on our lips — because the baker is already baking in the night before the prayer of thanksgiving is spoken. **For Kingdom Obedience:** The primary obedience this meditation calls forth is both relational and intercessory: to invest in covenant friendships that cross difference and distance at real cost; to intercede for the anointing of others before it is visible, holding territory in prayer before the arrival of the one being prayed for; and to receive the first halting steps of men and women moving toward God with the pastoral patience that allows the seed to germinate rather than rushing to organize it. Kingdom obedience also means stopping the use of the Law as a behavioral management system and letting it do what it was designed to do — show us our need for Christ, daily, freshly, without embarrassment. When someone says *at least this time we’re actually being honest with each other*, that is the Kingdom breaking through ordinary life, and it should be named as such. **For Prophetic Posture:** The prophetic posture that emerges from this full arc of conversation is one of receptive attentiveness that refuses to compartmentalize the sacred from the practical. The prophetic eye watches for the coordinates on a phone screen that reveal what God has already arranged, for the name embedded in a genealogy that announces divine ownership before the first prayer is prayed, for the first genuine prayer of a man who did not know he was praying, for the provision that arrives through the wound. Those who abide well are those who have trained themselves to see God moving in the logistics of ordinary life — not only in the cathedral moments, but in the payment confirmations and the late-night phone calls and the laughter that carries more theology than it knows — and who can declare, with recognition and without exaggeration: *This is the Lord’s doing, and it is marvelous in our eyes* (Psalm 118:23). ## A Closing Prayer Father, I come before You as one who has been acquired — and I do not use that word lightly. You embedded Your name in an ordinary Levite man in the hill country of Ephraim during one of Israel’s darkest cycles and called him אֶלְקָנָה (el-kah-NAH) — God has acquired — before Hannah ever whispered a prayer at Shiloh. And I find You doing the same thing still: placing Your name in the ordinary, threading purpose through the imperfect, answering the prayers we have already forgotten, and providing through the instruments we never would have chosen. I give You thanks for the Law that would not let us settle — the Law that cornered us into seeing that without You, we can do nothing, and that drove us at last to the One who came not to condemn but to dwell, עִמָּנוּ אֵל (im-MAH-noo-EL), God with us. I give You thanks for Χριστὸς ἐν ὑμῖν (krees-TOHS en HEW-min), for the presence that does not wait for us to arrive but descends to meet us where we are. I give You thanks for provision that arrives before Monday, for brothers learning to keep their word, for a \$55 arrangement that is exponentially more than it appears because it is Your answer to a prayer prayed on a drive home. I give You thanks for the booked flight and the hotel that was already within walking distance, for the coordinates on a phone screen that revealed what You had already arranged, for the first prayer of a man who did not know he was praying. I ask that You continue to open the eyes of Your people to the typological glory of Your whole canon — that the Law would do its diagnostic work in every heart, and that μετάνοια (meh-TAH-noy-ah) would be the outcome rather than despair. Guard the covenant being built between brothers across oceans and histories and different registers of danger. Let the story of Hannah speak again into every barren place, every closed womb, every situation where provision seems structurally impossible. Guard my brother Samuel — bind every dark assignment against him, let the authority of the name above every name be the final word spoken over his life, and let the discernment You have given him be deployed as a weapon of intercession rather than carried as a weight of dread. You are יְהוָה יִרְאֶה (yah-WEH yeer-EH) — You see, therefore You provide. Let that be our settled confidence and our resting place, now and in every season to come.
